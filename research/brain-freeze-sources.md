# Brain-freeze guide: source notes

Checked October 16, 2026 for `blog/ice-cream-brain-freeze-memphis.html`.

## Primary medical sources

- Johns Hopkins Medicine, “How to Ease Brain Freeze” (updated October 10, 2026): https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/expert-qa/how-to-ease-brain-freeze
  - Reviewed its expert description of a leading proposed mechanism: rapid cold contact cools the palate; nearby blood vessels constrict and then open, with pain signals traveling along the trigeminal nerve and being felt elsewhere. Johns Hopkins says multiple theories exist. Also used its practical suggestions to stop the cold stimulus, warm the palate with the tongue or warm water, and eat/drink cold items more slowly. Notes possible migraine trigger for people prone to migraine.
- Cleveland Clinic, “Brain Freeze” (medically reviewed; page last updated February 15, 2021): https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21478-brain-freeze
  - Used definition, alternate terms, typical brief duration, broad cold triggers, warming/room-temperature measures, slower consumption, and advice to discuss frequent headaches that last a while with a healthcare provider.
- Harvard Health Publishing, “What happens during a brain freeze and is it harmful?” (February 11, 2026): https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/what-happens-during-a-brain-freeze-and-is-it-harmful
  - Used cautious summary of referred pain and proposed blood-vessel/pain-receptor explanation; states cause is debated, typical cold-stimulus headache is short and described as harmless, and slowly eating cold foods is a prevention suggestion. Its medical disclaimer is retained in article framing.
- Chebini A, Dilli E. “Cold Stimulus Headache.” *Current Neurology and Neuroscience Reports*. 2019;19(7):46. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31172287/
  - Review abstract notes limited literature and says age, stimulus type, comorbidities, design, speed, and exposed area can affect reported occurrence/intensity/latency. It summarizes a study in which ice water produced more frequent and higher-intensity headache with shorter latency than ice cubes. The article identifies this as a specific study comparison, not a universal dessert-risk ranking.
