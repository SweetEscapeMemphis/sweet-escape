## Primary and authoritative sources

1. American Chemical Society, “Changing State—Melting”: https://www.acs.org/middleschoolchemistry/lessonplans/chapter2/lesson5.html
   - Used for the bounded paper-towel comparison and dry-ice fog explanation. The article does not reproduce the lesson as a home experiment and explicitly says this is not handling guidance.
2. CDC / NIOSH Pocket Guide, “Carbon dioxide”: https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/npg/npgd0103.html
   - Carbon dioxide is colorless and odorless; solid form is dry ice; approximate sublimation temperature −109 °F; relative gas density 1.53; frostbite and exposure hazards. No numerical exposure limits appear in the article.
3. FDA, “Liquid Nitrogen and Dry Ice in the Food Code” (Food Code Reference System; October 31, 2018): https://www.fda.gov/media/117281/download
   - The cited reference reports dry ice has been used to preserve frozen foods where mechanical means are unavailable and for visual smoke effects in food/beverages; it cautions not to consume it or allow direct skin contact and explains the confined-space CO₂ issue. It is a dated model Food Code reference, not a claim about Sweet Escape, a current local requirement, or legal advice.
4. ASHRAE, “Air-Conditioning and Refrigeration Chronology”: https://www.ashrae.org/File%20Library/About/Mission%20and%20Vision/ASHRAE%20and%20Industry%20History/Air-Conditioning-and-Refrigeration-Chronology.pdf
   - The timeline lists Thilorier’s first production of dry ice in 1835 and Faraday’s liquefaction of gases from 1823 onward. No claim about the text or preservation of Thilorier’s original paper is made.
5. NOAA / NASA, “What’s the Difference Between Fog and Clouds?”: https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/about/k-12-education/atmosphere/whats-the-difference-between-fog-and-clouds
   - Visible fog/cloud droplets form through condensation; supports the miniature-cloud comparison.
6. CDC / NIOSH, “Guidelines for Safe Work Practices in Human and Animal Medical Diagnostic Laboratories,” section 3.6: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/su6101a1.htm
   - Extreme cold, frostbite, avoiding bare-handed contact/ingestion, sealed-container pressure hazard, ventilation, and carbon dioxide hazards. Article gives only high-level warnings, not a handling procedure.
7. USGS, “Sublimation and the Water Cycle”: https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/sublimation-and-water-cycle?page=1
   - Solid-to-gas transition without an intermediate liquid, deposition as the reverse transition, frost, and dry ice’s −78.5 °C / −109.3 °F sublimation temperature.
8. NIST Chemistry WebBook, “Carbon dioxide”: https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C124389&Mask=4
   - Triple-point temperature 216.58 K and pressure 5.185 bar; the article uses rounded Celsius/atmosphere equivalents, not a detailed phase curve.
