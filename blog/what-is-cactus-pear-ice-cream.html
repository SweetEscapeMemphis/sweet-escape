A Cactus Pear Isn’t a Pear. So What Is It?

Published November 18, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Suppose you ordered a cactus pear ice cream and got to see the fruit that inspired the name. You might recognize the shape of a pear, but you wouldn’t find a stem that came off a pear tree. The fruit grows on a cactus, and the green paddles it sits on aren’t leaves. There’s one more surprise: the big spines are easier to spot than the tiny barbs you actually need to watch for on the fruit.

Cactus pear is another name for prickly pear fruit. In Mexican food contexts you’ll also see it called tuna. At Sweet Escape in Memphis, the name turns up in our ice cream catalog as Cactus Pearfection. To understand what the name is referring to, start with the plant instead of trying to picture a spiky version of the pear in your fruit bowl.

Where the pear comparison ends

The shape is a useful clue to the nickname, not to the plant family. The Missouri Department of Conservation’s eastern prickly pear profile describes that species’ fruit as pear-shaped. But look up the common pear, Pyrus communis, in Kew’s plant database and you’ll find a tree in the rose family. A widely cultivated prickly pear, Opuntia ficus-indica, belongs to the cactus family.

That distinction is useful at a market. If an ingredient list calls for prickly pear juice, ordinary pear juice isn’t the same ingredient. Likewise, the name tuna on a fruit stand is no reason to look for a fish. The FAO crop profile records names including cactus pear, Indian fig and Barbary fig for the cultivated species. Several familiar fruit words can point to one unfamiliar plant without making those fruits interchangeable.

There’s a wrinkle even within the cactus label. Prickly pear is used for multiple species, not only the cultivated one Kew profiles. The eastern prickly pear in the Missouri field guide is an example from a different setting. Its photograph is a way to understand the shapes on the plant, not evidence of which variety supplied a dessert.

Look at the paddles, then look above them

The big green structures are flattened stems. A stem segment is called a cladode, although “pad” will do just fine while you’re pointing at a photograph. On an eastern prickly pear, the true leaves on young pads are much smaller and short-lived. The pad is still there after those little leaves dry and fall off. It’s easy to mistake the largest green surface for a leaf if you don’t know what to look for.

Now look at a picture with flowers. The cactus makes blossoms on the pads, and the fruit develops where there was a flower. You can see the two structures together in the National Park Service’s prickly pear photographs. What looks from a distance like a red extension of a green paddle is a separate part of the plant. A pad can have several fruits attached, like a row of small ornaments along its edge.

That also explains the difference between nopales and tunas in a kitchen. The UC Davis nopalitos guide describes the young, tender stems; those are the vegetable. Tunas are the fruits. If you see sliced nopales used in a savory dish and cactus pear juice used in a frozen dessert, you’re seeing two ways to use different parts of the plant, not two names for the same ingredient.

It’s the tiny barbs you can miss

Pictures of a cactus make the long, needle-like spines easy to pick out. The smaller bristles deserve just as much attention. These are glochids, which occur in clusters at spots called areoles. University of Nevada, Reno Extension notes that the pads and fruit can carry the small, barbed bristles, which detach easily and can lodge in skin. A cactus described as spineless can still have them.

That’s why a nearly smooth-looking fruit or one pictured with no obvious spikes isn’t an invitation to pluck or taste it. If you’re curious about the ingredient for your own kitchen, start with commercially prepared fruit or juice intended for food and follow the seller’s preparation directions. A photograph is plenty for getting to know the plant’s anatomy.

Those bristles are an outside problem. The hard seeds mixed through the pulp are a separate, inside one. A prepared fruit, a strained juice and a syrup won’t all deliver the same kind of texture in a dessert. A few words on the front of a menu can name the fruit without telling you how much of that structure makes it into the spoonful.

One fruit name, several colors

If the first prickly pear you ever saw was purple, a pale green or yellow one might look like a different fruit altogether. UC Davis’s cactus pear guide lists colors ranging from pale green to purple across cultivars. It treats color as one clue to maturity alongside features such as fullness and firmness. Comparing a fruit with others of its own variety is more informative than expecting every ripe cactus pear to turn the same shade.

The colors have a chemistry story, too. Elena Castellanos-Santiago and Elhadi Yahia’s 2008 study examined ten prickly pear cultivars or lines grown in Mexico. The team found differing amounts and combinations of pigments called betalains, which include red-violet betacyanins and yellow betaxanthins. The purple Camuesa fruit had the highest total betalain content in that set. The lesson isn’t that one shade is better; it’s that even fruit sold under the same everyday name can hold measurably different mixtures of pigments.

Cactus pears also don’t follow the same ripening script as an orchard pear. UC Davis recommends picking cactus pears fully ripe, while its guide to pear harvest describes picking pears before they’re fully ripe. If you’re used to buying a firm orchard pear and giving it time, don’t assume that habit transfers just because both names say “pear.”

A sour cousin with the seeds in a different place

Not every fruit in this group is used the same way. In a study comparing Mexican xoconostles, researchers describe these acidic cactus pears as having a thick, fleshy layer outside the seed core. That layer is the part eaten. Compare the cross-sections in their first figure: in the sweet cactus pear, the seeds sit throughout the pulp that people eat; in the xoconostle, the seeds are concentrated in the middle, away from the thick outer flesh.

That picture is more useful than trying to sort the fruit from peel color. Imagine making a neat slice across the middle of each one. With the sweet fruit, you see seeds dotted across the center you’re interested in; with the sour one, the useful flesh makes a substantial ring around a distinct center. If a recipe names xoconostle, that word is doing more than asking for a different color of tuna. It points to the sour fruit and a different part to work with.

From seeded pulp to something you can pour

The University of Arizona Cooperative Extension’s guide describes serving prepared fruit as a topping for ice cream and using its juice for syrup. These are two different ways to put the fruit next to something cold. The first keeps the fruit as a separate part of the serving. The second starts with liquid separated from the fruit and turns it into something that can be poured.

There’s another possibility: put the fruit into the frozen dessert itself. Mexico’s agriculture ministry office in Hidalgo lists ice cream among the culinary uses of tuna, along with juices and preserves. The next time you see the fruit named on a dessert menu, a useful question is where it appears: on top, as a sauce, or in the frozen part? The answer will tell you more about what you’re ordering than the word “cactus” alone.

If you’re choosing an ingredient at the grocery store rather than choosing a dessert, check whether the package says juice, purée or syrup. The Arizona guide shows fruit juice as a starting point and syrup as a further preparation. That matters when a recipe expects a pourable sauce over a scoop: a bottle labeled juice and a bottle labeled syrup answer different needs, even when the fruit pictured on the front is the same.
