Why Do Ice Cream Trucks Play the Same Tune?

Published November 26, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Sometimes the first thing you notice about an ice cream truck isn’t the truck. It’s four or five notes that sound familiar from inside the house. Then the tune starts over. The music is doing the job of a sign you can hear: it announces a shop that won’t be on the same corner all day. Repeating a tune gives people who heard only the tail end another chance to recognize it.

That doesn’t make it one universal song. There’s a difference between the same truck playing the same tune and every truck playing the same tune. The old bells, the music boxes that followed, and the way sound travels along a street all help explain why the experience feels familiar even when the song is different.

Before the song, the bells were enough

The Smithsonian’s 1938 Good Humor truck is a reminder that the signal didn’t need words or even a melody. The museum describes bells that announced the presence of the refrigerated truck. The cold compartment and the bells solved different problems. One kept the dessert frozen while the driver went from street to street. The other told people where the driver was without expecting them to be looking out the window.

Look at the Wisconsin Historical Society’s photograph in the International Harvester collection. The caption doesn’t just point out the chimes. It also describes the truck on its daily scheduled route. Put those two details together and the signal makes more sense. A sound is easiest to interpret when you’ve heard it before and know what followed it. If you live on the route, the chime can tell you the truck is close even if you don’t know the time.

A bell still leaves the listener to supply the meaning. It doesn’t announce the menu, how long the truck will be there, or what’s left in the freezer. It simply gets your attention. Our story about the ice cream stick follows the serving side of that same trip. A handle made a bar easier to hand over. A bell made it easier to tell when the bar was nearby.

How a box can keep a tune going

A metal bell makes a sound when it’s struck. A traditional cylinder music box stores an order for many sounds. In an 1894 music-box patent by Alfred Paillard and Alfred Sueur, small pins on a turning cylinder pluck tuned metal teeth. Their positions determine which teeth sound and when. Picture the cylinder as a little playlist you can hold: the pattern is physically on it, and when the same part comes round, the same notes are there to play again. That’s the general music-box principle, not the design of every truck chime.

The clever part of that patent is how it makes room for a different tune. The inventors describe shifting the cylinder a little lengthwise so the teeth can meet a different line of pins. The box doesn’t have to learn a new song; that song is already laid out next to the first. It’s more like moving over to the next row on a keyboard than turning a volume dial. Long before electronic memory, a maker already had to decide whether a box should repeat or advance to another tune.

Electronics can give a driver more choices without losing that sound. Nichols Electronics’ product documentation describes a Mark IV programmed with one song and an Omni II with 32 selections. One of the Omni II’s channels uses arrangements that evoke the older mechanical sound. Hearing that bright, plinky tone isn’t proof there’s a spinning cylinder in the truck. An electronic box can give an old sound a new place to live, and a box with many selections can still play the one its driver keeps choosing.

That’s why the length of the tune matters in a different way from the size of the song list. A driver could have dozens of choices and still use a short, memorable stretch of one of them. On a moving route, a person who’s washing dishes might catch only the second half; someone farther down the block might hear the beginning once the truck turns. A repeat makes it possible for both to hear a recognizable part without standing still to listen to the entire piece.

That tune probably had another life first

One good example is Scott Joplin’s The Entertainer. The Library of Congress has the 1902 piano score, and its Joplin biography notes that the rag became a centerpiece of the 1973 film The Sting. You don’t need to decide whether the movie or the chime is where everyone first heard it. The sheet music establishes that the tune was a piano piece long before a driver could select it on a modern music box.

Open the Nichols tune list and you’ll find two versions of that title, one in each channel. That’s a useful distinction between the tune itself and the way it’s played. You can recognize a melody from its rising and falling notes even if the instrument or sound changes. And a list that also includes “Twinkle, Twinkle” and “Pop Goes the Weasel” is evidence of choices, not a chart of what every neighborhood hears.

Some melodies have picked up associations that aren’t apparent when you hear only the notes. In 2020, Good Humor described its collaboration with RZA on a new jingle and acknowledged the racist minstrel-show history associated with versions of “Turkey in the Straw.” The new tune also appears in the Nichols list. The same music box format can carry a fresh tune; it isn’t tied to any one inherited song.

Why it can sound different as the truck passes

From a porch or sidewalk, you can hear two separate changes. The tune usually gets easier to hear as its source comes closer and harder to hear as it goes away. That’s a change in level. A building can screen some of the sound without stopping it entirely, as the Federal Highway Administration’s sound-barrier explanation illustrates. A sudden change in volume as the truck rounds a corner needn’t mean anybody reached for the volume knob.

There’s a useful contrast in the physics of sound spreading out. In an ideal open space, doubling your distance from a small source that sends sound evenly in every direction cuts the sound power arriving per unit area to a quarter. That doesn’t mean it sounds exactly a quarter as loud. And a real speaker points somewhere, streets have walls and other sounds compete for your attention. There’s no universal number of houses away at which the chime becomes audible.

Pitch is a different change. In the Doppler effect, the moving source crowds sound waves closer together in front of it and leaves them farther apart behind. As an illustration, pretend the speaker plays a steady note at 440 cycles a second. If it moved straight toward you at about 20 miles an hour in still air, the note would reach you at roughly 452 cycles per second; straight away, about 429. A real truck usually takes a path past you rather than directly toward you, and the chime itself keeps changing notes. The numbers show the size and direction of the effect, not something you have to be able to hear in a particular melody.

If you hear a truck from a safe place next time, listen for what repeats and what changes. The recognizable notes may keep coming back even as they get fainter. That’s a different question from whether music changes the way dessert tastes, which we explored in our story about music and flavor.

Quick questions about truck tunes

Do all ice cream trucks use the same song?

No. Some boxes hold just one tune, while others offer a choice. One driver can use a familiar tune again and again without every truck playing it.

Was The Entertainer written for an ice cream truck?

No. Scott Joplin’s piano rag was published in 1902. Later music-box arrangements are a different way to hear the piece.

Why can the tune change pitch as the truck goes past?

When the source moves toward you, sound waves arrive closer together, raising their frequency. When it moves away, they arrive farther apart, lowering it. That’s the Doppler effect.
