Why Did Ice Cream Once Look Like George Washington?

Published November 15, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Imagine finishing dinner and watching someone set a tiny George Washington in front of you. The nose is there. So is the collar. But before you can get a good look at the face, it starts to go soft around the edges. This is a portrait you’re supposed to eat.

That’s not just a party idea somebody dreamed up. Memphis’s Dixon Gallery and Gardens owns a pewter mold made to turn ice cream into Washington’s likeness. The same trade produced shapes as different as a dolphin and a bunch of asparagus. The fun part is figuring out what the surviving objects tell us: how the portrait came out of the metal, why some molds served a crowd, and what a dessert maker could do when the surprise needed to be inside the ice cream instead.

The little clue inside a Memphis museum object

The Dixon’s George Washington mold is an early twentieth-century piece by New York maker Eppelsheimer & Co. Look through the museum’s photos and you can see it shut, then open. The inside is the revealing view. What looks like a face in one photo becomes a hollow impression in another. One half of the metal carries the maker’s stamp and the number 1084, a useful clue when you’re looking for the same design in a catalog.

The mold was essentially a three-dimensional negative. Think of pushing a thumb into a ball of clay and lifting it away. Your thumb makes a hollow, but a cast of that hollow would give you a shape that sticks out again. A projecting nose on the dessert needs a place to go in the mold. Looking at the outside of the closed metal piece might help you guess the subject; looking into the cavity tells you what the ice cream can actually copy.

The two halves matter, too. A shape with a strong profile can be hard to lift straight out of a rigid, one-piece container. Opening the mold exposes opposite sides of the figure instead of making the whole portrait travel through one opening. Picture trying to pull a figure with a jutting chin out of a drinking glass. Even if the glass were the right size overall, the chin could catch. The hinged design makes much more sense once you picture the finished dessert getting out.

One portrait for a plate, one monument for a table

The catalog reproduced by the Dixon lists the Washington design at eight servings per quart. Divide a U.S. quart’s 32 fluid ounces by eight and you get four fluid ounces per serving on paper. That’s a helpful way to picture the scale, not a measurement of the surviving mold or a claim about how much anyone actually ate. The important part is that the whole portrait was sized as one serving. A guest didn’t have to wait for someone to divide up the face.

The same catalog shows how far the idea could go in the other direction. Its Statue of Liberty stood 37 inches high with its base, and the two parts were molded separately. The base took more ice cream than the statue. In a big display like that, you’re looking at two different experiences: the intact centerpiece people see first and the portions they get once it’s cut. A small Washington could arrive recognizable on each plate; a slice from a pedestal couldn’t tell you much about Lady Liberty without the whole dessert in view.

For comparison, the Smithsonian preserves a quart-sized rectangular mold from the Hendler family collection. It’s a brick, not a sculpture. Picture setting a block on a board and cutting straight across. The shape gives the person serving it an easy way to judge the next portion. The box and the Washington mold solve almost opposite problems: one makes the slices easier to divide; the other makes division unnecessary if every guest is getting a whole portrait.

A dolphin told you less about flavor than you might think

The Smithsonian also has an Eppelsheimer dolphin mold and a mold shaped like a bundle of asparagus. Asparagus on a museum label names the object’s shape, not the flavor of the dessert. That distinction seems obvious with a dolphin. It gets less obvious when the mold looks like something you normally eat. A fruit-shaped ice could look as though its flavor was decided before anyone took a bite, but you’d need a recipe or serving record to know.

London offers another window into the idea. The London Museum’s account of Agnes Marshall shows illustrated designs associated with her 1885 Book of Ices. The book covered more than dairy ice cream. Its pictures included forms that would look at home on different parts of a dinner table, from birds to fruits and vegetables. It’s worth looking at the pictures before reading about a dish. Ask whether you’d have recognized it as dessert at all. Then check the text to see what kind of ice Marshall meant.

Even when you do know the shape, the finish is a separate question. A dolphin has a silhouette you might recognize from across the table; details like the eye and fins reward a closer look. The Smithsonian’s record describes the possibility of coloring details on the molded dolphin. A plain version and a colored version could come from the same piece of metal. The mold established where the features went. It didn’t require the person making dessert to draw attention to them in any particular way.

An ice cream sample that wouldn’t melt

There was also an unlikely answer to the problem of showing a customer what an empty mold could do. The Dixon catalogs a small figure of Cupid as Vulcan alongside a plaster cast believed to be original. The cast shows the raised figure that the hollow mold was meant to produce. The museum also notes that the Eppelsheimer catalog listed display models separately from molds. A sample made of plaster could show someone the pose and the raised details without needing a cold plate or a fresh batch of ice cream each time.

Those are different clues. A catalog listing tells you the company offered display pieces. A surviving cast lets you compare a finished shape with a cavity. Neither tells you whether a particular Memphis dinner once ended with that figure on the table. If you see something like this in a collection, check which object the photo is showing: the tool, the solid sample, or a photograph of actual food. The same design can survive in all three forms, and each answers a different question.

What if you wanted the knife to reveal the design?

A portrait gives away its trick as soon as it arrives. William W. Cassell’s 1925 ice-cream-center mold patent shows a different idea. His drawings include molds for inserts shaped like a slipper and a heart in cross section. The insert belonged inside a larger block of ice cream. In that design, the first view of the uncut block wouldn’t tell you much. Slice across the insert, and the cut face shows the profile.

Think of the heart as having depth, like a long cookie cutter rather than a single paper cutout. If the same profile runs through the insert, the second slice can show the same shape as the first. Slice the long way instead and you’ve changed what you can see. That’s one reason the patent drawings are interesting even if you never find a photograph of a dessert made from one. They show where the design was supposed to appear and what job the cut had to do. A patent documents the proposal; it doesn’t tell us how often ice cream makers used it.

Look at the mold; leave it out of the kitchen

The Dixon warns that historic pewter molds can contain lead, and the Smithsonian specifically identifies lead in the metal of its dolphin mold. The FDA’s guidance on lead and food-contact items is another reason to treat antique pewter as an object to study, not as kitchen equipment. You don’t need to fill one to see the idea. Next time you look at a museum photo, find the seam, then look for a hollow that would become a raised detail. Try it with the nose on Washington. Once you see it that way, the open mold starts to make more sense than the closed one.
