Can You Hear Crispness? The Science of a Crunchy Bite

Published November 22, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Think of the first bite of a thin waffle chip. Before you’ve had much time to decide how it tastes, there’s a quick snap. Now try to imagine that same bite without the snap. Would it seem quite as crisp? What you hear can change how crisp a bite seems, even when the food you’re given hasn’t changed.

That’s a different trick from playing music while you eat. Our story about music and ice cream is about sound happening around the dessert. Here, the sound starts with the food itself. Researchers have used a microphone, a pair of headphones, and a stack of potato chips to find out how much that little crack can tell you—or convince you it’s telling you.

What happens when you turn up a bite?

In a 2004 experiment, Massimiliano Zampini and Charles Spence had people bite similar potato chips with their front teeth. A microphone picked up the sound next to the mouth and sent it back through headphones as the person bit down. The researchers could change the playback without swapping in a different kind of chip. Nobody had to try to match the sound of a bite they took five minutes earlier.

The team adjusted two things separately. They changed the overall level, like a volume control. And they changed the strength of the higher frequencies, more like turning up the treble without turning up everything. On each trial the participant rated either crispness or perceived freshness. On average, a louder replay or one with more of those higher frequencies made the chips seem crisper and fresher.

Timing matters here. When you hear a recording of a crunch a few seconds later, it’s clearly a recording. In the booth the sound arrived with the bite, and participants typically reported that it seemed to come from their mouths, even though they were hearing it through headphones. That makes the setup more like changing the sound of an event as it happens than asking people to rate the crunchiest sound effect.

The unusual part is what the participants thought they’d been given. When asked afterward, 15 out of 20 thought the chips had come from different packages. The researchers had used the same kind throughout. Individual chips aren’t literally identical, but the design didn’t assign fresh chips to one sound and stale ones to another. A change at the headphones was enough to shift a judgment about the food.

Notice the word seem. Those freshness ratings weren’t dates or storage measurements. The sound didn’t remove moisture or make a chip newly cooked. It gave the person a different clue about how the chip broke. That’s what makes this a clever experiment: it pries apart a clue and the fact we normally use it to guess.

Why your own crunch sounds different across the table

A bite can travel to your ears by more than one route. Some vibrations move through the surrounding air, which a microphone can pick up. Some travel through the bones of your head to your inner ear. A person sitting across the table gets the airborne sound, not the same vibrations traveling through your jaw. A recording from outside the mouth therefore captures only part of what eating sounds like to you.

In a study of air and bone conduction in food sounds, Dacremont and colleagues compared these routes across six foods. They found that the balance could change depending on whether someone bit or chewed. They didn’t find a simple rule that sorted the foods by those routes alone. That helps explain why snapping a piece in your hands, biting it, and listening to someone else eat it are different ways to listen.

It also puts the potato-chip experiment in perspective. The researchers turned up the part caught by the outside microphone. They didn’t turn off the sensations from the teeth or all the vibrations the eater could hear internally. Yet changing just the airborne playback made a difference. That’s more interesting than saying crispness comes from sound alone: one clue changed, while the eater still had other clues to work with.

Can a machine hear what a taster calls crisp?

There’s another way to ask about the sound: instead of changing what a person hears, measure what a food actually does when it breaks. In a 2018 study of nine wafer brands, a team compared two ways of fracturing the samples. One test supported the wafer at two points and pressed in the middle. The other cut through it. The instruments tracked force while a microphone picked up the sound.

Think of the difference between bending a wafer across two fingers and pressing an edge through it. Even if both produce a snap, they don’t ask the wafer to fail in quite the same way. The scientists found that sound measurements from the cutting test lined up more closely with the panel’s crispness scores in their comparison. The whole sound over the course of the cut was more informative than relying only on the single loudest moment of a bending test.

That’s a useful wrinkle if you’re tempted to buy the loudest chip. Loudness depends on how the food is broken and where the sound is measured. The wafer experiment compared a physical measurement with a tasting panel; the headphone experiment changed what a person heard. They answer different questions. One tells us something about the sound a break makes. The other tells us what can happen when the sound changes but the food stays similar.

There’s a third question you can ask at a dessert table: did the texture itself change? If a wafer sits against a wet filling, it can take up moisture and lose some of its crispness. Our article about cookies next to ice cream follows that process. Changing the wafer, changing how you break it, and changing what you hear after it breaks are three different ways to end up with a different impression of the bite.

Crisp, crunchy, and not always the same

You don’t have to sort every dessert into a neat texture category. But there are reasons why two people can agree they heard a sound and still choose different words. In Zata Vickers’s comparison of crisp and crunchy foods, the foods selected as more crisp tended to make higher-pitched eating sounds than those selected as more crunchy. The way the food was eaten also mattered. That’s a pattern from the foods tested, not a rule that defines every cracker, nut, or cone.

Nor is the headphone result a promise for every snack. In a different experiment with biscuits, blocking the sound lowered some sound-related ratings for people who started without the usual visual and auditory cues. The researchers did not find the same change for groups that started in other conditions. Having already seen or heard a food may help shape what you notice later. That’s another reason to take any one lab result as a clue, not an instruction for how dessert ought to sound.

And “crisper” isn’t the same answer as “I liked it more.” In the potato-chip study, tasters rated how crisp or fresh a bite seemed, not whether they wanted a second helping. A quick snap might be exactly what you want from a thin wafer. A softer bite might be what you want from a brownie underneath a scoop. Keeping preference separate from observation gives everyone a little more room to describe the dessert honestly.

Two quick questions before the next bite

Can sound make a chip seem fresher?

Yes, in one potato-chip experiment, people rated similar chips as fresher when they heard a louder or brighter version of their bite. The researchers changed the sound, not the age of the food.

Is a louder bite always crisper?

No. Loudness is only one cue. How a food breaks, the frequencies in the sound, and what you feel in your mouth can also affect the judgment.
