Why Are Pistachios Green? A Sweet Escape Fun Fact

Published October 23, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Pistachio is both a dessert flavor and a color word. But have you ever stopped to ask why the inside of a pistachio is green in the first place? That little kernel has a more interesting palette than its familiar name suggests.

For your next Sweet Escape conversation in Memphis, here is the short answer: pistachios contain plant pigments, including green chlorophylls. Their outer skin can contain purple pigments, too. What looks like one simple green ingredient is actually several colors and several kinds of chemistry.

Following those colors from kernel to dessert also reveals a useful distinction: explaining a pistachio’s natural pigment is not the same as identifying every ingredient in a finished scoop.

The green comes from real plant pigments

Scientists have measured the pigments inside pistachios rather than relying only on their appearance. A 2006 study of Sicilian pistachio kernels, recorded in FAO’s AGRIS research database, identified chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b among the major components.

The researchers also found lutein, a carotenoid pigment, and several compounds related to chlorophyll. Their analysis separated and measured a collection of pigments, not a single substance called “pistachio green.”

That is the first surprise: an everyday color name can hide a mixture. Saying a kernel is green describes the overall appearance. Identifying chlorophylls and carotenoids describes some of the compounds behind that appearance.

The study concerned particular Sicilian kernels. It gives us evidence about pistachio pigments, but its measurements are not a nutrition panel or an ingredient specification for any Sweet Escape dessert.

Why chlorophyll looks green

Chlorophyll is familiar from leaves, where it helps plants use light. Its color has to do with how light interacts with the plant material, not with an added green coloring.

NASA’s explanation of reflected light describes chlorophyll-rich plants absorbing strongly in red and blue portions of the spectrum while reflecting green. The pattern of absorbed and reflected wavelengths helps explain why we see green vegetation.

That is a remarkable connection to make over dessert: the pigment family behind a pistachio’s green also matters to scientists looking at vegetation from above Earth. You do not need to turn your dessert into a satellite experiment to appreciate the shared chemistry.

Keep the two questions separate, though. A pigment can help explain color without telling you how strong a food will taste. “Green” is a visual description, not a measurement of pistachio flavor.

A little autumn science belongs in this story

If chlorophyll sounds like a leaf-science word, that is because it is. NOAA’s guide to autumn leaf colors explains how green chlorophyll can dominate the appearance of leaves and mask other pigments.

Its color guide also identifies yellow pigments and red-to-purple anthocyanins. As chlorophyll fades in autumn leaves, other colors become more visible. A plant’s apparent color can therefore depend on which pigments dominate, rather than on the presence of just one pigment.

Pistachios are not tiny autumn leaves, and leaf-season changes are not a recipe for making ice cream. The connection is the broader principle: plants contain more than one color-producing compound. Looking for a single “green ingredient” can miss the rest of the palette.

The edible kernel is only one layer

Before following the purple color, it helps to know which part of the pistachio we mean. The fruit develops with an outer hull, a hard shell, and the edible kernel inside.

University of California researchers’ study of pistachio development distinguishes those tissues and identifies the kernel as the seed. The hull and shell are not simply different names for the edible center.

That vocabulary makes a description more precise. A statement about the color of a fruit’s outer hull is not automatically a statement about the kernel. Likewise, a thin skin around the kernel is not the hard shell you open.

Once you separate the layers, the next fun fact becomes easier to picture: the purple and green can belong to different parts of the same little food.

Why the skin can be purple

In Bellomo and Fallico’s 2007 pigment study, researchers identified anthocyanins in pistachio kernels’ external skin, alongside chlorophylls and lutein in the inner portion.

Those anthocyanins help explain the purple layer. Purple skin and a green center are not competing explanations; they are different pieces of the same color story.

A pistachio can therefore give you more than one answer to “what color is it?” The answer depends partly on whether you are describing the skin or the inside.

Not every kernel has an identical shade

The same 2007 study found that ripeness and the origin of the sampled products influenced pigment concentrations and color measurements. That is a reason to expect variation, not a reason to invent one universal shade that every pistachio must match.

The research compared identified samples with laboratory analysis. A glance is not that kind of test: it cannot identify a farm, harvest date, or variety.

A greener appearance alone does not establish a growing region.

Roasting can change the pigment picture

Color is not fixed forever once a pistachio is harvested. In a 2014 study involving Ohio State researchers, scientists followed chlorophylls, their breakdown products, and lutein during oven roasting.

The paper describes conversion of bright-green chlorophylls into compounds associated with more olive or yellow-brown colors. The researchers also observed early increases in some extractable pigments, which they attributed to improved extraction, before later changes in the pigment profile.

That second detail is worth keeping. A laboratory measurement can change because a compound becomes easier to extract, not simply because the food suddenly creates more green pigment. The methods and conditions matter.

The practical takeaway is modest but useful: heat treatment can affect the color story. It is not a rule that every roasted pistachio reaches one exact shade, and the study does not describe Sweet Escape’s ingredients or preparation process. The interesting part is that an ingredient does not necessarily keep the same pigment balance throughout processing.

Why an oil study can tell a different story

Here is another twist: researchers can study the oil extracted from pistachios, rather than only the kernels. A 2018 study of roasting before oil extraction found that higher roasting temperatures favored pigment transfer into the oil. The oils ranged from yellow to brilliant green under the tested treatments.

That does not cancel the kernel-roasting study. The researchers were measuring different materials and using particular conditions. Pigment movement into an extracted oil and pigment changes remaining in a kernel are not identical questions.

When two food-science headlines seem to disagree, asking “what exactly did they measure?” can be more revealing than picking the more colorful result. Neither oil color nor kernel color, by itself, supplies the formula of a frozen dessert.

A green kernel and a green dessert are different questions

Knowing that pistachios contain chlorophyll answers why the ingredient can naturally be green. It does not provide a complete explanation for the appearance of every pistachio-named frozen dessert.

A finished product has its own formula. To learn what is in it, start with that product’s description and current ingredient label. Do not work backward from a photograph and announce a percentage of nuts, a particular processing method, or the presence or absence of coloring.

This makes menu browsing simpler, not more suspicious. Color is something to notice; a product record is something to read. Neither needs to do the other’s job.

If your main question is “does this contain pistachio?” ask it directly. If your question is “what other ingredients are included?” ask for the full current information. Those questions are more useful than choosing a dessert solely because it matches an expected shade of green.
