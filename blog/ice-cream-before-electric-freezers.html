A scoop of history

How Did People Make Ice Cream Before Electric Freezers?

Published October 21, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A frozen dessert on a warm Memphis afternoon feels ordinary now. But imagine planning that treat without an electric freezer. Where would the cold come from? How would you keep it through the summer? And once you had ice, how would you turn a liquid mixture into something you could serve with a spoon?

Here is the fun fact: some early American households saved winter ice for warmer months. Harvesting, insulated storage, and hand-operated equipment worked together to make frozen desserts possible. The freezer in this story could be a container surrounded by ice, rather than an appliance plugged into a wall.

It is a history of planning and invention, but also of physical labor and unequal access. Following that chain makes a familiar dessert look a little different. Before the first spoonful came a much bigger job than choosing a flavor.

First, somebody had to harvest the cold

At George Washington's Mount Vernon, the ice supply began in the Potomac River. The estate's account describes enslaved workers cutting ice from boats in winter, hauling it uphill, and placing it in a storage well. The Washingtons' ability to serve ice cream depended in part on that work.

The ice house used a well surrounded by wooden planks, with straw between the structures and additional insulation from earth and sod. A ladder allowed workers to retrieve the stored ice. These were practical arrangements for conserving a winter resource into warmer weather, not an electric system making replacement ice as needed.

Mount Vernon also makes a useful distinction between documented facts and popular curiosity: the Washingtons served ice cream, but George Washington's favorite flavor remains unknown. A good dessert story does not need an invented favorite to be interesting. Source: Mount Vernon's Ice House.

Winter ice could become an autumn resource

Monticello provides another specific example. Its ice house stored ice or snow from the Rivanna River. The historical account describes enslaved craftsmen constructing the building and enslaved laborers working alongside hired wagoners to move the winter harvest uphill. In 1802–1803, filling the new storage space took 62 wagonloads.

The stored ice served more than dessert. Preserving meat and dairy was a central concern; making ice cream and chilling wine were additional uses. A record from 1815 says the ice lasted until October 15. That date is evidence of what happened in one recorded year, rather than a guarantee that every ice house could perform the same way.

Think of the planning involved: a supply had to be collected when conditions permitted, moved to storage, and conserved for later. The surprising part is the distance in time between the winter work and the moment someone enjoyed a cold dessert. Source: Monticello's Ice House history.

Storing ice and making ice cream were different jobs

An ice house answered the supply question. The kitchen still needed a method for freezing the dessert mixture. A surviving recipe in Thomas Jefferson's handwriting describes an inner vessel set in ice with salt, repeatedly turned and opened so frozen material could be loosened from its sides. The finished mixture went into molds, which remained surrounded by ice until serving.

The vessel was called a sabottiere, an older form of the French word for an ice-cream maker. The record shows a sequence of active steps rather than a mixture being placed on a shelf and left alone. The ice supply and the working container were separate parts of the process.

Read the manuscript as evidence of historical practice, rather than instructions for a modern kitchen. Its value here is what it reveals about the work: turning, scraping, packing, and timing were all part of producing the dessert. Source: Monticello's ice-cream recipe and history.

The salt belonged around the freezing vessel

Why would salt appear in a sweet-dessert process? In an ice-and-salt setup, salt changes the surrounding water's freezing point, allowing the bath to become colder than a plain ice-and-water mixture. The dessert is held separately inside a container while heat moves into the colder bath.

That explains how a stock of ice could support an active freezing process. Saving ice for later and creating a cold bath were related, but different, tasks. One conserved a resource; the other put it to work around the dessert mixture.

Our guide to why salt helps make ice cream explores that science in more detail, using educational sources. Here, the historical point is the arrangement: the recipe's salt-and-ice instructions concerned the surroundings of the vessel. They should not be read as a claim about any Sweet Escape recipe or production method.

A hand crank changed the equipment story

The Library of Congress identifies Nancy M. Johnson's September 9, 1843 patent for an “Artificial Freezer” as an important American ice-cream invention. Her hand-cranked maker belongs to the history of freezing equipment before electric household appliances.

A patent date is a useful anchor because it lets us attach an invention claim to a particular document. It does not mean ice cream itself began on that date. The earlier records of stored ice and ice-cream preparation already show a longer story.

The distinction helps keep food history clear: an improvement in a machine, a surviving recipe, and the invention of a food are different claims. A familiar dessert can have many important contributors across different kinds of work. Source: Library of Congress, Ice Cream Inventions.

Look closely at Johnson's original design

The original patent, US3254A, describes a beater shaft with wings reaching toward the bottom and sides of the freezing vessel. It also describes a cover over the outer wooden tub, protecting the ice, salt, and vessel from surrounding warmth. The drawing and written description let us examine what the inventor actually proposed.

You do not have to understand every mechanical detail to appreciate the design problem. A maker needed to move the mixture while managing its cold surroundings. Reading the patent makes those two concerns visible in one object.

For a small history activity, open the drawing and identify the container, the moving part, and the surrounding tub. Then compare your interpretation with the written description. The goal is observation, not building or operating a replica. Source: Nancy M. Johnson's 1843 Artificial Freezer patent.

Ice became a business as well as a winter harvest

The Library of Congress's history of the ice trade describes Frederic Tudor and Nathaniel Wyeth's roles in expanding the American harvested-ice industry. The trade reached beyond local storage: Tudor's business exported ice to the Caribbean and India.

The same account follows the rise of manufactured ice, including John Gorrie's 1851 patent and later machines associated with Ferdinand Carré. Harvested ice continued alongside artificial production before manufactured ice eventually dominated the industry. The transition involved businesses and infrastructure, as well as individual inventions.

This broadens the answer to our opening question. A household ice house is one documented arrangement, but the story of cold also includes merchants, transport, and ice factories. There was no single moment when every place switched from winter harvesting to the same new technology. Source: Library of Congress, Ice Cold: The Business of Ice.

Three questions that make an old object more interesting

When you see an antique ice-cream maker in a museum photograph, try looking beyond its age. Ask where its ice came from, what moved inside it, and who did the work. Those questions connect an object to the larger system that made it useful.

Keep the answers specific to the evidence. A drawing might reveal a crank but say little about the person operating it. An inventory might list a vessel without recording every occasion on which it was used. A photograph can start a question without settling it.

This is a good family conversation because nobody needs to memorize a timeline. Each person can spot one detail and explain what they think it did. Then use the linked museum or library record to check the idea. Curiosity works best when a guess remains a guess until the evidence supports it.
