A curious cold-weather fact for dessert fans

Why Is Dry Ice “Dry”? A Strange Frozen Fact

Published October 31, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Here is a frozen-dessert fact that sounds like a magic trick: a piece of dry ice can seem to smoke, stay solid, and disappear without leaving a puddle. It is not ordinary water ice with a dramatic nickname. Dry ice is solid carbon dioxide, and at everyday air pressure it takes a different route back to the gas phase.

That strange change of state helps explain the eerie white fog seen around dry-ice displays and demonstrations. The cloud is not smoke, and it is not a stream of visible carbon dioxide. The real explanation is a small meeting between a very cold solid and the water already floating invisibly in the air.

It is a fun fact for anyone who likes the science behind frozen treats. It is also a subject where a little safety context matters: dry ice is not a novelty to touch or taste. Let’s separate what looks spooky from what the chemistry actually says.

The short answer: dry ice skips the puddle

When a cube of water ice warms in an ordinary room, it melts. Its molecules move from an ordered solid into liquid water, which can spread across a table as a puddle. If you keep heating that water, it can eventually become water vapor.

Dry ice behaves differently under normal room pressure. As solid carbon dioxide warms, it changes directly into carbon-dioxide gas instead of first becoming a liquid. Chemists call that solid-to-gas transition sublimation. Because there is no liquid carbon dioxide left behind on the table, the surrounding surface does not get wet in the familiar way that it does under a melting ice cube.

The American Chemical Society’s classroom explanation makes the comparison easy to picture: put regular ice and dry ice on separate paper towels and the water ice leaves a wet mark as it melts, while the dry ice does not melt into liquid water. This is a general chemistry illustration, not a suggestion to handle dry ice at home.

Sublimation has a reverse partner, deposition: gas can turn directly into a solid. Frost forming on a cold surface is a familiar example of water vapor depositing as ice crystals, without first forming a visible puddle. Dry ice’s room-temperature story runs in the other direction, from solid carbon dioxide to gas. The everyday words “melt” and “freeze” describe liquid-solid changes, so sublimation is the more precise word for what a dry-ice block does in open air.

Water can sublimate too. The USGS describes snow and ice becoming water vapor without first melting, especially in dry, windy conditions. So the trick is not exclusive to carbon dioxide. What makes a dry-ice block memorable is that sublimation is its expected warming route at normal atmospheric pressure. A disappearing patch of snow and a vanishing dry-ice block can involve the same category of phase change, even though the substances and conditions differ.

A temperature cold enough to change the route

At standard atmospheric pressure, carbon dioxide’s sublimation point is about −78.5 °C, or −109.3 °F. That is far colder than the temperature at which water freezes. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health lists dry ice as solid carbon dioxide and gives the same approximate sublimation temperature.

Temperature alone does not tell the whole story. Pressure matters too. A substance’s phase diagram maps which forms—solid, liquid, or gas—are stable at different temperatures and pressures. At one atmosphere, carbon dioxide does not have a stable liquid phase, so warming a dry-ice block takes it from solid to gas. With sufficient pressure and the right temperature, liquid carbon dioxide can exist. The route you see in open air is not a rule for every carbon-dioxide sample under every possible condition.

The triple point is the temperature-pressure combination where all three phases can coexist. NIST’s carbon-dioxide data place it around −56.6 °C and 5.1 atmospheres. So “dry ice never becomes liquid” is too broad: liquid carbon dioxide exists under pressure, but not in an ordinary open-air demonstration.

The white fog is mostly tiny water droplets

Carbon dioxide gas is colorless and invisible. So what is the billowing white cloud? The dry ice cools nearby air; water vapor in that humid air condenses into tiny liquid droplets. Those droplets scatter light, making a visible mist. The American Chemical Society describes the cloud as condensed water vapor carried along by cold carbon-dioxide gas.

Think of the fog as a miniature cloud: invisible water vapor in the air cools into visible droplets. Humidity affects how dramatic the cloud looks, and warming air eventually lets the droplets evaporate. The carbon dioxide remains invisible.

The cloud may flow downward, but its path also depends on air currents, heat, and the room. It is never a safety detector: carbon dioxide is invisible and odorless, so sight and smell cannot tell you whether ventilation is adequate.

There is a small language trap here: people often call the entire effect “carbon-dioxide smoke.” That is a useful visual shorthand, not a literal description of the cloud. The droplets are liquid water suspended in air; the carbon dioxide that chilled and moves the mixture cannot be picked out by sight. This is why photographs show the fog but cannot tell you how much carbon dioxide is present or where invisible gas has moved.

A French experiment put the odd solid on the map

The solid has a longer history than its theatrical appearance might suggest. ASHRAE’s refrigeration chronology lists Thilorier’s first production of dry ice in 1835. The same timeline records Faraday liquefying a series of gases in the 1820s. These were steps in understanding how pressure and temperature could transform materials people usually encountered as gases.

That history adds a nice twist to the spectacle: a dramatic modern-looking cloud rests on chemistry explored nearly two centuries ago. The surprise is not that a special kind of water has been invented. It is that another substance, carbon dioxide, has been cooled into an unfamiliar form. Knowing what the solid actually is makes the disappearing-block effect more interesting, not less.

Today, dry ice has a practical role as a cold source for transporting products that need to remain frozen. The solid gradually sublimates, so it can provide cooling without turning into a pool of liquid water. That distinction is useful for a shipment where meltwater would be inconvenient. It does not mean every frozen-food package uses dry ice; packaging methods vary, and this article makes no claim about Sweet Escape’s shipping, storage, or production practices.

Dry ice also appears in food demonstrations because its fog is dramatic. FDA’s Food Code reference material notes that dry ice has been used for smoke effects in food and beverages, while warning that it should not be directly consumed or allowed to contact exposed skin. A theatrical effect and an edible ingredient are not the same thing. The fact that a material is used near food in some controlled contexts never means a loose piece is safe to swallow, touch, or leave where a guest might encounter it.

How it relates to ice cream—and where the comparison ends

Ice cream itself is a water-based frozen dessert with its own changing structure as it warms. Dry ice is a separate material: frozen carbon dioxide. Both invite questions about temperature and state changes, but one should not be mistaken for the other. A scoop softening in a dish is not sublimation, and a dry-ice fog effect does not tell you anything about a dessert’s ingredients or recipe.

Dry ice is fascinating—but not a toy

Dry ice is extremely cold and can cause frostbite after skin contact. Never pick it up with bare hands, put it in someone’s mouth, or use it to chill a drink where a person could swallow or touch a piece. The FDA warns that it should not be directly consumed or touch exposed skin.

As dry ice warms, it releases carbon dioxide gas. In a confined or poorly ventilated space, that gas can build up and displace oxygen. Because carbon dioxide is colorless and odorless, follow current supplier and workplace safety instructions; do not rely on your senses to judge a room.

Never put dry ice in an airtight container: gas can build pressure until the container ruptures. CDC guidance calls for vented storage and a well-ventilated area. This article is not a handling procedure.

The fog can make the solid harder to see, so enjoy a demonstration from a safe distance and leave the material to trained adults.
