The surprisingly useful science of stopping partway

Why Doesn’t Churning Ice Cream Just Make Butter?

Published November 9, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Churn cream and you can make butter. Churn an ice cream mixture and you want a scoopable dessert. So why does one moving paddle not lead to the same result as the other? The surprising answer is that ice cream can benefit from fat droplets joining together—but only to a controlled extent.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, that is a fun question to bring to your next dessert conversation. The interesting difference is not simply “one is colder.” It is the structure being built. This explanation concerns dairy-based ice cream science, not every frozen-dessert formulation or a specific Sweet Escape production method.

Begin with droplets, not a smooth-looking liquid

The American Oil Chemists’ Society explains emulsions as tiny droplets of one liquid dispersed within another liquid that would not ordinarily mix with it. Milk is an oil-in-water example: small fat droplets sit within the watery surrounding phase.

“Water phase” does not mean a glass of plain water hidden in the milk. It identifies the surrounding part of the emulsion. Thinking about which material surrounds which is more useful here than thinking only about the mixture’s overall creamy appearance.

The society also describes how emulsifiers help droplets remain dispersed. Their molecular structure can interact with both oil and water at the boundary between them. The important starting idea is that a mixture can look uniform to us while containing many separate microscopic droplets. Smooth-looking does not mean structurally simple.

Butter changes which part surrounds which

Tetra Pak’s Dairy Processing Handbook describes butter as principally a water-in-oil emulsion. That reverses the cream example: the finished butter has a continuous fat phase containing dispersed water.

In the handbook’s butter-making description, agitation brings fat together into butter grains. The cream separates into grains and buttermilk; after the liquid is drained, working the butter develops the continuous fat phase and distributes the remaining water finely within it.

That is a much more specific change than “the cream became thicker.” The identity of the surrounding phase changes, and liquid is separated from the grains during the process. A thick spoonful and a piece of butter are therefore not just different points on a single thickness scale. Their internal arrangements differ, even though milk fat and water belong in both stories.

Ice cream needs some joining, not the butter endpoint

The University of Guelph’s ice cream structure chapter describes fat globules forming a partially coalesced structure that helps stabilize incorporated air bubbles. In plain language, some droplets associate into clusters without the whole mixture becoming a continuous mass of butter.

The chapter connects this to whipped cream and notes the boundary explicitly: whipping too far can form butter particles, including in ice cream. So the answer is not that ice cream is incapable of buttering. The useful structure sits short of that unwanted result.

“Partial” is doing important work. A controlled change in the emulsion can help build the dessert, while pushing the system farther can produce a different outcome. For this particular food, complete resistance to every droplet interaction is not the goal either.

The starting droplets have already been prepared

Guelph’s separate homogenization chapter explains that this stage reduces the size of milk-fat globules and increases their total surface area. The newly created surfaces need an interfacial covering; the chapter lists membrane formation among the resulting changes.

There is a useful geometrical idea here. Divide a given amount of material into smaller droplets and you create more surface to manage. The amount of fat alone does not describe that new arrangement. Droplet size and the material at each droplet’s surface also matter.

For an idealized thought experiment, divide one spherical droplet into eight equal-volume spherical droplets. Each new droplet has half the original radius. The total volume stays the same, but the total surface area doubles. That is geometry, not a measured example from an ice cream factory.

Homogenization and churning therefore describe different jobs. One prepares the fat emulsion; later whipping and freezing develop other parts of the finished structure. Treating every mechanical stage as simply “mixing harder” hides the sequence. The history of the mixture before it reaches a freezer belongs in the explanation too.

Cooling can prepare the fat before freezing

The same book’s chapter on ageing an ice cream mix describes time allowed for fat crystallization, protein and stabilizer hydration, and rearrangement at fat-droplet surfaces. Fat crystallization helps make later partial coalescence possible.

This is a second reason the paddle alone cannot explain the result. The mixture arriving at that stage has a particular physical condition, not merely a list of ingredient names. Some changes take place during a controlled cooling-and-holding stage rather than being created instantly by the final whipping action.

In this manufacturing vocabulary, “ageing” is preparation of the mix. It is not a claim that the dessert is fermented, old, or served after an extended storage period. Nor does this general description give us a verified timing or temperature program for any Sweet Escape product.

An emulsifier can have a counterintuitive job

If an emulsifier can help keep droplets apart, why add one when some joining is wanted? A 1989 study in the Journal of Dairy Science examined different emulsifiers in ice cream processing. It described displacement of protein from fat-globule surfaces and greater susceptibility to coalescence during agitation and ice formation.

That is the apparent contradiction resolved: the surface is being adjusted for a particular food and stage, not given a universal instruction to remain unchanged forever. The paper found that different emulsifiers did not have identical destabilizing effects.

For a curious eater, the useful lesson is that an ingredient category does not explain its complete function by itself. “Emulsifier” names a role at interfaces, but the surrounding formulation and process still count. It is not a basis for guessing an exact recipe from the word on a label.

Researchers can change the paddle conditions

In a 2018 study by Maya Warren and Richard Hartel, researchers varied emulsifier content, incorporated air, and dasher speed. The dasher is the moving mixing element in this processing context.

Conditions that increased shear stress in their freezer produced smaller air cells and a greater extent of fat destabilization. The researchers also examined how the resulting structures affected drip-through during melting. They were measuring interacting features, not merely timing how long a machine ran.

This helps explain why “churn for the same number of minutes” is not a universal scientific description of two different mixtures or machines. A time value leaves out the mechanical conditions and formulation. The study describes its own experimental products; it does not establish a preferred setting for a home appliance or a shop’s equipment.

Protein can change the result too

A 2015 study by Daw and Hartel investigated ice creams made with different milk-protein sources and blends. In those formulations, increasing protein generally reduced the amount of partially coalesced fat, with an exception for mixes made using only nonfat dry milk.

The exception is worth keeping. “More protein” was not a single, context-free switch giving the same result in every mix. The source of the protein was part of the experiment.

Read this as a structure finding, not nutritional advice or a ranking of desserts. It shows why replacing one component can alter more than the label’s headline number. The mixture has several interacting parts, and a result from a controlled formulation does not automatically predict another recipe’s behavior.

Butter’s remaining water is part of its structure

The Canadian Dairy Commission’s ingredient Q&A adds a useful detail to the butter side. It explains that churning cream produces butter grains and releases buttermilk, while working the grains disperses retained moisture into fine droplets.

Its discussion of water appearing on butter’s surface relates that separation to the size and association of the water droplets. That is another reminder that the ingredients present and their arrangement are different questions.

Compare the two destinations, then: the ice cream explanation centers on controlled fat clusters supporting a foam, while the butter explanation centers on a continuous fat phase containing water droplets. Shared ingredients and movement do not make the final structures identical.

What this adds to your next scoop

The short answer is a balance, not an absolute barrier. Dairy ice cream can use limited fat-droplet association; butter-making takes cream toward a different emulsion arrangement. Preparation, formulation, and mechanical conditions help determine where the process ends up.
