A milkshake and a small invention

A Milkshake Helped Inspire the Bendy Straw

Published November 5, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Here is a milkshake fact worth bringing to your next Sweet Escape visit in Memphis: a child’s difficulty reaching her drink helped inspire the bendable straw. The prototype involved a paper straw, a screw, and dental floss. No new ice-cream flavor was needed. The interesting change happened in the little tube between the glass and the person enjoying it.

The story belongs to inventor Joseph B. Friedman and his daughter Judith. It is a good reminder that dessert history includes more than recipes. Sometimes a small inconvenience at the counter becomes a problem someone decides to solve. Follow this one and you find folded paper, stubborn manufacturing challenges, a later redesign, and even the physics hiding inside an ordinary sip.

The milkshake that suggested a different shape

The Smithsonian’s Lemelson Center recounts the scene at the Varsity Sweet Shop, Friedman’s brother’s fountain parlor in San Francisco. During the 1930s, Friedman watched young Judith struggle to drink a milkshake through a straight paper straw.

His experiment used a screw inside the tube. Dental floss pressed the paper into the screw’s grooves, leaving a corrugated section. Once the screw was removed, that section could bend over the glass’s edge. The drink could stay where it was while the mouthpiece changed direction.

It is an appealingly specific origin story: not “someone invented something useful,” but a father noticing a reach problem. The unexpected tools make it memorable, too. This is a description of a historical prototype, not advice to put hardware or dental supplies into a drinking utensil. The clever part to carry away is the change in geometry.

Why the little ridges mattered

Friedman’s 1937 Drinking Tube patent explains the intended difference between a useful bend and a squashed tube. Its goal was to let the mouthpiece change angle without substantially narrowing the passage through which the liquid traveled.

The flexible region sat near one end, positioned above the container’s rim. Corrugations ran around the straw there, while the longer section could remain in the drink. The drawing makes the arrangement easy to follow: a glass, a mostly upright tube, and an angled mouthpiece.

That is more precise than saying Friedman simply made a straw that could be bent. A tube that folds shut would defeat its own purpose. The design had two jobs at once: accommodate a different drinking position and preserve an open route for the beverage.

If you look at the patent illustration, compare the small flexible region with the rest of the tube. The change is concentrated where the direction needs to change. You can understand the main idea without decoding every line of the specification.

Paper straws were already part of the story

It is easy to picture a modern plastic bendy straw and assume the invention began with plastic. The Smithsonian account instead describes paper. An earlier document supplies another useful piece of context: Marvin Stone’s Artificial Straw patent, issued January 3, 1888.

Stone described a paper strip wound into a tube, with an adhesive securing its edge and a waterproof coating such as paraffin. His specification explicitly discussed replacing natural straws. The material, the seam, and the coating were all parts of the construction problem.

Put the two documents side by side and their questions differ. Stone’s concerns included forming and protecting a paper tube; Friedman’s later design addressed a controlled bend. Those are separate improvements, not competing answers to one identical problem.

This also keeps the history grounded. A patent for a particular artificial straw is not evidence that nobody had ever used a hollow stem for drinking before. The earlier document itself mentions natural straws already in use.

A prototype was only the beginning

The Smithsonian’s guide to Friedman’s papers follows the idea beyond the counter. Friedman tried unsuccessfully to sell his straw patent to existing manufacturers, then pursued production himself. The Flexible Straw Corporation was incorporated in California in 1939.

World War II interrupted work on his manufacturing machine. Financial support from family members and an associate, together with business advice from his sister Betty, helped him complete the first machine in the late 1940s. Betty also handled sales and distribution. This was a family-supported business effort, not just one isolated flash of inspiration.

Another surprise: the archive identifies hospitals as an initial market and dates the first sale to 1947. The observation began with a child at a dessert counter, but the early customers were not simply more soda shops.

The collection includes sketches, blueprints, correspondence, photographs, and company records. Together they document several different kinds of work: conceiving an object, figuring out production, and finding customers. A memorable prototype can begin that sequence without completing it.

The bend created a new problem to solve

A later Friedman patent, issued May 1, 1951, reveals a wonderfully ordinary snag. Raised ridges on neighboring straws could interlock in a dispensing container. Pulling out one could bring other straws along with it.

The proposed improvement kept the flexible region within the maximum diameter of the smooth end sections. In other words, the bendable part still needed to work, but its outer shape also needed to behave among other straws.

The document addressed packaging as well, describing a coating intended to make the flexible portion temporarily rigid and to fracture when the end was adjusted. These were proposals in that particular historical patent, not a description of every straw sold today.

This is our favorite twist in the story. An object can work by itself and still cause trouble when a whole batch is packed together. Friedman’s later problem was no longer just “Can a child reach the drink?” It was also “Can someone remove one straw without accidentally collecting its neighbors?” That second question is small, practical, and very revealing.

The air around your glass helps move the drink

There is another fun fact inside the same object: ordinary straw drinking depends on a pressure difference. The University of Guelph’s physics demonstration explains that lowering the pressure inside the straw allows the higher air pressure on the drink’s exposed surface to push liquid up toward your mouth.

So “sucking up a drink” is a convenient everyday description, but it leaves out the atmosphere’s role. Your mouth creates the lower-pressure end of the arrangement; the surrounding air supplies pressure on the liquid in the glass.

The university’s video uses a smoothie and compares different straw arrangements. It is a useful companion to the patent drawing because the two explain different things. Friedman’s design concerns the tube’s position and shape. The demonstration concerns what makes liquid travel through an open tube.

Neither explanation requires the straw to contain a tiny pump. That invisible pressure difference is part of the action even when the only thing you notice is a sip arriving.

Even scientists find an ordinary straw interesting

Straw flow is not always as simple as one familiar classroom formula suggests. In a Journal of Fluid Mechanics study of finite-length pipes, researchers tested slender stirrer-type straws, standard soda straws, and larger bubble-tea straws over a range of pressures.

They found that typical sipping conditions could involve several flow effects together, including the entrance region and the distinction between laminar and turbulent flow. Their results challenge the assumption that ordinary straw drinking always follows the simple law for fully developed laminar flow.

That does not turn the study into a prediction for your particular milkshake. It does show why a tiny everyday object can be a serious research subject. The tube’s length, opening, and flow conditions belong in the explanation, not just the fact that it has a hole.

For a dessert conversation, that is enough to make the object interesting twice: first as a human-centered invention, then as a small example of fluid mechanics.
