Peanuts Grow Underground—But They Aren’t Roots

Published November 1, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A peanut-butter ice-cream name can hide a surprisingly unusual plant story. Peanuts grow underground, but they are not little swellings on a plant’s roots. Their journey starts with flowers above the soil, followed by a structure that carries the developing fruit downward.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, familiar dessert ingredients often give us a good reason to look a little closer. The peanut is a particularly good example: its everyday name, its place underground, and the part we eat can all lead us toward the wrong picture of the plant.

Here is the useful mental image: green plant above, roots below, and peanut pods connected to special downward-growing pegs. The edible kernels are seeds inside those pods. Once you separate those parts, the whole underground arrangement becomes much easier to understand.

The shell is a fruit, and the kernel is a seed

Botanically, the cultivated peanut is Arachis hypogaea, a member of the legume family, Fabaceae. Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew places it with relatives such as peas, beans, lentils, and chickpeas rather than treating it as a true nut.

That does not mean anyone needs to rename their snack bowl. Everyday food names and botanical descriptions serve different purposes. “Peanut” is a perfectly familiar food name; “legume” tells us something more specific about the plant and its fruit.

The University of Kentucky’s peanut crop profile distinguishes the outer pod, also called the hull or shell, from the seed or kernel inside. So the rough shell around an in-shell peanut is part of a fruit. The peanut you eat after removing it is a seed, not a piece of root.

In botany, fruit does not have to mean something juicy or sweet. Here it describes a reproductive structure associated with the flower. That is why “underground fruit” makes sense even though it sounds like a contradiction at first.

The flowers begin above ground

A peanut plant is not a tree with nuts falling into the soil. It is a low-growing plant that produces small flowers above ground. Kew describes leaves made up of four smaller leaflets and flowers that can appear in yellow-to-orange shades.

Those flowers are not underground buds waiting to turn into roots. They belong to the plant’s reproductive system. According to Clemson Cooperative Extension, flowers arise where leaves join the stem. After fertilization, a specialized structure begins extending toward the ground.

This is the step that makes a peanut different from the familiar picture of a pea pod hanging above the soil. The flower starts in the air, but the developing peanut fruit does not stay there. Its next stage depends on reaching the space below the surface.

A peg takes the developing fruit into the soil

The downward-growing structure is commonly called a peg. Clemson explains that the developing fruit is at its tip. The peg penetrates the soil, then pod enlargement and seed development continue underground.

It can help to imagine the peg as a connection between the flower’s reproductive structure and the place where the fruit matures. It is not an ordinary root that happens to have acquired a peanut. Its origin and job are different, even though both structures enter the soil.

The name for this underground fruit development is geocarpy. University of Florida IFAS uses the term to describe the contrast between aboveground flowering and belowground fruit maturation. The unusual part is the sequence, not simply the fact that something edible was found under dirt.

That also explains why a photograph of leaves alone cannot show the full crop. Much of the development that matters is out of sight. A drawing that shows the pegs and pods can tell the story much more clearly than a photograph of the green canopy.

Going underground is a developmental turning point

The peg is not burying a fully formed snack. Early embryo development can pause above ground and resume after soil entry, in darkness. A 2016 study in BMC Genomics examined this transition by comparing the tip and basal regions of peanut pegs before and after they entered the soil.

The researchers observed changes in embryo development and differences in gene activity, including genes associated with light signaling and plant hormones. Their work helps describe a coordinated developmental change; it is not evidence of one simple switch that explains every part of the process.

For a dessert fan, the takeaway is wonderfully concrete: the underground stage is part of making the fruit, not merely storing it. The developing pod and seed are still changing after the peg reaches the soil. That is a much more interesting journey than imagining a finished peanut attached to a root.

Roots have a separate underground job

Peanut plants still have real roots. Those roots are separate from the pegs and fruit. They also have an interesting association with bacteria: Utah State University Extension describes the nitrogen-fixing bacteria associated with peanut root nodules, as in other legumes.

That gives us two very different underground structures to keep apart. A root nodule is not a peanut pod. A pod holds developing seeds; a nodule belongs to the root-and-bacteria association. Both can appear in a diagram of the same plant without being interchangeable parts.

The distinction is a useful reminder that location does not define a plant organ. “Underground” tells us where to look. It does not, by itself, tell us whether we are seeing a root, a fruit, or another structure.

There is another protective layer inside the shell

The rough outer shell and the thin skin around an individual kernel are not the same covering. The University of Minnesota’s seed-morphology guide uses peanuts to explain this difference: the shell comes from the fruit wall, while the red or tan skin is a seed coat.

Inside, the familiar two large halves are cotyledons, or seed leaves. A University of Georgia peanut-production guide illustrates the seed’s cotyledons and embryo, including the parts that can develop into a new plant’s shoot and root.

This is a different use of the word “leaf” from the green leaves on a mature plant. The cotyledons are part of the seed’s early-life structure. In a botanical diagram, you can follow protective shell, seed coat, and seed contents as distinct layers rather than treating everything inside the shell as one featureless nut.

Peanut types are not dessert recipes

NC State Extension’s Plant Toolbox describes four principal peanut types grown in the United States: Runner, Virginia, Spanish, and Valencia. Runner peanuts are commonly associated with peanut-butter production, while Virginia types have large kernels and are often sold in the shell.

That is useful crop background, but it cannot identify the peanuts in a particular dessert. A flavor name such as Peanut Butter Cup does not tell you the cultivar, farm, growing region, roasting method, or exact peanut-butter formulation.

Think of the crop type as one possible starting detail in a much longer ingredient story. It should not be filled in by guesswork just because a common commercial use exists. The general plant fact and the details of a finished product are separate questions.

Harvesting means lifting the underground crop

Because the pods are below ground, harvesting looks different from collecting fruit from a branch. Purdue Extension describes lifting the plant from the soil with its peanuts still attached. Its account also notes that flowering continues through the season, so pods can be at different developmental stages.

NC State’s peanut-breeding report explains why this creates a harvesting challenge: the plant is harvested together even though its pods have not all developed on the same timetable. That is more complicated than waiting for every individual peanut to send a visible signal above ground.

The story does not end when a plant is lifted, either. Purdue describes drying before further processing. None of that establishes the supplier or processing history of a Sweet Escape ingredient; it simply shows why the route from field to familiar food includes more than picking a ready-made snack.

“Not a true nut” does not mean allergy-safe

The botanical surprise should never be turned into a shortcut for choosing food with an allergy. The FDA lists peanuts and tree nuts separately among major food allergens. Calling a peanut a legume does not remove its allergen significance.

Nor should a plant-family relationship be used to predict what an individual can safely eat. For a dessert decision, the relevant information is the exact product, its current ingredients and allergen statements, and how it is handled. Botany answers what the plant is; it does not replace those checks.
