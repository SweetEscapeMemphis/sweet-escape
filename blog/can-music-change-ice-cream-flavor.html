A fun fact for your next dessert conversation

Can Music Change How Ice Cream Tastes?

Published October 30, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Here is an ice-cream fact worth bringing to the table: researchers have changed the sounds people heard while they ate, then recorded changes in their descriptions of the dessert. The spoonful did not need a new recipe for the experience to shift.

That raises a delicious question for a music-loving city like Memphis: can your playlist actually change how ice cream tastes? The careful answer is that sound can influence perceived flavor under studied conditions. It does not add sugar, replace ingredients, or guarantee that everyone will notice a difference.

Let’s look at the experiments, separate three easily confused measurements, and turn the idea into an enjoyable little comparison—not a contest to find the scientifically correct dessert song.

First, sweetness is not the whole flavor

The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders distinguishes basic tastes, including sweetness and bitterness, from the wider experience of flavor. Aroma, temperature, texture, and other sensations contribute too.

That distinction gives us better questions. “Did it seem sweeter?” asks about one quality. “Did cocoa stand out first?” asks about attention to a flavor characteristic. “Did you enjoy it?” asks about preference. A person could answer those questions differently without contradicting themselves.

Keep those three questions separate as you read the research. Otherwise, a finding about which sensation captured attention can quietly become an exaggerated claim that music improved the entire dessert.

The experiment with chocolate ice cream and personal playlists

In a 2022 study published in Foods, 64 adults evaluated chocolate ice cream under different musical and non-musical sound conditions. Researchers selected liked and disliked music using each participant’s own ratings, rather than declaring one genre universally pleasant.

Participants tracked the sensation that stood out as they ate. Under liked music, sweetness had the longest dominance duration. Other conditions brought cocoa, bitterness, or roasted characteristics into prominence. The researchers also collected emotional responses.

“Dominant” here means the sensation attracting attention at a particular moment. It is not automatically the most intense sensation or a vote for the best flavor. The study concerned one chocolate ice-cream preparation, and the authors called for further work in real eating environments.

That is an interesting result precisely because it is specific: a timed sensory description changed alongside the soundtrack. It is not a recipe for making every scoop sweeter with one famous song.

Birds and machinery entered the picture too

A 2019 ice-cream study used a café soundscape, then added bird, machine, or forest sounds. Researchers followed flavor descriptions over time rather than asking only for a final overall score.

With café-and-forest sounds, sweetness and creaminess stood out early. With the café control, cocoa stood out early instead. Bitterness appeared toward the end with the café control and café-plus-machine recordings. Emotional responses also differed across conditions.

The timeline matters. Saying “forest sounds made ice cream sweet” leaves out when the sensation emerged and what was compared. The more useful finding is that the sequence of noticeable sensations differed.

It also broadens the question beyond songs. An eating environment contains sounds even when nobody has pressed play on a playlist. This experiment investigated particular recordings; it did not establish that every real café or outdoor setting creates the same result.

A laboratory booth is not a dessert outing

Another team examined chocolate gelato in laboratory, immersive, and natural eating environments. Their 2018 paper recorded which characteristics participants noticed over time while hearing disliked, neutral, liked, or no music.

Sweetness, cocoa, and milkiness were reported more often in the natural eating environment than in the laboratory. Liked and neutral music increased sweetness reports, while disliked music increased bitterness reports.

Those reports describe what participants selected during tasting, not a chemical analysis of the gelato. They also show why context belongs in the discussion: a controlled booth and a more ordinary eating setting are not interchangeable.

For a customer, the useful question becomes “What was happening around this tasting?” A conclusion based on a particular environment deserves that context when we repeat it. None of these experiments was conducted at Sweet Escape, and they do not measure our shop’s atmosphere.

Different flavor descriptions do not always mean more enjoyment

The “Smooth Operator” chocolate experiment offers an especially useful distinction. Participants tasted identical chocolates twice without knowing they were identical. Each tasting had a contrasting soundtrack designed around creamy or rough associations.

The creamy-associated soundtrack produced higher perceived creaminess and sweetness ratings than the rough-associated one. Yet the soundtrack difference did not appear to change overall enjoyment of the chocolates.

This was chocolate, not ice cream, so it should not be presented as another frozen-dessert trial. Its value here is the separation of measurements: a sensory rating can shift without an accompanying change in how much someone likes the food.

That is a good antidote to headlines promising an automatic upgrade. Noticing a different characteristic and preferring the experience are two distinct outcomes. A dessert does not need to win both for the observation to be interesting.

Even enjoyment results depended on the gelato

A study of background music and food pleasantness tested 50 people with milk-chocolate, dark-chocolate, and bittersweet-chocolate gelati. Participants heard music differing in their own preference, with silence as a reference.

Non-preferred music reduced pleasantness ratings for all three types. Preferred music increased pleasantness ratings for dark and bittersweet gelati, but not milk-chocolate gelato.

That final difference is worth keeping. Even within one experiment involving related chocolate desserts, the result was not identical across every product. “Favorite music makes all ice cream better” would be a much broader statement than the findings support.

If you share this fact with friends, include the comparison and the exception. They make the story more surprising, not less: perception can respond to context without following a simple rule that works for every food.

Try a listening comparison without chasing a result

For a casual version at home, use a dessert you already know is suitable for you. Choose two short audio selections at a comfortable volume, or compare one selection with quiet. There is no reason to use unpleasant or loud sounds.

Decide what you want to compare before the first spoonful. One option is a familiar favorite against another track you also enjoy. Another is a single instrumental passage against the ordinary quiet of the room. Give the selections neutral labels such as A and B, rather than names that predict a flavor.

Keep the listening setup consistent too: changing from headphones to a room speaker at the same time would add another difference. Do not interrupt conversation or impose a soundtrack on other people. This works best as a brief, optional activity everyone has agreed to try.

Keep the dessert choice the same. Adding a topping or switching flavors between listens would create a different question. Use separate clean spoons for different people, and let everyone write a note before discussing it.

Make three small entries: what stood out first, how sweet the spoonful seemed, and how much you enjoyed it. Use a simple scale if helpful, but give each question its own answer. “No difference” is a perfectly useful observation.

For example, reserve one line for a first-impression word and two lines for ratings from one to five. Define the endpoints before starting: barely sweet to very sweet for one line, and not enjoyable to very enjoyable for the other. These are personal descriptions, not measurements of sugar or a professional quality grade.

Keep everyone’s results separate. If one friend gives sweetness a four and another gives it a two, do not assume they use numbers identically. The more useful comparison is each person’s answer under A versus that same person’s answer under B. Ask what they meant by their words before trying to explain the difference.

On another occasion, reverse the listening order. This is still a casual comparison, not a controlled scientific replication. Its purpose is to make the idea tangible while leaving room for uncertainty.

Save the notes with the track names and order. If you try again, compare your own observations before looking at anyone else’s. A change that appears only once should stay a one-time observation, not become a rule about a genre or a particular dessert.

Watch the melting clock—and the power of a leading question

Do not deliberately leave a dessert sitting out to extend the activity. Follow its storage directions and ordinary safe handling. If the second spoonful has become noticeably softer, record that difference instead of assigning the whole experience to music. Our temperature-and-sweetness article explores that separate question.

Also avoid announcing, “This is the sweet song.” Ask an open question instead. You are trying to hear everyone’s observations, not supply the answer in advance.

A useful note might read: “Sweetness stood out first during the first track; I liked both spoonfuls equally; the second was softer.” It is more informative than “Song A won,” because it preserves the different observations and a possible complication.
