A different kind of scoop

Why Does Turkish Ice Cream Stretch?

Published November 3, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Ice cream does not always break away in a tidy scoop. Some Turkish ice cream can pull into a long, cohesive stretch—a very different sight from a spoonful that simply curls, crumbles, or drips. The surprising part is that this behavior has a food-science explanation, not just a clever serving trick.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, we enjoy questions that make a familiar dessert feel new again. Why would one ice cream stretch while another does not? The answer leads to a regional tradition, an ingredient made from certain orchid tubers, and the difference between being thick and being elastic.

Here we are exploring Maraş-style ice cream from Türkiye, not announcing a Turkish ice-cream offering at Sweet Escape. The useful discovery travels well: texture is a part of dessert worth noticing, and a flavor name does not tell its whole story.

Start with a place, not just a stretching video

GoTürkiye’s guide to Kahramanmaraş identifies the city as the home of Maraş ice cream. Its account of the traditional preparation names milk, sahlep, and sugar, with goat’s milk used traditionally. That gives the texture a culinary setting beyond an isolated surprising image.

The same guide describes adding sahlep and sugar to heated milk, stirring until the mixture becomes gummy, then resting and cooling it. This is an overview of a tradition, not a complete home recipe. The sequence matters because the finished dessert depends on what happens to the mixture, as well as which ingredients go into it.

It is also worth being specific about the style. A discussion of Maraş ice cream should not become a claim that every frozen dessert made anywhere in Türkiye has one identical formula or behaves in precisely the same way.

The orchid connection is underground

Salep, also written sahlep, is the unusual ingredient at the center of this story. Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew explains that a powder from the tuberous underground parts of certain orchids has thickening properties and is used in dondurma.

That does not mean colorful orchid petals are being stirred into the ice cream. The plant part matters. An orchid flower, a vanilla pod, and a tuber are not interchangeable ingredients simply because they come from members of the same large plant family.

There is a nice connection to another dessert fact: vanilla also comes from an orchid, but its familiar ingredient story concerns cured fruit pods. Our vanilla orchid guide follows that separate path. One plant family can contribute to desserts in quite different ways—through flavor in one case and, here, through a material that helps change consistency.

Meet glucomannan: sugar units joined into long chains

Research on salep points to glucomannan as an important component. A 2017 study by Abdullah Kurt and Talip Kahyaoglu describes it as long chains containing glucose and mannose units. Scientists call a carbohydrate built from many linked sugar units a polysaccharide.

The word sugar can be misleading here. A long carbohydrate chain is not the same material as the spoonful of table sugar added for sweetness. The way small units are connected changes what the larger molecule can do. In a dessert mixture, that distinction matters to structure.

Think of a handful of separate beads beside a collection of long beaded strands. They contain similar small pieces, but you would not expect them to move in the same way. That is an analogy, not a microscopic picture of ice cream; it helps explain why molecular arrangement deserves attention alongside an ingredient’s familiar name.

Thick, elastic, and frozen are different descriptions

The National Library of Medicine’s definition of viscoelastic materials combines viscosity and elasticity, with a response to force that depends on time. It is a useful word for a material that cannot be described only as a freely flowing liquid or only as a simple spring.

Viscosity concerns resistance to flowing. An elastic response concerns recovery after deformation. Stretching a portion asks a different question from pouring a liquid or pushing a spoon into something hard. Those actions can reveal different aspects of the same food.

That is why “it is very thick” is not a complete explanation for a long stretch. Thickness, resistance to a spoon, and the ability to remain connected while being pulled are related observations, but not synonyms. In the research, measuring how a mixture responds to controlled movement helps separate those ideas.

Researchers test the mixture, not just its appearance

In a 2023 study in Food Science and Technology, Aysen Arslan and colleagues compared ice-cream formulations using different salep samples from Türkiye. They examined flow, responses to oscillating movement, and recovery after a period of stronger deformation.

The tested mixtures showed shear-thinning behavior: their apparent resistance to flow decreased as the rate of shearing increased. They also showed viscoelastic, solid-like and recoverable behavior under the study’s test conditions. Different salep samples produced different results.

These are measurements on particular formulations, not a promise about every serving. A controlled laboratory test and a dramatic pull are not interchangeable observations. Still, the experiments support an important point: the consistency is a property researchers can investigate, rather than something that has to remain a mysterious party trick.

Salep is not one perfectly uniform powder

A 2021 study by Nazli Turkmen and colleagues examined salep from ten wild orchid species and its use in Maraş-type ice cream. The researchers evaluated composition as well as the resulting frozen dessert’s properties.

Glucomannan content affected characteristics including hardness, melting behavior, and rheology—the study of flow and deformation. The team also compared salep alone with a formulation using a stabilizer-emulsifier mixture. Changing that combination affected the outcomes too.

For a curious eater, the lesson is that an ingredient name is a starting point, not a complete specification. The source material and the other ingredients matter together. It would be too simple to say that any powder sold under one name, added to any milk mixture, must produce an identical stretch.

Heat and concentration help shape the result

In another 2023 investigation, Nazli Kanca studied salep from Orchis sancta in model ice-cream mixtures. The work varied both salep concentration and heat treatment, then examined properties including water-holding capacity and apparent viscosity.

Increasing the salep concentration increased water-holding capacity and apparent viscosity in those models. Temperature also mattered. Microscopy showed a more uniform structure as treatment increased from 70°C to 80°C, but clusters appeared at 90°C under the reported conditions.

That does not establish a universal kitchen temperature or a shortcut recipe. It shows why “add the special ingredient” leaves out part of the explanation. The preparation conditions can change how an ingredient behaves. Traditional technique and controlled research approach the mixture differently, but both remind us that the process belongs in the texture story.

A stabilizer’s job is broader than making stretch

Guelph’s chapter on stabilizers describes ingredients that add viscosity to the mixture and its unfrozen portion, helping manage changes such as ice-crystal growth during storage. That is a broader job than creating an obvious stretching effect.

The chapter also notes limits: excessive viscosity can produce an undesirably heavy or chewy body. Whether a texture is wanted depends on the dessert being made. A cohesive, elastic style can be the point of one preparation without becoming the goal for every scoop. There is no reason to turn a distinctive tradition into a rule that all other ice cream should behave the same way.

Stretch does not mean melt-proof

There is another distinction to keep: holding a shape does not prove that the ice inside has stayed frozen. H. Douglas Goff’s University of Guelph explanation of ice-cream meltdown separates ice melting from collapse of the fat-stabilized foam structure.

In general ice cream, some structure can remain after ice crystals have melted. That makes a photograph an incomplete account of what is happening. Outside temperature and heat transfer also affect melting; appearance alone cannot tell you every detail of a dessert’s condition.

So enjoy the stretch without turning it into a claim that the dessert never melts. Nor should shape retention be used as a food-safety test. A fascinating texture is a reason to ask better questions, not a replacement for appropriate storage and handling.

The plant story has a conservation side

Orchid ingredients are not just a scientific curiosity. In its 2026 discussion of orchid conservation, Kew describes pressures from over-collection where wild orchids are valued for food or medicine, specifically including salep used in drinks and stretchy dondurma.

That is a reason to learn about the ingredient responsibly, not an invitation to dig up wild orchids or treat a houseplant as a pantry substitute. Understanding a traditional food can include caring about the plants behind it. Enjoying the story is an opportunity to value both the dessert tradition and the living plants it draws on.
