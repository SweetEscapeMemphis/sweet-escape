Did People Really Make Parmesan Ice Cream?

Published November 2, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Parmesan ice cream sounds like a modern dare: take something associated with pasta and turn it into a frozen dessert. But the idea is older than a social-media food challenge. A London confectionery book published in 1789 includes a recipe that explicitly puts Parmesan cheese into ice cream.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, that makes a good question for the dessert conversation: was this actually cheese-flavored ice cream, or just an old-fashioned name for something else? The answer is more interesting than a simple yes. There was real cheese in this particular recipe, but some other historical “iced cheese” names described a dessert’s form.

This is a visit to ice-cream history, not an announcement of a Parmesan flavor at the shop. The fun is seeing what an old recipe can tell us—and noticing the details that make the story stranger than the headline.

The evidence is on the page

Wellcome Collection’s catalog identifies a 1789 London edition of The Complete Confectioner, attributed to Frederick Nutt. The digitized copy comes from the University of Glasgow Library. That gives us a specific book to inspect rather than an anonymous claim about what people supposedly ate long ago.

Recipe 150, on printed pages 125–126, is headed “Parmasan Cheese Ice Cream.” The spelling looks a little different, but the instructions settle the ingredient question. The passage names eggs, syrup, cream, and grated cheese. The cheese quantity in the printed recipe is three ounces. The cheese is mixed into the preparation before it is frozen; it is not merely a decoration mentioned in the title.

You can open the original scan and continue to the following image to see the recipe across its page break. That short passage is the foundation of the fact: a published Parmesan ice-cream formula existed in 1789.

A book for the dessert table

The title page also gives the recipe a setting. The book presents itself as an assistant to “genteel families” and promises instruction in decorating a table with taste and elegance. Its scope is confectionery and presentation, not a record of an ordinary household’s daily meals. The contents extend beyond ice creams into water ices and fruits preserved in brandy.

The apprenticeship behind the book is another part of the story. Cambridge’s Fitzwilliam Museum explains that Nutt trained with Negri and Witten, successors in the London confectionery business associated with The Pot and Pineapple in Berkeley Square. This gives the author a connection to a working confectionery trade. Read alongside the title page’s emphasis on table decoration, the Parmesan recipe becomes part of a broader dessert-making repertoire: choosing a preparation, making it, and presenting it at the table. The cookbook carries some of that craft knowledge onto the printed page.

Yes, there was syrup alongside the cheese

The syrup deserves as much attention as the unexpected cheese. If you imagine a frozen spoonful of cheese sauce with no sweetener, you are already adding a detail the recipe does not support. Nutt’s formula includes syrup as well as dairy and eggs.

That does not tell us exactly how a finished batch tasted. We have not tasted a surviving eighteenth-century serving, and a brief recipe is not a sensory review. What it does show is that the words “cheese” and “sweetened frozen dessert” were not treated as mutually exclusive in this example.

It is a useful way to read surprising food history: look at the full combination before reacting to the unusual ingredient. The headline supplies the surprise; the recipe supplies the context. Here, the context is an egg-and-cream preparation containing both syrup and Parmesan.

It sat among other recognizable ice creams

The book’s contents provide another good clue. Parmesan appears in an ice-cream section alongside names including coffee, chocolate, ginger, brown bread, burnt almonds, and peach. You do not have to imagine a completely separate category of frozen meals to understand where Nutt placed it.

Read those names slowly and the old list becomes more interesting than a collection of oddities. Some ingredients still sound immediately familiar in a dessert setting. Others ask us to set aside our own expectations. The Parmesan entry belongs to that second group for many readers today.

Its presence in a cookbook is evidence of a recipe, not a survey of everyone’s favorite flavor. We should not turn one printed formula into a claim that all eighteenth-century families ate it, or that Nutt was necessarily the first person to think of it.

Why you may also see the year 1807

Mount Vernon publishes a modern Parmesan ice-cream adaptation that credits Nutt’s 1807 cookbook. Its ingredient list likewise includes eggs, simple syrup, cream, and Parmesan. That is a present-day interpretation of a historical recipe, clearly labeled as an adaptation.

The 1789 library copy and the 1807 reference are different dated sources, not a reason to discard the story. When a recipe circulates through editions and modern adaptations, checking which version an institution cites helps keep the timeline clear.

There is another distinction worth keeping: finding an adaptation on Mount Vernon’s website does not, by itself, prove that George Washington ate this particular flavor. The interesting fact stands without attaching it to a famous person’s dinner.

An extra puzzle in the old print

If you open the scan, some letters may look wrong at first. The word cheese can appear to contain an f. The National Archives’ guide to historic documents explains the long s, a letterform that resembles a lowercase f and appears in both handwritten and printed material into the early nineteenth century.

That is a reading clue, not a mystery ingredient. Use the surrounding words to decide whether you are looking at an actual f or a long s. Once you recognize the letterform, the short recipe becomes much less strange to read, even if its flavor still surprises you.

Also follow the passage across both pages. In this copy, the cheese addition appears after the page break. Looking only at the first image would leave you with an incomplete account of the formula.

Not every “iced cheese” meant a cheese ingredient

Historical dessert names can be slippery. The Library of Congress holds H. Llewellyn Williams’s The Book of Ices, published in 1891. On printed page 50, the heading Fromages Glacés is accompanied by the English name “Iced-Cream-Cheeses.”

The explanation that follows describes ice cream shaped in covered metal molds, like fancy puddings. A neighboring section discusses colored versions, and the page then turns to fruit imitated in ice cream. In that context, the “cheese” designation concerns a molded presentation rather than establishing Parmesan as an ingredient.

So two different questions need two different checks. Does a recipe actually name cheese in its ingredients? Or does a historical title describe a shape or style? Nutt’s Parmesan recipe passes the first test because the cheese appears in the instructions themselves.

Frozen desserts could be table ornaments, too

The Museum of Royal Worcester’s historic-ices activity shows the variety of Victorian dessert molds, using examples from A. B. Marshall’s 1885 The Book of Ices. The museum explains that molded ices could become colorful centerpieces for special meals, served on Worcester porcelain dessert plates.

That helps us picture a different presentation from an everyday scoop. A frozen dessert could be designed to make an entrance at the table. Its outline and colors were part of the experience, separate from the question of which ingredients flavored it.

Surviving objects make that point tangible. The Smithsonian’s collection includes a quart-sized metal ice-cream mold probably used between 1900 and 1920. A museum label, an illustrated mold, and an ingredient list each preserve a different piece of the dessert story; none should be mistaken for the others.

The serving pieces tell another part of the story

The Victoria and Albert Museum’s ice-cream history discusses small handled cups for eating frozen food and a Sèvres porcelain ice-cream pail made in 1778. The pail held ice around an inner liner containing the dessert.

Those objects remind us to ask more than “what was the flavor?” A historical ice cream also had a serving vessel and a place at the table. If the freezing equipment interests you, our guide to ice cream before electric freezers follows that separate part of the history.
