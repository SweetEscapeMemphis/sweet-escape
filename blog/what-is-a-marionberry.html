What Is a Marionberry? From Oregon Blackberry to Ice Cream

Published November 23, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

If you see marionberry on a pie or ice cream menu and picture some distant cousin of the blackberry, bring it a little closer. A marionberry is a blackberry, and its name comes from the Oregon county where it was tested. It’s a particular cultivated variety, not a different fruit hiding under a similar color. Think Granny Smith and apple: one name narrows down which kind of the other you’re talking about.

Before it had a place name, it had a test number. And decades after it was introduced, researchers were still using it as a reference point, tasting other blackberries alongside it in two forms. The surprising part: an unimpressive little blackberry in one cup could do much better in the other.

Before it was Marion, it was 928

In 1945, USDA breeder George F. Waldo crossed two blackberries called ‘Chehalem’ and ‘Olallie’ while working with Oregon’s agricultural experiment station. He selected a promising plant from that cross in 1948. Its testing identifier was ORUS 928, and the variety was introduced in 1956. The USDA plant collection record gives the reason for naming it ‘Marion’: it had been tested extensively in Marion County, Oregon.

Those dates tell three different parts of the story. Making the cross produced offspring to choose from. Selecting one meant deciding which plant was worth keeping and testing. Releasing it came later, after there was a chance to see how it grew and fruited. The name belongs to the selection, not to everything that comes from crossing those same parents or to every blackberry that grows near Salem.

Why do some people say Marion blackberry and others say marionberry? ‘Marion’ is the variety name. In their history of Pacific Northwest blackberry breeding, Chad Finn and Bernadine Strik describe a regional habit of putting berry right onto the name. Olallieberry and boysenberry share that pattern. A shopper who reads “Marion blackberry” and a baker who asks for “marionberries” can be talking about the same fruit.

A good pie berry isn’t always a good traveler

The name doesn’t tell you how the plant grows. Oregon State University Extension groups ‘Marion’ with the trailing blackberries. The new canes tend to lie along the ground without support, which is why you see them trained onto wires. That’s a different growing habit from a blackberry that sends up stiffer, relatively upright canes. The long canes also have prickles; this was no small detail to the people developing berries for machine harvest.

There’s a trade-off after picking, too. In a 1997 report on ‘Marion’, Finn and colleagues described how the tiny juicy units on the fruit, called drupelets, could break under the weight of berries piled above them. Berries with leaking skins don’t travel as neatly as firm fruit, even though pieces and juice can be useful in a pie. The researchers also observed that ripe ‘Marion’ fruit retained its color well in processing.

That helps explain why you might meet the name on a bag of frozen berries before seeing it on a fresh clamshell. Oregon State’s blackberry cultivar guide lists ‘Marion’ primarily for processing. That doesn’t mean you can’t eat it fresh or that only one kind of blackberry belongs in a pie. It means a farmer deciding what to grow for a freezer or a purée maker might value different traits from someone growing berries to ship fresh.

The same berry got judged two ways

USDA and Oregon State researchers wanted to find blackberries that performed like ‘Marion’ in processed fruit but lacked its prickles, which can break off during machine harvest. In a 2005 blind comparison, they tested nine thornless selections alongside four established varieties. The cups were coded so panelists didn’t know whose fruit they were tasting. The team could have handed out one plate of berries and called it a day. Instead, it ran two tastings.

One used whole berries that had been individually quick-frozen and then thawed for the panel. The tasters could look at and chew the berries, so appearance and how seedy they seemed were part of the score. The other used fruit that had been thawed and made into purée through a fine screen. The panelists had no whole berry to look at; they judged things like the purée’s aroma and flavor instead. This wasn’t an ice cream tasting, and nobody was asked to bite into a solid frozen blackberry.

Two small-fruited siblings, known by the test numbers ORUS 1843-1 and ORUS 1843-3, scored low for whole-fruit appearance. Yet they had the two highest average overall scores in the purée tasting. That isn’t evidence that either was reliably better than ‘Marion’: the differences between those scores and ‘Marion’ weren’t statistically clear. The useful part is that small fruit, which counted against them when laid out whole, didn’t keep them from making well-liked purée.

‘Marion’ still showed up as a yardstick when breeders announced the thornless blackberry ‘Columbia Star’ years later. The 2014 release paper includes both field measurements and two blind purée tastings. In the first tasting, the researchers found no clear difference between those two varieties on the traits they scored. In the replicated Oregon field trial, ‘Columbia Star’ berries weighed more on average. One result came from a scale, the other from cups of purée. Growers and purée makers got answers to different, equally practical questions.

Here’s how to borrow that idea in your own kitchen: decide what you want the fruit to do before judging it by looks. An intact berry might matter on top of a dessert, where you’ll see its shape. If you plan to blend it into the base, an uneven outline is beside the point. The fruit still contributes flavor, but you’ve taken shape out of the job description.

What changes between a pie and a scoop?

In Heather Arndt Anderson’s marionberry galette recipe for Oregon Public Broadcasting, fresh or frozen berries go into the pastry as fruit rather than purée. The filling gets cornstarch and bakes until it thickens. Ice cream is an optional accompaniment. In that dessert you can decide how much of the cold scoop and how much of the berry filling land on your fork together.

The Oregon Raspberry & Blackberry Commission’s marionberry cheesecake ice cream sandwich takes the other route. Thawed, blended berries are stirred into the ice cream mixture before it churns. The fruit flavor becomes part of the frozen filling rather than something you add next to the scoop. The recipe also gives frozen blackberries as a substitution. That’s a useful distinction: a substitution can do the same job in a recipe without being the same variety of fruit.

A third way to serve the two together would be to spoon berry sauce over plain ice cream. Then you get a little of each or a more berry-heavy bite depending on where you dip your spoon. Those are different desserts even if the berry on the shopping list is the same. Our fruit-flavor guide uses a similar starting point: pick the kind of dessert you want, then read what the listing actually says about the fruit.

If you’re baking with a bag from the freezer, read the preparation step as well as the ingredient list. The sandwich recipe tells you to thaw and blend; the galette recipe lets you reach for fresh or frozen berries for the filling. Neither calls for choosing the biggest berries in the bag. Save that decision for a dessert where you want the fruit to hold its shape on the plate.

Two quick marionberry questions

Is a marionberry the same thing as a blackberry?

A marionberry is one particular kind of blackberry, the cultivated variety named ‘Marion.’ A package labeled simply “blackberries” may contain another variety.

Can you use frozen marionberries in dessert?

Yes. Some recipes bake the fruit whole, while others thaw and purée it for an ice cream base. Follow the recipe for the format you want to make.

Two names to look for in Memphis

In Sweet Escape’s scoop catalog, Marion Blackberry and Marionberry Pie are two separate ice cream entries. The fruit words refer to the same kind of blackberry, but the names alone don’t tell you where fruit was sourced, how each ice cream was made or whether there are pieces of crust.

The Marion Blackberry reference lists milk and notes shared equipment with peanuts, tree nuts, wheat, soy and eggs. Marionberry Pie lists egg, milk and wheat. Its published summary doesn’t give an additional equipment note; that doesn’t rule out cross-contact. The item pages show their nutrition reference servings: ⅔ cup (95 g) for Marion Blackberry and ⅔ cup without a gram weight in the Marionberry Pie summary.
