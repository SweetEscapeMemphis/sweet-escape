What Is Ube? Filipino Purple Yam and a Memphis Gelato Guide

Published October 11, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

If you have spotted the word ube on a dessert menu and wondered what it means, the useful short answer is purple yam. It is a real root crop with a rich place in Filipino food traditions, not simply a name for any purple-colored dessert. At Sweet Escape in Memphis, the gelato catalog includes an item called Ube Purple Yam, described as inspired by Filipino ube desserts. This guide gives the word some context, shows how to read that specific listing, and explains what to check before ordering.

The distinction matters because color alone does not tell you what an ingredient is, and a flavor name does not provide a complete recipe. A little background makes a menu easier to understand while leaving the most important product questions—ingredients, allergens, and today’s availability—to the exact current item information.

Ube is a yam, with a specific botanical name

The Philippine Department of Science and Technology’s Food and Nutrition Research Institute identifies purple yam as Dioscorea alata and lists Ubi/Ube as its alternate or common name. That scientific name is a useful anchor when reading recipes or food information. It ties the word to a particular yam species rather than treating “ube” as a catch-all label for purple foods.

The same institute’s food-composition database lists purple sweet potato separately as Ipomoea batatas and taro as Colocasia esculenta. These are distinct crops, so one name should not be substituted for another when you need to know which ingredient a product actually references.

If you want to dig into the classification, the FNRI’s food list under B places purple yam and purple sweet potato in separate entries, and its purple-yam food report names Dioscorea alata. A DOST-supported Indigenous Vegetables of the Philippines profile also identifies ube/purple yam by this species name. These sources are more dependable for identifying the crop than judging a photograph or relying on color.

A familiar ingredient in many Filipino sweets

Ube is closely associated with Filipino desserts, where it appears in more than one form. A research article in the Philippine Journal of Science describes purple yam as commonly used in sweetened food delicacies and gives examples that include halaya, puto, halo-halo, hopia, and different rice cakes. The list is a window into variety, not a single recipe that defines every household or region. An ingredient can travel through many dishes while each finished dessert keeps its own identity.

One way ube appears is as ube halaya, a sweetened yam preparation. Another well-known context is halo-halo, whose name means “mix-mix” and which brings different ingredients together in one chilled dessert. Ube can also appear in baked sweets and pastries. The Philippine Department of Science and Technology’s Industrial Technology Development Institute describes processing ube into jam, powder, candy, and flakes, and notes uses of these forms in cakes, pastries, flans, and ice cream.

Those examples help explain why the word appears on contemporary dessert menus, including outside the Philippines. They also show why one scoop cannot stand in for every way ube is prepared. A gelato inspired by ube desserts is one interpretation of a broader ingredient tradition, not the same dish as halaya or halo-halo.

For readers who want a source trail beyond a quick definition, the Philippine Journal of Science article on greater yam discusses ube’s place in sweetened Filipino delicacies. A DOST-ITDI overview of ube processing describes several processed forms and dessert applications. We use these as background on the ingredient, not as evidence about the recipe or sourcing of a Sweet Escape product.

Questions Memphis guests often ask about ube

What is ube?

Ube is purple yam, identified in the Philippine Department of Science and Technology Food and Nutrition Research Institute’s database as Dioscorea alata. The same database lists it under Ubi/Ube.

Is ube the same as purple sweet potato?

No. The FNRI database identifies purple yam as Dioscorea alata and purple sweet potato as Ipomoea batatas. They are separate crops, even if both can be associated with purple-colored foods.
