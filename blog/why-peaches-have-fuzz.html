Why Do Peaches Have Fuzz but Nectarines Don’t?

Published November 15, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Set a peach and a nectarine next to each other and close your eyes. Chances are you can tell them apart before either one gets anywhere near a knife. The peach feels like it has a little coat. The nectarine doesn’t. Now for the surprise: a nectarine is a kind of peach, not a peach crossed with a plum.

That little coat has its own story. Scientists have looked at the hairs under a microscope, followed the trait through a family of trees, and even shaved peaches to see what changed. One result is particularly good dinner-table material: a shaved peach in their experiment could still bead water just as readily as a fuzzy one, yet lost more water while it sat. Looking at a drop on the outside and measuring moisture leaving the inside turned out to be two very different tests.

What is peach fuzz, exactly?

The hairs are called trichomes. They’re structures the fruit grows from its outer layer of cells. In a 2011 study of the Calrico peach, Victoria Fernández and colleagues used microscopes to see that each hair they examined was a single cell with a thick wall. The longest ones in their samples reached about a millimeter. The skin felt like it had one even coating from a distance, but up close there was a whole range of lengths.

Fuzz also isn’t the only thing between the flesh and the air. Beneath that visible layer sits the cuticle, a surface covering that includes waxes. If you picture the peel as a drawing, don’t draw a plain line with hairs floating just above it. The hairs grow from the skin, and the cuticle follows the surface underneath. The researchers still saw some short hairs after they removed the long ones for their comparison. That matters when you hear about a “shaved” peach: this wasn’t stripping the fruit down to the flesh.

You can see part of the difference without a microscope. Take a whole peach into angled light and turn it slowly. Look along the rim rather than straight at the color of the skin. That’s where the individual hairs are easiest to pick out. If you have a nectarine, turn it the same way and look for the bright reflection instead. The edge and the reflection tell you about the surface. Neither will tell you what color the flesh is.

Why would anybody shave a peach for science?

The researchers wanted to separate the effects of the hairs from those of the waxy covering. First, consider a drop of water placed on the fruit. Researchers can photograph it from the side and measure the angle where the edge of the drop meets the surface. A drop that spreads into a puddle has a lower angle than one that stays rounded. You don’t need to remember the geometry to understand the comparison: the closer two measurements are, the less the drops differ on that particular test.

In the Calrico experiment, the average water-drop angles were about 134 degrees on both the intact and the shaved surfaces. Removing the long hairs didn’t make water suddenly flatten out. That’s an interesting result, because the two surfaces looked and felt different. The team also tested other liquids and found differences, so there was no single answer to whether the shaved surface was easier to wet. It depended on what you put on it.

Then the team asked a different question: how much water left the whole fruit? After two days at 24°C and 40 percent relative humidity, the paper reported about 5 percent water loss for intact peaches, 13 percent for shaved peaches, and 20 percent for peaches treated to remove surface wax. The three groups could now be told apart. Those numbers belong to one cultivar and the researchers’ test conditions, not to a peach sitting on your counter. In particular, they did not compare shelf life between store-bought peaches and nectarines.

Think of a rain jacket. You could watch a drop roll off the outside, or you could ask how readily moisture leaves from underneath. Answering the first question doesn’t give you a number for the second. That distinction is what makes the peach study memorable. The rounded drop was a clue about one interaction at the surface. It couldn’t stand in for the measurement of moisture leaving the fruit over two days.

Why doesn’t the nectarine grow that coat?

Peaches and nectarines belong to the same species, Prunus persica, as the University of Florida’s peach and nectarine variety guide explains. Brushing the fuzz off a fuzzy peach won’t make it a nectarine. The interesting difference is how the fruit developed before it was picked.

In a 2014 study, Elisa Vendramin and colleagues followed descendants of a fuzzy ‘Contender’ peach and a smooth ‘Ambra’ nectarine. They traced which offspring grew fuzzy fruit and which grew smooth fruit. Researchers could then look for changes in DNA that traveled along with the difference in skin. They found a stretch of inserted DNA disrupting a gene involved in whether hairs develop. They also tested a marker for that change in a wider group of peach and nectarine varieties.

The smooth-skin trait in that study was recessive. In the inheritance pattern the team followed, a tree with one working copy of the relevant gene could still produce fuzzy fruit. Fuzz, then, couldn’t tell you on its own whether the tree carried one or two working copies. Think of a school photo: it tells you who showed up that day, not who was on the full class roll. To infer the copies inherited by the trees, the researchers needed the cross, the fruit observations, and the DNA evidence together.

Later work by Yang and colleagues investigated how that gene works with a second one. It found connections to wax production as well as to hairs. This is another reason the 2011 shaved-peach experiment isn’t a substitute for comparing the two fruit types. The team changed the outside of a peach that had already grown; a nectarine develops its own surface from the beginning.

The peel is one choice; the flesh is another

Ever cut a wonderfully soft peach and had to pry the flesh away from the pit? The variety guide keeps track of that trait separately from skin. In a freestone variety the flesh comes away more readily; in a clingstone it holds on. Flesh color and how the flesh softens are separate characteristics, too. The guide’s variety descriptions distinguish white versus yellow flesh and list peaches with different softening behavior. If you’re choosing fruit to slice into tidy wedges, ease of removing the pit can be more useful than the bit you can feel through the produce bag.

Here’s a simple way to see those traits without mixing them up. Put a peach and a nectarine on the same board. Look at the uncut skin first, then slice each and look at the flesh. Finally, see how much fruit stays on the pit. You’ll have three observations about two pieces of fruit. If they separate from their pits differently, that tells you something about those particular varieties, not that fuzz makes the pit stick.

The same distinctions help when you read a frozen dessert recipe. One published peach ice cream recipe from Williams Sonoma calls for peeled, diced peaches. Its fresh peach ice cream recipe uses two forms of fruit: a purée in the base and chopped peaches mixed in separately. The first recipe tells you what happens to the skin. The second shows why a cook might split up the fruit instead of using it all in one form. Each tells you something you couldn’t know just from seeing the words “peach ice cream” at the top of the page.

If you’re comparing recipes, check where the fruit goes before you decide which one you want to make. Does a spoonful get a bit of fruit from the base, an individual piece, or both? That’s an easier question to answer from a recipe than “Which one tastes more like peach?” It’s also more useful than guessing from the photograph. A beautifully cut wedge beside a bowl can tell you what somebody put on the table, but it doesn’t tell you what went through the ice cream maker.
