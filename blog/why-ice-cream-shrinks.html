A little ice cream science

Why Does Ice Cream Shrink in the Freezer?

Published November 19, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Open a carton that once held ice cream snug against its sides and you might see a little moat. No spoon dug it, no puddle sits at the bottom, and the ice cream still looks frozen. What happened to the part that used to touch the wall? Sometimes what went missing is space: the tiny pockets of air that helped the ice cream fill the carton no longer take up as much room.

Food scientists call that loss of volume shrinkage. Ice cream doesn’t have to turn into soup for it to happen. The surprising part is how much you can learn by looking at the gap itself. Its location matters, the height of the ice cream doesn’t tell the whole story, and the way the dessert behaves on your spoon won’t necessarily explain what happened in the freezer.

A pint has air on the inside, too

It’s easy to see the space between the lid and the ice cream. The space that matters here is harder to spot: air is scattered through the dessert in tiny bubbles. In microscope studies of ice cream during hardening and storage, researchers could follow changes in individual pockets. It’s not a solid block like an ice cube. It’s a foam held in place by ice, fat and the concentrated liquid between them.

Picture a simple measuring-cup example. If one cup of mix becomes two cups of frozen dessert, its volume has increased by 100 percent. Ice cream makers call that increase overrun. It does not mean the result is 100 percent air: the extra cup is only half of the two-cup total. This is round-number math, not a formula for a Sweet Escape flavor or a way to determine exactly how much air a particular carton holds.

Now imagine those two cups settling to a cup and three-quarters as the foam loses space. The starting point for calculating shrinkage is the two cups of finished dessert, not the single cup of mix. A quarter cup lost from two cups is a drop of 12.5 percent in volume. If you weighed the sample, you’d be asking a different question. Losing a quarter cup of space isn’t the same as losing a quarter cup of mix. Our guide to planning ice cream by volume or weight explains why that distinction is useful when portions, rather than bubbles, are on your mind.

A bigger bubble doesn’t necessarily mean more air

In their microscope study, Y. H. Chang and Richard Hartel watched two neighboring bubbles join in one spot. Elsewhere, air could move from smaller pockets into larger ones through the material between them. One change removes a wall between neighbors. The other can make a pocket grow without ever touching its neighbor. Both can leave you looking at fewer, larger bubbles than you started with.

Neither observation alone tells you how much less room the dessert takes up. If two bubbles join, a larger bubble can initially hold the air that was in both. The change becomes a carton-size problem when the foam no longer holds its original volume. In Samantha VanWees’s University of Wisconsin research, images of some stored samples showed air pockets joining into channels. Think about the difference between a wall full of little sealed cavities and one with cavities connected to each other. A picture of that change helps explain why the outside shape could give way, but the picture itself doesn’t measure the missing volume.

Ice crystals can change during storage, too. That’s the subject of our guide to why ice cream gets icy. The two changes can happen in the same dessert, but they aren’t interchangeable: one question is how large the ice crystals got, and the other is how much space the frozen foam still occupies.

What would a mountain pass have to do with it?

Here’s the oddest chapter in the carton story: temperature isn’t the only thing that can change along a delivery route. Air pressure is lower at higher elevations. For a fixed amount of gas at the same temperature, Boyle’s law describes why a gas pocket that’s free to expand tends to take up more space as pressure drops. Picture a sealed snack bag puffing up on a drive into the mountains. Now picture tiny pockets inside a dessert, each pressing against the material around it.

In their history of frozen-dessert research, Hartel, Scott Rankin and Robert Bradley describe how pressure changes in transport can contribute to shrinkage. Think of the ascent and descent as a stretch and squeeze. If the structure around those air cells yields during the trip, bringing the pressure back to where it started doesn’t guarantee the dessert will return to its original shape. The gas-law example explains the push on the bubble, not whether its walls will hold.

A mountain trip isn’t necessary. Researchers have also studied shrinkage during cycling temperatures in storage. These are different stresses on the same general problem: keeping a network of small pockets intact over time. A gap can tell you the ice cream no longer meets the carton wall. It can’t tell you which stress, if any, created it, or whether it was filled to that spot in the first place.

How do you measure a gap you can barely see?

Suppose ice cream pulls away from the sides but leaves its top nearly where it was. A ruler dropped down from the lid could miss most of the change. VanWees solved that problem in the lab by taking the frozen sample out of its container and briefly lowering it into chilled water. The team could read how far the water level rose and convert that rise into a volume. The reading counted all the space the sample occupied, not just the distance from top to bottom.

That’s the same idea behind water rising when you lower a mug into a full dishpan. Here the researchers calibrated the apparatus with a known amount of water, accounted for the tongs, and discarded the dessert after each measurement. Comparing stored samples with starting measurements let them tell whether the whole shape had gotten smaller, even when shrinkage hadn’t occurred evenly.

In the resulting study of high-protein frozen desserts, changing the kind of dairy protein and the ingredients at the bubble surface changed how some samples held up over six weeks. Some test formulas lost more volume than others. There wasn’t a single outcome just because the samples had a lot of protein and air. Knowing what’s in the space between the bubbles helped make sense of what happened to the bubbles themselves.

One comparison makes that difference concrete. A version made with sodium caseinate, a form of milk protein, showed measurable shrinkage under the study’s cycling conditions. Versions made with whey protein isolate and the same added emulsifier did not show significant shrinkage in that experiment. The scientists photographed channels in a caseinate sample, giving them a picture to compare with the volume measurements. That’s more useful than sorting packages by the word “protein” and expecting them all to behave alike.

The researchers pulled samples at two, four and six weeks. That let them compare the changes already visible at the first check with changes that became clearer later. A photo taken only at the end would show the result, but couldn’t tell them whether the same signs were there at week two. It’s the difference between seeing a finished puzzle and having a few snapshots of how the pieces came together.

The team also measured a very different thing: what happened when dessert sat on a wire screen at room temperature and liquid dripped through. One test asks how much of the frozen shape has already disappeared in storage. The other asks how the dessert behaves as it warms. A formula that delays the first drip hasn’t automatically passed the freezer test. The experiment kept those questions separate, and so should anyone curious about why their own scoop is sitting the way it is.

Two questions about the gap in a carton

Does a gap around ice cream prove that it melted?

No. Frozen foam can lose volume without the whole dessert liquefying. A gap by itself does not show the original fill level, the temperature history, or whether the ice cream is safe to eat.

Is shrinkage the same thing as freezer burn?

No. Shrinkage is a loss of volume. Freezer burn is surface drying. Both can change the appearance of a frozen food, but they describe different changes.

For more about surface drying, see the USDA’s freezer-burn explanation.
