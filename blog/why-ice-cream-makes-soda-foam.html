The bubble science behind a fizzy dessert

Why Does Ice Cream Make Soda Foam? A Sweet Escape Fun Fact

Published October 26, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A scoop meets a fizzy drink, and suddenly the glass seems too small. Anyone curious about an ice-cream soda has a good question to ask: why does adding ice cream make all that foam?

Here is a frozen-dessert fun fact for your next Sweet Escape conversation in Memphis: the soda already contains dissolved carbon dioxide. The ice cream provides places where that gas can gather into bubbles. The dramatic rise is not evidence that a scoop has magically manufactured a glassful of new gas.

To understand the whole show, separate three events: gas leaving a drink, bubbles forming, and bubbles collecting into foam. They are connected, but each answers a different part of the question.

This is a general science story about ice cream and carbonated drinks. It is not an announcement of a float or soda service at Sweet Escape.

The fizz is there before the scoop arrives

Carbon dioxide can be dissolved in a liquid even when you do not see a layer of bubbles. An unopened carbonated drink is a familiar example.

OpenStax’s chemistry explanation of gas solubility describes how carbonated beverages are exposed to relatively high carbon-dioxide pressure before their containers are sealed. That pressure supports a higher dissolved-gas concentration.

Opening the container releases pressure. The drink then contains more dissolved carbon dioxide than it would retain at equilibrium under the new conditions. Over time, gas leaves, and the beverage becomes flat.

So the starting point is not simply “soda has bubbles.” The liquid holds a supply of dissolved gas that can move into a gas phase. Seeing bubbles tells you that some of this transition is occurring.

That distinction makes the later foam less mysterious. A quiet-looking liquid can already contain the material that will fill many bubbles once conditions favor their formation and growth.

A smooth-looking scoop has a much smaller landscape

The American Chemical Society’s explanation of ice-cream sodas points to tiny ice crystals in ice cream. Carbon-dioxide molecules from the drink gather at those sites and form bubbles.

Your eyes see a scoop. At a much smaller scale, the surface offers features that are not obvious from across the table. “Looks smooth” is not the same as “has no microscopic features.”

The society also notes that wet ice can have smoother points, with fewer bubbles forming at them. That is a useful reminder that the condition of a surface can matter, not just the name of the material.

This gives the scoop a specific role: it helps provide starting locations for bubbles. It does not need to create the original carbon dioxide in order to help release it visibly.

Think of the drink and scoop as contributing different parts of the scene. The soda supplies dissolved gas, while the ice cream changes the opportunities for bubbles to begin.

Nucleation is the name for getting a bubble started

Scientists call bubble initiation nucleation. A nucleation site is a place where a bubble can start growing more readily.

In its lesson on gases dissolving in water, the American Chemical Society uses carbonated water and different surfaces to explain this idea. Tiny bumps and pits on a surface can provide gathering places for carbon-dioxide molecules.

The lesson connects this principle with the familiar candy-and-soda demonstration. That is not the same food combination as an ice-cream soda, but it illustrates why microscopic surface features belong in the explanation of a very visible result.

You do not have to remember the scientific word to use the idea. Ask where the bubbles first appear. Do they seem to begin at a particular surface? Does the bubbling change as the surface changes?

Those are better observations than treating every fizzing mixture as the same event. They direct attention to the interface between the food and the liquid, where a small feature can make a large-looking difference.

Researchers can study those tiny starting points

The details of nucleation are interesting enough for controlled laboratory research. A 2021 study by Thomas and Jacob Kuntzleman investigated bubble formation in the Diet Coke and Mentos experiment.

The researchers combined theory and experiments involving ethanol-containing solutions to explore degassing and estimate the dimensions and number of active nucleation sites on the candies.

The important connection is the method of asking the question. A fountain that looks like one big burst can be investigated through the behavior of many very small bubble-starting locations.

The study did not test Sweet Escape products or establish an exact foam height for ice cream. Nor does mentioning the experiment turn this article into instructions for making a soda fountain. Its value here is showing that bubble initiation is something researchers can measure and model.

A dramatic appearance is the beginning of a science question, not the end of one. To explain it well, you need to know the material, the conditions, and what the experiment actually tested.

Cold matters, but it does not cancel the surface effect

There is an apparent puzzle: if ice cream is cold, why would it encourage bubbling? Does cold not help a drink hold its carbonation?

The answer is that temperature and bubble-starting surfaces are different factors. The gas-dissolving lesson above explains that more gas can remain dissolved in cold water than hot water. That does not mean a cold surface cannot provide a location for a bubble.

The University of Wisconsin–Madison’s root-beer demonstration illustrates the temperature side separately: carbon dioxide is less soluble in warm root beer than cold root beer.

There is no need to warm a drink or recreate that demonstration to understand the distinction. One question is how much gas a liquid can retain under particular conditions. Another is where bubbles can begin when gas is coming out.

In a real dessert, several conditions change together. A single explanation such as “it is cold” cannot stand in for the drink’s carbonation, the surface it contacts, and the bubbles’ subsequent behavior.

Foam is more than a stream of bubbles

A bubble rising through a drink and a layer of foam at the top are related sights, but they are not identical structures.

NASA’s Foam Optics and Mechanics investigation describes aqueous foams as gas bubbles dispersed through a continuous liquid phase containing surface-active molecules that can slow or prevent bubbles from merging directly.

That description highlights two ingredients in the physical structure: gas and liquid. A tall foam is not simply extra liquid that appeared from nowhere. Gas-filled spaces contribute to the volume you see.

NASA studies how foams and bubbly liquids change over time using optical measurements, including observations of bubble sizes, shapes, and rearrangements. Microgravity helps researchers separate behaviors that gravity complicates on Earth.

These are general foam experiments, not tests of a particular dessert recipe. They help explain why “how many bubbles form?” and “how long does the foam remain?” are separate questions. Producing bubbles does not, by itself, tell you how stable the resulting foam will be.

Why the foamy head changes while you watch

The European Space Agency’s foam explanation identifies several ways a foam evolves. Gravity draws liquid downward between bubbles. Smaller bubbles can shrink while larger ones grow. Films can rupture, changing the structure again.

Drainage, coarsening, and rupture are distinct processes, even though you may notice them together as a foamy head settles.

That means the first few seconds do not describe the entire drink. If you are watching a fizzy dessert, notice the sequence: initial bubbling, a layer of foam, and the later changes in that layer.

The practical lesson is modest: leave room for a foamy head rather than judging capacity from the liquid alone. Do not expect the first height of the foam to be its permanent height, and do not use a closed container for an observation involving a foaming carbonated mixture.

There is no universal foam-height formula here. Different drinks, frozen desserts, quantities, and conditions would need their own measurements.
