Two fruit names, two different plants

Guanábana vs. Guava: Two Different Fruits on Sweet Escape’s Memphis Ice-Cream Menu

Published October 14, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

If you have seen both Guanaban-Ahhh and Guava Have It in Sweet Escape’s scoop catalog, it is fair to pause over the names. Guanábana and guava sound related, and both appear in tropical-fruit conversation. But they are not two spellings for one fruit: guanábana is a common name for soursop, while guava is a different species with a different botanical name. Sweet Escape lists a separate ice-cream item for each.

This guide answers the naming question, then shows what the two product pages document. A short description can identify a flavor concept, but it is not a full recipe, nutrition panel, current-stock report, or allergy-safety guarantee.

The short answer: guanábana is soursop, not guava

The University of Florida’s Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences (UF/IFAS) lists soursop, graviola, and guanabana as common names for Annona muricata. Its separate guava resource identifies guava as Psidium guajava. Those scientific names put the fruits in different genera: Annona and Psidium. So if a menu uses “guanábana” and another item uses “guava,” the words point to distinct fruit identities rather than alternate labels for the same thing.

That simple distinction is the useful answer for a guest scanning Sweet Escape’s catalog. “Guanaban-Ahhh” refers to the guanabana-named product record; “Guava Have It” refers to the guava-named record. Each has its own product page, image, manufacturer nutrition-label link, and caution information. The shared fact that both are fruit names—or that both are listed as ice cream—does not make their records interchangeable.

What guanábana means

Guanábana, often written without the accent as guanabana, is one of the common names for soursop, Annona muricata. UF/IFAS places it among several related fruits in the Annona genus. Sweetsop, also called sugar apple, is a different species, Annona squamosa; cherimoya is another member of the genus, Annona cherimola. Their names can overlap in informal food writing, which is one reason a scientific name is handy when a precise identification matters.

UF/IFAS describes soursop’s general fruit characteristics, but those are not tasting notes for an ice cream. Sweet Escape’s published Guanaban-Ahhh copy is shorter: “Refreshingly tropical guanabana ice cream.” It does not give a fruit percentage, cultivar, complete recipe, or comparison with fresh soursop.

The Food and Agriculture Organization’s food-processing chapter on guanabana discusses puree and frozen-dessert contexts, but that does not establish how Sweet Escape’s product is formulated or made. For the shop’s item, use its first-party record.

What guava means

Guava is the common English name for Psidium guajava; guayaba is a Spanish common name used for guava. UF/IFAS’s guava overview describes the fruit as variable: its shape can range from round to oval, peel color can range from green to yellow, and flesh can be white, yellow, pink, or red depending on variety. The source notes that cultivars differ. “Guava” identifies a fruit species but does not specify one single appearance or a universal flavor profile.

This variability is one reason not to infer an ice cream’s exact ingredients or taste from the fruit name. Sweet Escape describes Guava Have It as “Mouth watering guava ice cream”; it does not name a variety, sourcing region, fruit percentage, or mix-in. Treat that sentence as the boundary of the published description.

When reading an ingredient statement or catalog card, preserve the exact product wording rather than carrying details from one fruit to the other.
