The color clue in cocoa powder

Why Is Dutch-Process Cocoa Darker?

Published November 16, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Put two unsweetened cocoa powders next to each other. One might look light brown, the other nearly black. It’s tempting to guess that the darker one contains more chocolate, or that the lighter one has sugar mixed in. But the difference can start with what happened to the cocoa before it went into the tin.

Dutch-process cocoa gets treated with an alkali, which raises its pH and can deepen its color without adding sugar. That sounds simple until you try to read a scoop the way you read the powder. A darker frozen dessert might use a darker powder, but the finished color can’t tell you which powder went in, how much was used, or whether cocoa powder was the only source of chocolate.

“Dutch-process” describes what was done to the cocoa

Natural and Dutch-process are names for two broad kinds of cocoa powder. The American Society of Baking’s cocoa powder overview describes a familiar route to powder: remove some of the fat from ground cocoa, then grind the solid material that’s left. Making powder and treating it with alkali aren’t the same job. Pressing takes out a portion of the cocoa butter. Alkalization changes the treated cocoa, including its acidity and color.

The Van Houten brand’s history describes both ideas as part of the Dutch family business’s story. It discusses the cocoa press separately from the treatment with alkaline salts. That’s more useful to a shopper than remembering a particular date: one innovation helped make a powder possible on a different scale, and the other helped change what that cocoa was like. The word Dutch on a package is about processing, not a map of where the cacao tree grew.

The pH scale tells you another part of the story. Lower values are more acidic, and a reading near seven is roughly neutral under ordinary conditions. Raising the pH of an acidic cocoa moves it away from that starting point. It doesn’t mean every treated cocoa ends up at the same number, any more than the word “toasted” tells you exactly how dark a slice of bread will be.

How researchers put a number on the shade

In one controlled study of cocoa and alkaline processing, the researchers compared untreated powder, a sample heated without alkali, and samples treated with alkali. On their color scale, a lower L value meant a darker sample. The untreated powder measured about 33. The sample heated without alkali measured about 31. The treated samples were around 29. Because the team included the heat-only comparison, the results show more than a before-and-after picture. They give you a way to see the change that went beyond heating alone in that experiment.

The untreated and heat-only samples both measured pH 5.57. The treated samples ranged from about 7.5 to 8.0. Those numbers describe the powders under the researchers’ measurement conditions. They aren’t values you can assign to a tin from the grocery store by opening it and deciding it looks like the study photo.

A color measurement can also separate changes we casually lump together as “darker.” The study tracked a lightness value alongside readings related to red versus green and yellow versus blue. A drop in lightness can make a powder look deeper in color without telling you whether the undertone reads more reddish or more brown. If you’ve ever stood two similar paint swatches side by side and finally noticed that one leaned red, you’ve already made a version of that distinction.

Black and reddish cocoa aren’t opposite answers

A study of commercial cocoa powders included both brown-to-black and red examples among the treated powders. So when you hear that Dutching can darken cocoa, don’t picture every sample moving toward the same charcoal gray. King Arthur Baking’s side-by-side baking guide gives some of the names you might see on a package, including black cocoa and cocoa rouge. Both can be Dutch-process. They don’t have to look interchangeable.

To see the differences at home, look at the plain powders on the same white plate under the same light. You can compare color first without changing the mix or tasting a spoonful of dry powder. Also look at the label before you draw a conclusion. A cocoa powder, a sweetened hot-cocoa mix, and a chocolate drink mix don’t tell the same story just because they are all brown and come in a tin.

Can natural cocoa be dark, too?

Yes. An interesting counterexample comes from a published process disclosure about dark natural cocoa. Its author reports that treating cocoa cake with water under industrial conditions darkened it without added alkali. The resulting powders had pH readings between 5.4 and 5.7. Two of the trial samples measured between the document’s examples of red and dark brown alkalized cocoa on a lightness scale. So even before you put the powder into ice cream, a dark shade isn’t enough to prove that Dutching was involved.

Those readings were made on samples mixed into water, not on dry powder. It wouldn’t be useful to take the exact lightness numbers from that document, set them next to the first study’s numbers, and make a league table. The comparison that matters is the one within the water-treated trials: some of the samples overlapped the reference shades while staying acidic. It’s a good reason to treat a measured color as an observation, not as a complete history of how the cocoa was made.

Read the label before you try a swap

On a U.S. cocoa package, one phrase worth looking for is processed with alkali. The federal breakfast cocoa standard calls for that disclosure or one naming the specific alkali when the standard’s optional alkali is used; the related cocoa standard refers back to those labeling requirements. In the pantry, the practical clue is that these words describe the powder’s treatment. They don’t give you a sugar percentage, a flavor ranking, or a test of how dark the dessert will turn.

In baking, the choice can affect more than color. Baking soda can react with an acidic ingredient to release carbon dioxide. If a recipe counts on natural cocoa for some of that acidity, a swap to Dutched cocoa can change how the batter rises. It depends on the whole recipe, including its other acidic ingredients and leaveners. If you want a cake to behave as written, the cocoa listed in the recipe is a better starting point than whichever tin looks darkest.

Why one ice cream recipe uses two kinds of chocolate

A churned ice cream isn’t relying on the same acid-and-baking-soda reaction to raise a cake. But there’s still a good reason to read past the words “chocolate ice cream.” In King Arthur’s Chocolate Decadence Ice Cream, the Dutch-process cocoa goes into the base and the chopped chocolate is stirred in later to melt. They start as two different ingredients and both end up in the base. The powder isn’t a topping, and the chopped chocolate isn’t meant to stay in chunks.

That’s the detail that makes the recipe a useful example. If you saw two bowls made to look equally dark, you wouldn’t know from looking whether one had cocoa powder alone and the other included melted chocolate too. Replacing the powder would change one ingredient in that recipe; replacing the chopped chocolate would change a different one. Neither swap can be predicted by matching the color on the spoon.

If you’re testing powders in your own ice cream recipe, start with the version as written. On a second batch, change just the cocoa, not the chocolate, the sugar and the serving temperature all at once. Look at the two bases side by side before freezing and compare similar portions at the same serving temperature afterward. Ask two separate questions: did the appearance change, and did you prefer the result? A darker color is easy to spot, but it doesn’t answer the second question for you.

Give both samples the same background, too. A scoop in a dark bowl and a scoop on a white plate aren’t a fair color comparison. Put small amounts on matching dishes and swap their positions if one’s catching more light. That’s not a laboratory measurement, but it can help you tell a difference in the dessert from a difference in how it was served.
