If you are choosing between Peanut Butter Bullseye and Peanut Butter Cup at Sweet Escape, the names suggest a close family resemblance. The useful question is what the published records actually confirm. Both are listed as ice cream in Sweet Escape’s scoop catalog, and each has its own nutrition and allergen record. How to read the nutrition comparison fairly

The listed serving basis is the same for both records: 2/3 cup, or 95 grams. That makes the numbers directly comparable as published. Peanut Butter Cup’s record lists 20 more calories, 2 grams more total fat, 1 gram less saturated fat, and 90 milligrams more sodium than Peanut Butter Bullseye’s record. It lists 1 gram less total carbohydrate, 2 grams less total sugar, and 1 gram more protein.

These are descriptions of the figures shown on the records, not advice about what anyone should choose. A difference in a listed value does not make one flavor “healthier,” and a nutrition panel does not measure what an individual person needs. If you compare the labels, compare like with like: the stated serving basis matters, and the record values are not a measurement of the particular scoop served to you.

The table leaves out other values that appear on the product pages, such as cholesterol, trans fat, fiber, and added sugars. You can review all published fields and open the linked nutrition label from each individual card. The nutrition and allergen guide explains how Sweet Escape separates those fields. Rather than using one number as a shortcut, read the complete record that corresponds to the exact flavor you are considering.

Both source pages are part of a document whose product data is dated 2019. That date is important context: an online record is helpful for comparing what Sweet Escape has published, but it should not replace checking current packaging or asking the shop when an ingredient, formulation, or nutrition detail is essential. The product pages themselves also caution that recipes and labels can change.

Allergen details: similar records, same need to verify

Both product pages list milk, peanuts, and soy under “Contains.” Both also carry an equipment note naming tree nuts, wheat, and eggs. These are important distinctions to preserve exactly. The equipment note is separate from the declared ingredients in the “Contains” field; do not collapse the two into a claim that an item is free from any other allergen or safe for a particular person.

A product name is never an allergy check. Nor does a comparison between two peanut-butter flavors establish a safe choice for someone with a peanut, milk, soy, tree-nut, wheat, or egg concern. The records are dated, recipes and labels may change, and online information cannot guarantee the absence of cross-contact during handling.

If an allergy or dietary requirement affects the order, open the exact product page and then ask the shop to verify the current container label and relevant handling information before deciding. The nut-named flavor and allergen guide offers more detail on reading those cautions. A blank or brief product description is not evidence that an ingredient or cross-contact risk is absent.

Which one should you choose?

Start with what matters to you and use the records for the decision they can support. If you are comparing published nutrition information, both pages make it possible to compare values on the same stated portion basis. If an allergen is relevant, the records show matching declared and equipment cautions, but you should still verify the current label. If what you want to know is a specific flavor or texture difference, the available catalog descriptions do not answer that question in enough detail for us to declare a winner.

That does not mean you have to choose blindly. You can ask whether the current labels or product information explain how the flavors differ, and then choose based on the answer and the item that is actually available.
