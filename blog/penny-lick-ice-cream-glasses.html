A little glass with a surprisingly big dessert story

What Was a Penny Lick? Ice Cream’s Tiny Glass

Published November 11, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Imagine finishing your ice cream, then handing its little glass back to a street seller for the next customer. That was the idea behind a “penny lick”: a small reusable glass associated with ice-cream selling in nineteenth-century Britain. The dessert was yours; the container stayed in circulation.

It is a surprising bit of history to bring to your next dessert conversation in Memphis. Stranger still, some surviving glasses are mostly solid. What looks like room for a generous serving may actually be a shallow hollow at the top. Looking closely at these ordinary objects reveals a story about design, street work, and how people enjoyed a cold treat.

Why was it called a penny lick?

The Atkinson museum in Southport explains that the name referred to a penny’s worth of ice cream sold in one of these glasses. The customer ate the ice cream and returned the glass. It was a serving arrangement, not the name of a special flavor.

That helps prevent a funny misunderstanding: “lick” was not a meter counting individual movements of someone’s tongue. The name described the little ice-cream experience associated with the vessel and its price.

A penny in that setting also belongs to a particular historical economy. It is not a modern Sweet Escape price, a promise of a standard scoop size, or a useful shortcut for comparing today’s desserts. The historical name tells us something about the transaction; the glass and other records tell us more about how it worked.

The outside looked bigger than the inside

The pair described by Scarborough Museums and Galleries can initially resemble small liqueur glasses. Its description directs attention to the nearly solid bowl and the shallow indentation left for the ice cream. It also notes that the glass’s refractive qualities could make the serving appear fuller than it was.

That is the visual joke hiding in plain sight. We are accustomed to reading the outer shape of a drinking vessel as a rough guide to its inside. Here, that shortcut fails: glass occupies much of the space we might casually assume is available for dessert.

Look at the rim, then trace the inside surface rather than the outer silhouette. The important question is not simply, “How tall is this glass?” It is, “Where could the ice cream actually sit?” That change of viewpoint makes an unfamiliar museum object easier to understand.

Three surviving glasses do not make one standard portion

The Corning Museum of Glass catalogs an English example dated 1880–1905. Its description identifies a pressed-glass vessel with a bucket-shaped bowl that is solid except for a small depression at the top. The overall height is 8.2 centimeters.

A second Corning example, assigned the same date range, has a conical bowl and three horizontal ribs near its base. It is also 8.2 centimeters tall, but the two objects have different foot diameters.

London Museum’s glass holder, dated around 1900, has a solid conical bowl with a recessed top, a stem, and a foot. Its recorded overall height is 96 millimeters. These descriptions give us recognizable family features without requiring every penny lick to be an identical object.

The table is useful precisely because it does not tell us how much ice cream each glass held. Outside dimensions are not a capacity measurement. A mostly solid vessel makes that distinction especially important.

A street photograph puts people back into the story

The glass alone can make this sound like a tale of curious tableware. John Thomson’s Halfpenny Ices, in London Museum’s collection, supplies a different kind of evidence. The museum identifies customers gathered around an ice-cream seller’s barrow in a photograph published in Street Life in London.

The title itself is worth noticing. “Halfpenny” reminds us not to turn the phrase “penny lick” into a claim that every historical ice transaction had exactly the same price. This particular photographic record has its own title and context.

More importantly, there are customers and a working seller behind the tiny-glass novelty. The object belonged to an exchange between people, not just a display cabinet. Linking the vessel to the street scene helps restore that human scale.

A photograph can show a gathering around a barrow. It cannot, by itself, establish the ingredients of every serving, the quantity in every glass, or everything that happened before and after the exposure.

The picture was part of a deliberate documentary project

London Museum’s account of street life and work in 1877 places Thomson’s photographs alongside articles by journalist Adolphe Smith. The project documented workers, traders, and shopkeepers. The museum also explains that the pictures were posed.

“Posed” does not make a historical photograph worthless. It does mean we should avoid treating it as an unnoticed instant snatched from an uninterrupted sales queue. The photographer made choices about the scene, just as the writer made choices about the story.

For an enjoyable close look, begin with what the record identifies: the seller’s barrow, the gathered customers, and the photograph’s place in a larger account of working life. Then keep your guesses separate from those details. Imagining everyone’s favorite flavor may be fun, but it is imagination rather than something the image proves.

A cold treat depended on an early working day

The 1877 Halfpenny Ices essay, available in transcription, describes a trade organized around Saffron Hill. Supplies, milk sellers, barrows, and an ice depot formed part of the working network. The account places the morning activity around four o’clock, well before the customers’ afternoon treat.

It describes sellers preparing their ices, leaving for their selling locations, and returning toward evening. Read as an account of work, it reveals how much happened outside the moment when a customer received a small serving.

The same text contains prejudiced generalizations about Italian migrants. Those judgments should not be repeated as facts about the people who did this work. Its concrete descriptions of supplies and routines are useful historical evidence; the author’s stereotypes are not an explanation of anyone’s character.

That distinction lets us appreciate the effort behind the dessert without accepting every opinion in an old account. The tiny glass was only the customer-facing end of a much larger working day.

The glass’s life continued after the ice cream was gone

London Museum’s ice-cream history describes customers returning penny-lick glasses and sellers giving them only a light rinse between uses. Its account identifies the practice as a public-health concern.

The historical issue was the way shared serving glasses were handled—not a rule that every reusable dessert dish is inherently unsuitable. There is a difference between explaining an old transaction and recommending that anyone reproduce it.

The museum also notes that penny licks remained in use into the twentieth century even as edible cones became more common. That is a useful correction to a tidy before-and-after story. Different serving arrangements could overlap; a new idea did not require every older object to disappear at once.

For the separate question of edible holders and competing invention claims, see our ice-cream cone history. The penny lick deserves its own story without becoming another claim that one person invented the cone on one particular afternoon.

What makes the penny lick memorable?

It changes the way we look at an ordinary dessert container. A glass can resemble drinkware while having very little hollow space. A serving can be purchased without its vessel becoming the customer’s property. A small object can connect a museum collection with a street photograph and a description of people’s working lives.

The most useful fun fact is therefore a little richer than “people once ate ice cream from glass.” They used a particular kind of small holder in a particular selling arrangement, and surviving records let us examine that arrangement from several angles.

Next time you see an unfamiliar food object in a museum, try the same questions: where did the food go, how was it held, and what happened to the object afterward? You might find a whole story hiding inside an unexpectedly shallow bowl.
