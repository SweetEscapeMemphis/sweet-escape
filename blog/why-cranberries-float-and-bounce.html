Why Do Cranberries Float and Bounce?

Published November 27, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Put a fresh cranberry in a glass of water and it usually pops back to the surface. Drop one on a hard plate and it may hop away like a tiny ball. The two tricks look related, but they tell you different things. A cranberry floats because its air chambers make it light for its size. To bounce, it also needs flesh firm enough to spring back after impact.

Growers have made use of both tricks: one to gather the fruit out of a flooded field, the other to sort it. In a cranberry sorbet, neither trick survives the saucepan. That’s exactly what you want.

Cut across the middle to see why they float

A cranberry looks solid until you cut it through its widest point. Inside an American cranberry are four seed chambers, called locules. Look for the open spaces around the seeds, with pale walls between them. You can see the layout especially clearly in the first figure of a 2019 study led by Luis Diaz-Garcia. The researchers cut cranberries, inked the flat faces and stamped them on paper. The flesh left a print; the chambers showed up as gaps. They scanned more than 3,000 of those little prints from 16 varieties.

The gaps are the important part in a glass of water. A berry with pockets of air weighs less than one with that same space filled with flesh. The physics is the same as for a boat: what matters is the average density of the whole thing, air and all, compared with the water around it. It doesn’t matter whether every individual part of the berry is lighter than water.

Try the glass experiment with a few whole berries before you slice one. Push a floating berry under with the back of a spoon and lift the spoon away. The berry doesn’t have to take in new air from the surface to come back up; it was already carrying those pockets. A berry that floats higher than its neighbor isn’t necessarily the heavier or bigger one. You’re seeing how much of each fruit has to sit in the water to support its own weight.

Why growers flood a field that isn’t normally underwater

The photograph that makes cranberries look like an aquatic crop usually shows the harvest, not the growing season. The Pacific Northwest cranberry production guide describes shallow flooding at harvest and machines with reels that shake the fruit loose. The detached berries rise, and then the same water lets workers move them across the field. A cranberry still attached to the vine isn't going anywhere.

Floating barriers called booms help corral the loose berries. Growers can then lift them out on a stepped belt, which keeps the round fruit from rolling back down, or use a pump and a grate to separate the berries from the water. The striking part is what the machines don’t have to do: after releasing each berry, they can collect the crop on the surface instead of feeling around the bottom for it.

Dry harvest looks different. The fruit is picked directly off the vines and moved out of the bed without a harvesting flood. The guide says dry-picked berries generally go to the fresh market, while wet-picked fruit commonly goes into processed products. That’s a useful explanation of why “wet harvest” and “fresh fruit” aren't synonyms. It’s not a way to tell which method was used for a bag of berries just by looking at it.

A bounce measures something the water can’t

Oregon State University Extension describes the bounce of a firm, intact cranberry as a bit like that of an inflated ball. A resilient berry can give slightly when it hits, then recover and spring away. A mushy berry can take up that impact without much rebound. But a soft spot needn’t fill every cavity with liquid. A berry can float and still be softer than a grower wants.

That difference had a practical use. A 1975 EPA processing report describes berries sent through a sorter with three steps and barriers. A berry had to clear all three. It’s a wonderfully low-tech idea: instead of asking a machine to feel every berry, let a good bounce send the fruit down one path and a poor bounce send it down another.

It wasn’t foolproof. A 1996 cranberry sorter patent points out two things that can throw the test off: a good berry isn’t always round enough to hop in the intended direction, and a damaged berry may happen to land on its firm side. The proposed alternative rolled berries through repeated squeezes so that different sides could be tested. At home, a hard plate is fun for watching the bounce, but there’s no reason to treat it as a substitute for inspecting the fruit.

What researchers learned by squeezing berries

The 2019 researchers also pressed berries separately to measure their firmness. They didn’t assume a big chamber meant a softer berry. Fruit size, the areas of the flesh and chambers, and compression force didn’t relate in exactly the same way earlier and later in the season. The printed cross-section reveals a lot about the shape, but it doesn’t give a reliable reading of how a particular berry will feel.

A 2025 study compared six groups with X-ray imaging, buoyancy measurements and compression tests. The researchers found differences after postharvest freezing. The firmer and softer varieties they compared took similar amounts of force to compress after freezing, and density wasn’t a good shortcut for firmness. Those were measurements on the groups they studied, not a prediction for every bag. They help explain why a cook can use frozen berries for a purée without expecting them to behave like fresh berries on the plate.

Put the fruit in the scoop, or on top?

For the Massachusetts Cranberries sorbet, fresh or frozen berries are cooked and blended. Lemon goes into the cooled mixture before it’s churned. The fruit is part of the frozen base, so you taste it throughout the scoop. The intact berry that made its way across the water has done its job by the time it reaches the blender.

In Manny Haider and Jessica Drakulich’s cranberry ice cream, cooked fruit is puréed with spices and pressed through a fine sieve into a prepared custard. The sieve does a different kind of sorting: it takes out pieces that would otherwise be felt in the finished scoop. You can see how that compares with the way a custard base gets its own texture in our article on egg yolks.

In Nigella Lawson’s cranberry syrup dessert, the berries burst in the pan, but the sauce is spooned over vanilla or white chocolate ice cream at the table instead of being blended into it. If you want a contrast between the fruit and the scoop, that’s the version to read first. Churning fruit into a base puts it in every bite; serving it separately lets you decide how much lands on each spoonful.

If you’re making dessert for a table with different tastes, the syrup version gives you an easy decision to leave until serving: put the pitcher alongside the bowls. Guests can try one spoonful with a berry, another with just syrup, and another with plain ice cream. In the strained custard those same comparisons aren’t possible, because the skins are gone and the fruit is already mixed in.

Quick questions about cranberries

Do cranberries grow underwater?

No. The berries grow on low vines in beds that are not normally submerged. A wet harvest temporarily floods the bed so the loosened berries can float to the surface.

Does a floating cranberry have to be firm?

No. Floating depends on how heavy the berry is for its size. A bounce depends on how the fruit responds to impact. Floating is not a reliable test of firmness or food safety.

Can you use frozen cranberries for sorbet?

Yes. The Massachusetts Cranberries sorbet recipe calls for fresh or frozen cranberries in its purée. The recipe cooks and blends the fruit before churning, so it doesn’t rely on the texture of the raw berry.
