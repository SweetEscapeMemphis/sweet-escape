Did the 1904 World’s Fair Invent the Ice Cream Cone?

Published October 15, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Ask how the ice cream cone began and you may hear a neat story about a busy stand at the 1904 St. Louis World’s Fair. It is a memorable tale—but the history is older and less certain than that. For Memphis dessert fans, that makes the cone a fun reminder that a familiar treat can have more than one origin story. The short answer is not “one inventor, on one afternoon.” The better answer is that edible holders, patent experiments, fairground storytelling, and changing ways of serving ice cream all contributed to the cone we recognize today.

That distinction is more than a historical footnote. When a food becomes popular, people often remember the dramatic moment when it reached a crowd, not every earlier experiment that made it possible. A world’s fair is a perfect setting for an origin story: many visitors, many vendors, and a product designed to be carried around. But popularity and invention are different milestones. The cone’s past is a good example of why the most honest food history leaves room for both.

The famous fair story has several versions

The Library of Congress recounts one version in which ice cream sellers at the fair ran short of serving dishes and a nearby pastry maker rolled a wafer into a cone. The familiar retelling often names Ernest Hamwi, a Syrian immigrant who sold a crisp pastry called zalabiyeh, and says he rolled it into a “cornucopia” for an ice-cream vendor. It is a vivid scene: a warm pastry, a cold scoop, and a practical answer to a serving problem. Yet the Library of Congress introduces it as a popular story, not a proven account of the first cone.

There are reasons to be cautious. The fair took place in 1904, but different accounts credit different vendors and describe slightly different encounters. The Library of Congress history says no one knows for sure who made the first one. It also points to references to paper and metal cones in Europe before the fair and to edible cones in earlier cookbooks. Those earlier objects may not match today’s standard wafer cone in shape or use, but they make a clean “invented at the fair” claim difficult to defend.

A Smithsonian Magazine account gathers multiple people credited with the fairground idea. Its more careful takeaway is that the fair helped make portable ice cream a commercial possibility for American audiences. That is still a remarkable contribution. A busy exposition could introduce visitors to a convenient way to enjoy a frozen treat while walking, even if the basic idea had older roots. The fair can matter to the cone’s story without being its single, verifiable starting point.

Another recently reported history adds useful context about the immigrant entrepreneurs and earlier edible-container experiments behind the legend. The Associated Press review of the cone’s history likewise describes competing claims around the fair, rather than settling on one universally accepted inventor. When the evidence supports several claims but not a final verdict, “the fair helped popularize it” is a stronger sentence than “the fair invented it.”

An earlier clue: a recipe called “Cornets with Cream”

One historical detail reaches back before the fair. The Smithsonian article points to an 1888 cookbook by British cookery writer Agnes Marshall, which includes a recipe called “Cornets with Cream.” The horn-shaped pastry cornets were filled with whipped cream, and the cookbook suggested other cold fillings too. It is evidence that edible, horn-shaped desserts existed before 1904—not proof that Marshall invented the modern ice cream cone.

Why does that recipe matter? It shifts the question from “Who had the first cone?” to “What counted as a cone, and when?” A crisp pastry horn holding whipped cream is not necessarily the same commercial product as a thin, crisp cone holding a scoop of ice cream. But it demonstrates that cooks had imagined an edible vessel for a cold filling decades before the St. Louis fair. Marshall’s recipe is a documented predecessor in the broader idea, not a claim to sole invention.

Food ideas often develop this way. A shape may exist in one kitchen, an edible holder may appear in a recipe, and a later vendor may adapt it for a new customer or occasion. Historical records preserve different pieces: a cookbook can show that a form was described; a patent can show a device was filed; a newspaper can document a public event. None automatically proves who first made every version. The cone’s history becomes clearer when these clues are treated as related evidence rather than forced into a single origin story.

There is also a documented patent milestone. In 1903, Italo Marchiony received U.S. Patent 746,971 for a mold. The patent describes apparatus for making ice-cream cups and similar forms. The original patent record is a useful distinction: an edible cup mold is not automatically the same thing as the pointed cone familiar today, and a patent does not settle every claim about who first served ice cream in an edible holder. The Library of Congress’s ice-cream industry patent guide also identifies Marchiony’s patent as a cup mold.

Another early twentieth-century patent story concerns Antonio Valvona, an Italian immigrant confectioner in Manchester, England, who developed machinery for baking edible biscuit cups. The Associated Press summarizes that earlier technical work as part of the lead-up to the cone’s American rise. The point is not that cups and cones are interchangeable; it is that entrepreneurs were already working on edible containers and the equipment to make them before the fair’s best-known stories took hold. That context makes it easier to see the cone as an evolving product rather than a lightning-bolt invention.

So what should we say about the cone?

The most responsible short version is that edible ice-cream holders developed over time, with multiple people and stories in the record. The 1904 fair appears prominently in the cone’s popular American history, but the “one inventor, one sudden moment” version is difficult to prove. The Library of Congress ice-cream patent guide points readers to Marchiony’s 1903 mold patent, while its historical blog preserves the fair story as a popular account and notes the uncertainty.

It helps to separate three ideas that are often rolled into one sentence:

  • Predecessors: Earlier recipes and serving vessels show that the broad idea of a portable or edible holder was not limited to the 1904 fair.
  • Patented equipment: Marchiony’s record documents a mold for cups and similar articles. It is evidence of a particular device, not a universal “first cone” certificate.
  • Popularization: Fair stories connect the cone with a large public audience and a practical way to eat ice cream while moving around. This can be historically important even if the exact first serving remains unknown.

That distinction also explains why accounts can disagree. A recipe, a cup-molding patent, and a fairground serving describe related but different milestones. “First” depends on the definition: edible pastry horn, purpose-made ice-cream cone, patented mold, or widely noticed public serving. The surviving records do not produce one simple answer to all of them.
