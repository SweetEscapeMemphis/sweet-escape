Spaghetti Ice Cream Is Real—But It Isn’t Pasta

Published November 6, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A plate of spaghetti can be a frozen dessert. Not pasta with a scoop on the side, but ice cream shaped into long strands, dressed to resemble tomato sauce and grated cheese. It is called Spaghettieis, and it is a real part of Mannheim’s ice-cream story. At Sweet Escape in Memphis, we think it makes a particularly good conversation starter: how much of a familiar food do you recognize from its shape?

The answer involves a borrowed kitchen tool, a young inventor’s experiments, and a careful choice of sweet ingredients. It also opens a useful question about food presentation. Looking like another dish is one thing; changing what someone expects or experiences is another.

The “noodles” are vanilla ice cream

The Mannheim museum’s description identifies vanilla ice cream formed with a Spätzle press, strawberry sauce, whipped cream, and grated white chocolate. Those ingredients supply the parts of the pasta-like picture.

Each visible component plays a recognizable role. The pale strands stand in for spaghetti. The red fruit sauce takes the place of tomato sauce in the picture. The grated white chocolate supplies the finishing detail that resembles Parmesan.

It is an imitation of a plate’s appearance, not a transformation of ice cream into cooked pasta. The description becomes much easier to understand when you separate what the eye recognizes from what the ingredient actually is.

The hidden cream is another useful detail: not every component has to be visible to help construct the dessert. On the company’s illustrated preparation page, the strands cover the whipped cream rather than leaving it as a separate mound beside the “spaghetti.”

The idea developed in Mannheim in 1969

Mannheim’s Reiss-Engelhorn Museums identify Dario Fontanella as the creator in 1969. Their August 2026 feature places spaghetti ice cream among inventions associated with the city.

That location is part of the surprise. The visual reference is Italian pasta, but the documented ice-cream invention belongs to Mannheim in Germany. A food can borrow the appearance of one culinary tradition while taking shape somewhere else.

The museum also describes the inspiration as a dessert made with pressed chestnut purée. The shared idea is a way of forming soft food into strands. The ice-cream version did not need to begin with a new noodle recipe; it needed a different use for an existing shaping technique.

This is a good way to think about food inventions more generally. Sometimes the interesting change is not a newly discovered ingredient. It is an existing ingredient placed in a form that makes people see it differently.

The first colors were not the final colors

In a 2025 interview with Anne Feldkamp, Fontanella recalled experimenting with pistachio, lemon, and strawberry ice cream in the colors of the Italian flag. His father’s reaction was that he had not seen colorful spaghetti like that. He also recalled buying the press with money from his grandmother and experimenting during an Easter family visit.

Fontanella described switching to vanilla, trying chopped raspberries before strawberry purée, and grating a white chocolate Easter egg for the cheese-like finish. These are his recollections of the experiments, rather than instructions for every version sold under the name today.

The sequence reveals why the finished presentation is so legible. The original color idea referred to a flag. The later choices referred more closely to a plate of pasta. Both were visual ideas, but they pointed toward different things.

That is our reading of the design process, not a claim that every viewer would identify the plate in exactly the same way. Pale strands, red sauce, and light shavings work together to suggest the intended dish.

The press supplies the shape

A scoop gives ice cream one familiar outline; the holes of a press can give it another. Fontanella’s preparation photographs show ice cream being pushed through those holes and laid onto the plate as strands. The company specifically calls for a chilled press. Its account says early attempts produced melted ice cream before he realized the press itself needed to be cold.

That makes the tool’s job easy to distinguish from the toppings’ jobs. The press creates the long forms. The sauce and shavings complete the visual comparison. Without having to invent a new ingredient for each part, the presentation assembles several recognizable cues.

If you open the illustrated preparation, look at the plate before and after the sauce is added. Ask which stage first makes you think of spaghetti. Is it the strands alone, their arrangement, or the final combination?

You do not need to reproduce the dessert to enjoy that observation. Reading the ingredient list beside the photographs is enough to follow the change from familiar frozen dessert to a very different-looking plate.

Can presentation change the eating experience?

A playful shape is not proof that a dessert tastes different. But presentation has been studied experimentally. In a 2014 study by Charles Michel and colleagues, researchers served the same salad ingredients in three arrangements: tossed together, neatly organized, or arranged with inspiration from a Kandinsky painting.

Participants evaluated the dish before and after eating. Before tasting, the art-inspired presentation received higher ratings for qualities including artistry and liking. After eating, it also received higher tastiness ratings.

That result concerns a particular salad experiment, not spaghetti ice cream or Sweet Escape products. It does not show that a different layout adds sugar, changes ingredients, or makes every person prefer the same plate.

What it does provide is a useful distinction between composition and experience. Researchers can hold the ingredients constant while changing their arrangement, then ask people about the result. A presentation can be more than decoration without becoming a new recipe.

There is no universally “best” plate color

Another study, published in 2013 by Betina Piqueras-Fiszman, Agnes Giboreau, and Charles Spence, examined three desserts served on white or black plates in a restaurant. The researchers found that plate color influenced diners’ ratings, but the pattern depended on which dessert was served.

That detail matters. It would be too simple to turn the research into a rule that one background always makes dessert better. The authors discussed both color contrast and learned color-flavor associations as possible influences.

The study was about complex desserts in a restaurant setting, not a test of whether people mistake ice cream for pasta. It helps explain why presentation questions deserve careful answers rather than a single dramatic promise.

When you look at spaghetti ice cream, enjoy the visual joke first. If you want to talk about how it might affect expectations, keep that as a separate question. The historical recipe demonstrates the resemblance; the research explores aspects of presentation under particular conditions.

A familiar dessert can keep changing its form

The museum’s 2026 feature includes one more twist: alongside the traditional presentation, it describes a version called “Spaghetto” in a spherical form. That is a description from the museum’s dated article, not a promise about any shop’s current menu.

It is an interesting contrast to the original idea. A dessert known for strands can inspire a version described as a sphere. The name, the ingredients, and the shape are related parts of a dish, but they are not the same thing.

Try using those three questions when you encounter an unfamiliar dessert: What is it called? What is it made from? How is it arranged? Answering them separately can clear up confusion much faster than guessing from one photograph.

It also leaves room for the fun. You can recognize what a presentation resembles while still being curious about what is actually in it.
