A dessert ingredient’s surprising navigation story

Honeybees Dance Directions to Food

Published November 10, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Here is a dessert conversation starter with a very small choreographer: a honeybee can return from a useful food source and give other bees directions by dancing. Not a happy little wiggle that merely says food exists. The waggle dance carries information about where to go.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, honey-roasted almonds appear in our gelato catalog. Following that ingredient word back toward the hive opens a surprisingly rich question: how can movement on a comb help another bee find flowers outside? The answer involves sunlight, timing, experience, and even the dancer’s audience.

Start with nectar, not a jar of finished honey

The Natural History Museum’s honey explainer describes honeybees collecting flower nectar with their tube-like tongues and carrying it in a specialized crop, sometimes called a honey stomach. Nectar and finished honey are not simply two names for the same material.

Enzymes begin changing sugars, and bees deliver nectar to other members of the colony. Water is removed, including through wing-fanning, before honey is stored in capped comb cells. The museum also notes that some honeybees collect honeydew from sap-feeding insects.

The dance belongs to the food-finding part of this larger story. It helps recruit foragers; it does not itself turn nectar into honey. Keeping those jobs separate makes the whole process easier to picture.

The useful message sits inside a repeating pattern

Arizona State University’s bee-dance guide describes a bee moving forward while waggling, then looping around to repeat the movement. The straight central portion is called the waggle run. Other bees follow the performance inside the hive.

The same guide explains that smell and a shared nectar sample provide additional clues about the flower patch. The movement is therefore part of a communication system, not an isolated signal floating free of the food’s other characteristics.

That is a useful way to understand the word “dance” here. We are naming a repeated biological behavior that carries information, not imagining that the colony is enjoying a musical performance. The small movements have a practical destination.

Up on the comb can mean toward the sun

NC State Extension’s dance-language reference explains the directional code. On vertical comb, the angle of the waggle run relative to upright represents the food’s direction relative to the sun outside.

A run upward indicates a source toward the sun; a downward run indicates the opposite direction. A run angled to one side represents that angle to the same side of the sun. The bee translates an outdoor directional relationship into an orientation on the comb.

Timing matters twice. The straight run’s duration generally increases with the distance being communicated. Separately, interpreting direction requires the sun’s position at the time of the dance. The same patch can require a different dance angle as the sun’s position changes.

So an upward-pointing dancer is not giving a permanent instruction to fly north. The reference is the sun, not the top edge of a printed street map.

For a tabletop version, imagine a card with “toward the sun” written at its top. An angled arrow represents a direction relative to that label. Moving the imagined sun changes the real-world heading represented by the same arrow. The reference frame matters as much as the arrow.

Distance is more interesting than a tiny mileage counter

How does a bee obtain the distance information to communicate? A 2024 study by Randolf Menzel and C. Giovanni Galizia examined visual motion and knowledge of the surrounding landscape. Optic flow is the movement of environmental images across the eyes during travel.

Narrow tunnels can produce strong optic-flow signals, making a short flight register as a longer distance in the dance. But the researchers found that prior exploration of the surrounding area could override this tunnel effect when bees had some visual access to the familiar environment.

The important point is not that one simple counter explains every trip. Visual motion and learned surroundings can interact. A bee’s dance gives us a window into navigation, while controlled experiments reveal that the information behind it depends on experience and circumstances.

Scientists have followed recruits with radar

A 2005 Nature paper by Riley and colleagues used harmonic radar to record the flight paths of bees recruited after following waggle dances. That addresses a different question from simply measuring a dancer on the comb: what do the receivers actually do afterward?

The researchers tracked individual recruits outside the hive to examine how the communicated information translated into flight. The journal’s account reports that recruits used the dance information and compensated for wind drift on the way toward the target.

This distinction is worth noticing whenever an animal behavior sounds almost too remarkable. Recording a signal and testing a receiver’s response are separate kinds of evidence. The flight-tracking work lets the story move beyond the hive’s dance floor.

Young dancers benefit from experienced nestmates

In a 2023 Science study by Dong and colleagues, bees that had no opportunity to follow dances before their own first performance produced more disordered dances, with larger directional divergence errors and incorrect distance encoding.

Experience improved their directional disorder, but the distance-encoding difference persisted in the study. Bees that could follow other dancers before beginning did not show those initial impairments.

The result adds a social-learning dimension to a behavior often presented as a fully automatic routine. It does not require us to pretend that bees attend lessons or use human language. The narrower finding is already wonderful: exposure to experienced dancers helped shape the accuracy of this communication.

A successful performance depends on more than having the right body movements available. How a young bee encounters the colony’s existing behavior can matter too.

Even the audience can change the performance

A March 2026 UC San Diego account of its team’s PNAS research describes another twist. The researchers varied the audience in the dancing area, including changing its composition while holding the number of bees constant.

When followers were scarce or the surrounding bees were less interested in following, the dancers moved around more and their directional performance became less precise. Audience size alone was not the entire story; the presence of receptive followers mattered.

That makes the dance more than a one-way broadcast from a forager that ignores everyone nearby. The social setting can affect the signal itself. The entertaining image of an insect performer with an audience has a specific experimental basis, without turning bee behavior into a claim about human stage fright.

One wiggle is not a perfectly precise map pin

Researchers also have to decide how much dancing to measure. A 2012 study by Couvillon and colleagues examined variation among repeated waggle runs. The first and last runs were more variable than runs in the middle of a dance.

The team found that decoding four consecutive middle runs could provide a useful estimate of the whole dance’s average direction and duration. This made large-scale decoding more efficient without requiring every repetition to be measured.

Repeated runs are not flawless carbon copies. Averaging can help researchers distinguish the underlying message from variation in its delivery. That is why a short clip of one movement should not be treated as a perfectly exact map coordinate, even when the general code is understood.

The dances can reveal seasonal food-finding challenges

In a 2014 PLOS ONE study, Couvillon, Schürch, and Ratnieks decoded 5,097 dances over two years in a mixed rural-urban landscape near Brighton, England. Mean communicated foraging distances were longer in summer than in spring or autumn.

The reported seasonal means were approximately 493 meters in spring, 2,156 meters in summer, and 1,275 meters in autumn. Additional nectar measurements did not show a compensating improvement in summer nectar quality.

The researchers interpreted the longer summer journeys as evidence of greater difficulty finding profitable forage in that study landscape. Those figures are not a Memphis seasonal forecast or a statement about every colony.

There is a lovely reversal here: a signal that helps bees recruit other bees can also help scientists ask where and when food is harder to find. The dance becomes evidence about the landscape, not merely a spectacle to admire.
