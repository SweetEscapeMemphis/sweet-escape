Rainbow Sherbet vs. Orange Sherbet: What Sweet Escape’s Published Records Show

Published September 16, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Rainbow Sherbet and Orange Sherbet are two distinct names in Sweet Escape’s scoop catalog. If you are deciding which one to look up, it helps to separate three questions: what the catalog calls each item, what the published nutrition records say, and whether either one appears on the live menu today. Those answers come from different parts of the website, and none should be guessed from color, name, or an old article.

This guide compares the available records without adding a taste description or a promise about current stock. Sweet Escape’s catalog files both products under Sherbet. Their archived nutrition entries use the same serving size and list many of the same values, while their added-sugar figures differ by one gram in that dated source. Both records also disclose milk and a shared-equipment caution. Use the details as a reading aid, then verify current packaging and availability directly when either affects your decision.

Two separate products, not two names for one card

The scoop catalog contains an individual Rainbow Sherbet card and an individual Orange Sherbet card. Both are assigned to the Sherbet category. That shared classification is a useful navigation clue, but it does not make the items interchangeable. Each has its own catalog entry and its own linked nutrition-label page: Rainbow Sherbet references page 51 of the published PDF, while Orange Sherbet references page 52.

Start with the exact name you mean. If you saw one flavor on a menu, screenshot, or conversation, search for that full product name rather than stopping at “sherbet.” This avoids accidentally reading the neighboring entry. The scoop catalog is the place to compare product records; the specialties menu is for composed desserts with several parts. A specialty that includes scoops may raise additional ingredient and availability questions, so a single flavor card does not automatically describe every component in a finished dessert.

Compare the dated nutrition records on the same serving basis

Sweet Escape’s catalog data lists a serving of 2/3 cup (104 grams) for each sherbet. On that stated basis, both entries list 150 calories, 2 grams of total fat, 1.5 grams of saturated fat, 0 grams of trans fat, 10 milligrams of cholesterol, 20 milligrams of sodium, 31 grams of total carbohydrate, 0 grams of fiber, 24 grams of total sugars, and 1 gram of protein. The listed added-sugar values are 22 grams for Rainbow Sherbet and 23 grams for Orange Sherbet.

These are figures in Sweet Escape’s published data, not a ranking or recommendation. They do not predict which dessert you will prefer or what a shop serving weighs. The 2/3-cup basis belongs to the nutrition record; it is not a statement about ordering options or portions at Sweet Escape.

The FDA explains that nutrition-label information is generally presented per serving and that serving size matters when reading calorie and nutrient amounts. For a packaged product, the FDA’s serving-size guide offers useful background. Here, the practical habit is simple: compare like with like. Looking at one product per 2/3 cup and the other per an assumed scoop or an entire container would mix different bases and could mislead.

What the one-gram added-sugar difference does—and does not—say

In the available entries, total sugars are listed as 24 grams for both products. Added sugars are listed separately as 22 grams for Rainbow and 23 grams for Orange. Keep those as two separate lines when comparing the cards. The FDA describes added sugars as sugars added during food processing or packaged in forms such as sweeteners; its added-sugars overview explains how the field appears on packaged-food labels.

A one-gram difference in an archived panel is just that: a difference in the listed figures. It does not support a claim that one sherbet is healthier, better for a particular diet, or a meaningfully superior choice. Nor should the numbers be used to infer a recipe, the source of sugars, or how a current container was formulated. If nutrition information is important for a purchase, read the latest label for the exact item and use that current source rather than drawing a conclusion from an older catalog record.

Sweet Escape’s data also lists 31 grams of total carbohydrate for each item, along with no fiber reported and 1 gram of protein. These figures can help you locate corresponding lines in a nutrition record, but they are not a complete guide to personal nutrition needs. This article is a menu-information explainer, not medical or dietary advice. For questions about an individual dietary plan, use guidance from a qualified professional rather than asking a flavor name or short web summary to answer a personal health question.

Milk is listed, and the equipment note matters

Both published product records identify milk under “Contains.” That means a visitor should not treat the word “sherbet” as evidence that either Sweet Escape item is dairy-free. In these specific catalog records, milk is declared for both. The records also present an equipment caution naming peanuts, tree nuts, wheat, soy, and eggs. Read that as a separate warning associated with equipment; it is not the same statement as the “Contains: Milk” field.

For an allergy question, open the exact Rainbow Sherbet details or Orange Sherbet details and read every published note. Sweet Escape’s general caution says recipes and labels can change and asks guests to verify the current container label when an allergy matters. The shop also notes that in-store cross-contact is possible. A catalog record cannot certify how an individual scoop is handled, and neither the category nor a blank field is a safety guarantee.

If the information affects whether you can order, identify the product by its full name and ask the shop to check the current packaging and address your specific ingredient or handling question. Do not transfer one flavor’s detail to the other, and do not assume that a shared category means matching ingredients. The nutrition and allergen guide explains how to read Sweet Escape’s product fields and cautions across the menu.

Why the source date belongs beside the numbers

The underlying entries in Sweet Escape’s product-data file identify a source date of June 18, 2019, and link to the corresponding pages in the shop’s nutrition PDF. This guide makes the age of that source explicit because a numerical detail is more useful when readers can judge its context. The date is not proof that the information is wrong; it is a reason to confirm the current manufacturer label instead of treating the figures as a newly verified formulation. The product pages link to the available source and repeat the label-check request, useful starting points rather than substitutes for fresh verification.

A careful way to compare the two

  1. Decide whether you mean Rainbow Sherbet or Orange Sherbet, and open that exact catalog entry.
  2. Compare nutrition values only when the serving basis is the same; the listed basis here is 2/3 cup (104 grams).
  3. Keep total sugars and added sugars as distinct fields, and note that the published source dates to 2019.
  4. Read the “Contains” and equipment caution separately; do not infer allergy suitability from the product name.
  5. Check the live menu for reported availability and ask the shop to verify current packaging when it matters.

In short: use the catalog for product records, the label for current packaged-food details, and the stock page for reported availability. Ask the shop when an important question remains; do not infer ingredients or safety from a color name.
