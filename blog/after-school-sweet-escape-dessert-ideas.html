A little planning, lots of possibilities

An After-School Sweet Escape: Dessert Ideas for Memphis Families

Published October 2, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

When the school day winds down, dessert can be the easy part of the afternoon: a chance for everyone to choose something that fits their own mood. The tricky bit for a family is that “something sweet” can mean different things. One person may want a scoop, another may like building a frozen-yogurt cup, and someone else may be drawn to a dessert that already combines a warm base and ice cream.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, there are several formats to consider, so a little plan can make the menu feel less like a quiz and more like a set of simple choices. This guide offers a practical way to order when tastes differ, plus examples based on items and formats the shop documents. Because availability changes, use every example as an idea to check—not a promise that a specific flavor will be in stock when you visit.

Start with the format, not a flavor debate

“What does everyone want?” is a big question when nobody is looking for the same thing. Try narrowing it to a format first: hand-dipped ice cream, self-serve frozen yogurt, gelato or sorbetto, a milkshake, or one of the composed specialties. Sweet Escape lists these options across its scoop menu, frozen-yogurt menu, gelato and sorbetto catalog, and specialties page.

That first question helps each person get closer to an answer without asking everyone to agree. A scoop-focused person can browse one section while a toppings fan explores the yogurt format. Someone interested in a more composed dessert can look at specialties. Different choices can coexist; the whole family does not have to choose one category just to keep the order simple.

Use taste words as filters, then read the product card

Family members often describe a craving with one word: chocolate, fruit, mint, coffee, cookies, or familiar. Use that as a browsing clue, not as a substitute for the product description. The catalogs include individual product records, and those records are the place to check documented ingredients, flavor notes, nutrition information, and allergen statements where provided.

For example, a person who says “chocolate” may want to compare a straightforward chocolate choice with a flavor whose description names cookies, brownie pieces, or another component. Someone asking for fruit can browse the fruit-named options across the scoop, yogurt, and gelato catalogs. Those are different ways to narrow the list; they are not claims that every example is available today or that all products with similar names are alike.

Names can be playful, and two similar names can refer to different products. If a family is weighing cookie-inspired yogurt options, our Cookie Monster vs. Cookies ’N Cream guide shows why reading the exact product detail is more useful than guessing from a name or color.

An easy combination: two scoops, two opinions

Sweet Escape describes Ice Cream Nachos as two scoops of your choice served with crisp round waffle chips for dipping. That makes it an easy format to discuss when two people have different scoop preferences: each can name a flavor they would like to consider, then the family can check what is currently listed.

The format offers two scoop selections; it does not establish a serving-size promise, a sharing rule, or any particular price. Decide for yourselves whether that specialty suits your group. If one person wants a familiar flavor and another is curious about a different one, the “two opinions” approach can help you narrow choices without trying to manufacture agreement.

Before you settle on the specific scoops, open the live in-stock menu. It is updated separately from the wider catalogs. Then check each product card for details that matter to your family. A menu photo may help you spot a category, but the written product information is the better place to confirm what a flavor is described as containing.

An easy combination: pick a base, then consider one scoop

For someone who knows they want a warm dessert base but has not picked an ice-cream flavor, start with one of Sweet Escape’s two documented formats. Cookie Dough Base is freshly baked chocolate-chip cookie dough with one ice-cream scoop on top. Brownie Base is a warm brownie finished with one scoop. In each case, the site describes the base and the scoop as the core combination.

This makes the decision a two-step conversation rather than a long list: cookie dough or brownie first, then a scoop from the currently listed choices. The Cookie Dough Base vs. Brownie Base guide has more detail on how those specialties differ. If someone in the family is choosing based on the base, they can focus on that preference while the scoop choice stays flexible.

Keep the wording of the menu in view when ordering. A product image may show a particular scoop, but that does not guarantee the pictured flavor is available or included as a default. Ask which scoop options are currently available if you have a specific pairing in mind. The descriptions do not promise unlisted toppings or substitutions, so confirm rather than assume.

A choose-your-own route: frozen yogurt, then toppings

Sweet Escape documents a self-serve frozen-yogurt format followed by a self-serve topping station. For a person who likes making a few small decisions, separate them into two steps: first, which listed yogurt sounds right; second, whether any currently available toppings appeal.

This approach can work neatly when the family has different decision styles. One person can keep the choice focused on the yogurt, while another can spend more time considering the topping options at the station. The topping choice is optional in the sense that the format starts with frozen yogurt and then offers the station; no one needs to make the same set of choices as the rest of the group.

Specific toppings and their availability can change, so avoid promising a particular combination based on a past visit or photograph. The frozen-yogurt cup guide walks through the general decision order. When an ingredient or allergy question matters, review the exact yogurt and each topping separately and ask the shop about current labels and handling.

For a cookie person, clarify which “cookie dessert” they mean

Cookie cravings can point to more than one kind of choice. A person might mean a cookie-named scoop, a cookie-inspired yogurt, a Cookie Dough Base, or a Cookie Ice Cream Sandwich. Sweet Escape’s menu describes the sandwich as ice cream between two chocolate-chip cookies; the base specialties have their own separate descriptions. Asking “Do you mean a cookie flavor, or cookies as part of the dessert?” can prevent a mismatch between what someone pictured and what they order.

Make a family shortlist without overplanning

A useful plan does not require every person to pick a final order from home. Try this simple sequence:

  1. Each person names a format or a taste word.
  2. Browse only the matching catalog section or specialty page.
  3. Choose one first idea and, if useful, one alternative.
  4. Check both against the live menu before relying on them.
  5. Read product details for ingredients, nutrition, and allergens when relevant.

That list leaves room for a change of mind. It also helps avoid a common planning trap: treating a catalog as if it were today’s case. The catalog explains products; the stock page reports what Sweet Escape currently lists as in stock. Check the update time shown there, and remember that availability can change.

Families can use different routes through the menu. One person may decide quickly, another may compare two descriptions, and a third may need an adult to review an ingredient statement. There is no need to make the ordering method identical for everyone. A short, flexible shortlist is enough to keep the conversation moving.

Keep allergy and dietary checks precise

When an allergy or dietary need affects the order, taste preferences are only one part of the decision. Do not infer ingredients or dietary suitability from a flavor name, photograph, color, or broad category. Read the exact item information, including any listed facility, shared-equipment, or cross-contact caution. The nutrition and allergen guide explains the product fields and why they should be checked individually.

A composed dessert may bring several products together. Review the base, scoop, cookie, yogurt, and toppings as separate components; one component’s product card does not cover the others. Online information may not answer every question, and it is not a guarantee against cross-contact. If the details matter for your family, call Sweet Escape at (901) 718-5812 and ask about current labels and handling before ordering. Do not treat “fruit,” “yogurt,” “sorbetto,” or “no sugar added” as proof that an item meets an individual dietary need.

Make the final choice from today’s information

For a Memphis after-school dessert idea, think of the menu as a set of flexible paths rather than one family-wide answer. Scoops give each person a flavor to consider. Ice Cream Nachos are documented with two scoop choices. Cookie Dough Base and Brownie Base each pair a named base with one scoop. Frozen yogurt starts with a yogurt choice and can continue at the topping station. Those formats offer different ways to organize the decision without requiring everyone to like the same thing.
