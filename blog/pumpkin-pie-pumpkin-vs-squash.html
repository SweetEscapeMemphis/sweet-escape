Does Pumpkin Pie Need a Carving Pumpkin?

Published November 13, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

If you picture the pumpkin in a pie, you might imagine a round orange jack-o’-lantern with the candle taken out. The fruit that ends up in a can can look quite different. Some pumpkins grown for processing have tan skins, and the name pumpkin is used for fruits from more than one squash species. The pie you know by its orange filling may have started with a fruit you would walk right past at a carving-pumpkin stand.

That does not make the pie a trick. It makes “pumpkin” an unusually useful word to look at from three places: the garden, the grocery shelf, and the dessert counter. Each uses the word to answer a different question. Here is what changes as you move from one to the next.

A jack-o’-lantern is one example, not the definition

Illinois Extension’s variety list sorts pumpkins by jobs as well as by species. It describes carving types, miniatures, jumbo pumpkins and processing types. Some of the miniatures are orange, while another is white. Among the processing types it lists are tan-skinned pumpkins, including Dickinson Field. There is no rule that a pumpkin has to be orange on the outside, much less the right size to set on a porch.

The scientific names tell a different story from those categories. Many familiar carving and small pie pumpkins are Cucurbita pepo. So are zucchini and acorn squash, as an Illinois Extension horticulturist explains. Processing pumpkins such as Dickinson Field are listed by Illinois Extension under Cucurbita moschata, the species that also includes butternut. Some giant pumpkins are Cucurbita maxima.

In other words, two fruits called pumpkins can belong to different species, and a fruit called a squash can share a species with one called a pumpkin. Picture a small orange pie pumpkin and a zucchini side by side: they do not have the same kitchen job, even though the species name can be the same. On a seed packet, the variety tells you something the species alone does not. On a shelf full of autumn decorations, shape and color may be all the shopper cares about.

A good pumpkin for the porch has a different job

Imagine choosing two fruits at a farm stand. For one, you want a stable base, space for a face and enough room inside to work. For the other, your questions are about the flesh: how much usable pulp do you get, and what will it be like once cooked and mashed? Those are separate decisions, even if both fruits are sold under the same big sign.

Illinois Extension’s winter squash guide points to less fibrous flesh as a reason some cooks prefer certain winter squashes for pies. For a filling that should slice without recognizable strands of squash, what happens under the rind matters more than the silhouette. It is also why a particularly large pumpkin is not automatically a more useful pie pumpkin. Bigger is an achievement in a growing contest; it does not answer what the cooked flesh will be like.

If you are buying a whole fruit for dessert, a useful question for the grower is “Which ones are for cooking?” It is more specific than asking for the most pumpkin-looking pumpkin. If you are following a recipe, check whether it calls for mashed cooked flesh or for a canned product. A whole fruit, a spoonful of cooked pulp and a measured amount of canned purée are different starting points.

Another useful clue is the harvest purpose. A processing variety is named for what growers expect to do with it after harvest. A small pie pumpkin sold whole can be a different variety with a different shape and skin. Both can make sense in a dessert conversation without being substitutes for one another in the field or looking alike on a kitchen counter.

What the word on a can can tell you

The FDA’s published labeling guidance describes a long history of packing canned pumpkin from field pumpkins, certain golden-fleshed sweet squashes, or mixtures. It says it considers the pumpkin designation acceptable under the provisions discussed for products made from those sweet squashes or mixtures with field pumpkin. The guidance also notes that mixing can be a choice about the consistency people want.

That is narrower than “anything can go in a pumpkin can.” It also does not mean that every brand uses a mixture. A general labeling policy tells you why the word can appear on more than one kind of product; it does not identify what any one producer grows or puts in its cans. For that, look at the particular label and any variety information the producer supplies.

The USDA’s canned pumpkin and squash grades give another clue about what matters at this stage. Their criteria include consistency and finish along with color, flavor and defects. There is no whole fruit left to inspect for a nice stem or carving shape. The thing being evaluated is the food you can spoon out. Variety matters upstream, but a cook will meet it as a prepared ingredient.

Purée and pie mix are different purchases

Two cans can both show a slice of pie and still ask you to do different things in the kitchen. Illinois Extension’s pumpkin cooking guide distinguishes plain canned pumpkin from pie filling with added sugar. A producer can also include spices in its pie mix, as Libby’s product FAQ illustrates. When the recipe expects plain purée and has its own sugar and seasoning, using a prepared mix changes what you are adding before you have measured anything else.

Next time you shop for a pie, read the product name and ingredient list together. The photo tells you what the maker thinks you might do with the contents. The label is a better guide to whether you are buying an unseasoned ingredient or a mixture intended for a particular dessert. That small check is useful even if you never learn the variety that grew in the field.

It also puts “pumpkin pie spice” in perspective. The seasoning and the fruit do different work in a pie. The name of the spice tells you the dish it is meant to evoke, not that a particular pumpkin variety is in the jar. A pie recipe brings those jobs together, but the words on the separate ingredients need not mean the same thing.

Before a can could do the prep work

There is a familiar idea in the 1796 edition of Amelia Simmons’ American Cookery, even if the spelling “pompkin” looks unfamiliar. The Library of Congress’s account of the recipes points out that the pumpkin was stewed and strained. It went into a filling with eggs, dairy and seasonings, baked in a crust. The cook was making something closer to a custard than a stack of pumpkin slices.

That is a helpful way to see what the can changed. It did not have to invent the idea of a smooth pumpkin filling. It could take over part of the work that already came before the filling. The Library points to early twentieth-century advertising and trade accounts as evidence of a market for canned pumpkin. The cook still had a pie to make, but could start with pulp rather than a whole fruit.

Those old recipes are not a single formula that defines every pumpkin pie. They show how a cook could use the flesh, and why a pumpkin chosen for its cooked texture makes sense. The name carries the memory of a dish; the ingredient list and preparation are what give a particular version its shape.

What “Pumpkin Pie” means at Sweet Escape

At our Memphis shop, Pumpkin Pie is the name of a San Bernardo ice cream in the scoop catalog. The listing describes a warm, spiced pumpkin flavor. That is a description of the flavor, not a serving temperature or a recipe. It does not identify a squash variety, the form of any pumpkin ingredient, the spice blend or the presence of pastry. There is also a separate Pumpkin Pie frozen yogurt record. The two names point to the same dessert idea, but their product information should be read separately.

The scoop listing does not publish a complete ingredient, nutrition, allergen or equipment panel.
