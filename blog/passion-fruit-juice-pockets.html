A little fruit science

What’s Inside a Passion Fruit? Tiny Pockets of Juice

Published November 20, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Cut a passion fruit in half and it looks as though somebody filled a purple bowl with golden jelly and sprinkled black seeds on top. But scoop up just one seed and some of the jelly comes with it. That isn’t an accident. A passion fruit doesn’t hold its juice in one big chamber. It holds it in little sacs around the seeds. Look even closer and there’s another surprise: two parts of the same sac can have different amounts of acid and sugar.

That makes this fruit particularly fun to follow into a frozen dessert. A cook can use the juice and leave the seeds behind, or bring a few seeds back for a little crunch. Neither approach requires the whole fruit to go into the scoop. Here’s what separates so neatly when the fruit hits the strainer, and what doesn’t.

The juice comes in little packages

Look at a cut fruit from the edge toward the middle. Outside is the colored peel. Just beneath it is a pale, spongy layer. And inside that is the glossy pulp with its dark seeds. The University of Florida’s anatomy guide has a side-by-side photo of a seed with its juicy covering and a seed without it. They look like two different things until you realize the dark seed is hidden inside the orange one.

That covering is called an aril. It isn’t another seed, or simply a bit of loose pulp that stuck to the seed when the fruit was cut. In an intact fruit, the structure has a small attachment back to the wall. The juice sits among membranes around the seed. A spoonful looks like one pool because it gathers a bunch of these little packages together.

The packaging helps explain a surprise in the garden, too. A firm shell isn’t proof that the fruit is full of juice. Florida’s extension guide for home growers documents fruit with soft seeds and poorly filled or missing arils. A USDA pollination handbook also describes experiments linking successful seed development and juice in the surrounding pulp sac. The shell and the contents you’d want to spoon out are two things to look at separately.

How scientists took one juice sac apart

People who study juice usually need a sample they can pour. But if you squeeze the entire fruit first, you can’t tell where anything was before the squeezing. Kyoto University researchers Tomohiro Kondo and Hirokazu Higuchi asked a different question: what would they find if they collected juice from different spots in the sac? Their 2024 paper on passion fruit anatomy includes a drawing and photos of the pieces they separated.

Think of it as opening a package without spilling one part into the next. First they removed the outer membrane and the pulp it held. Inside, they separated the pale pulp surrounding the seed from a darker yellow, fringed part at the base. They pooled like pieces and squeezed them separately. The comparison wasn’t between the thick fruit peel and the sweet stuff inside. All three portions came from the small unit around the seed.

They tested a purple passion fruit, a yellow one and a hybrid called Summer Queen. In all three, the inner portion around the seed had more citric acid per amount of juice than either of the other portions. The measurements for malic acid didn’t show that same pattern. And the fringed base had the highest reading for total soluble solids, which includes dissolved sugars but isn’t the same as a direct sweetness rating. The researchers measured individual sugars separately.

The surprise is that you don’t even have to compare different fruits to see a difference. It shows up within one tiny sac. That’s also why a measurement from a glass of mixed juice answers a different question. It tells you about what reached the glass, not whether the fruit had an especially tart pocket beside a sweeter one.

Yellow outside doesn’t tell you everything inside

The three varieties didn’t divide up their juice in identical ways. In the purple fruit studied, about 42 percent of the juice by weight came from the outer portion of the sacs. In the yellow fruit, the portion around the seed supplied about 41 percent and the outer part about 26 percent. Summer Queen’s juice was split more evenly among the three portions. Those percentages belong to the fruits from that greenhouse study, not to every fruit with a yellow or purple peel.

That matters because the amount of juice in a portion and the concentration of acid in it are separate. If a tart portion supplies more of your glass, its chemistry counts more in the blend. The researchers found both effects in the yellow fruit they tested: it had higher acid concentrations in the portions, and the most citric-acid-rich portion also supplied the largest share of its juice. They suggested the way juice is extracted could matter if a maker wants a particular result. They didn’t test a kitchen strainer or declare that a harder press always makes a more sour dessert.

What about the wrinkles you sometimes see on the outside? A storage study of purple passion fruit measured whole-fruit weight separately from pulp yield. Fruit could lose weight and shrivel as moisture left the peel without losing the same proportion of pulp. A higher share of pulp by weight doesn’t mean new pulp grew inside: if the peel loses water, the same amount of pulp would account for more of the total. The researchers also saw pulp yield drop late in storage. Wrinkles aren’t a reliable way to tell how much juice you’ll get, much less which part of the sac supplied it.

Another lab asked what you could learn from the air above passion fruit juice. In a study of juices from Tenerife, researchers identified some aroma compounds common to both the purple and yellow samples and found a different profile in a group selected from purple plants. Two pieces of fruit can have similar-looking pulp without giving off the same mix of aromas. Nor would a reading for acidity alone tell you what either one smells like. That’s the same useful split between aroma and taste we explore in our vanilla story.

What a sieve changes in a frozen dessert

When you see seeds on a passion fruit dessert, you’re seeing a choice about texture, not necessarily how the base was made. In his banana–passion fruit gelato for the Institute of Culinary Education, chef Rory Macdonald collects the juice for the base and keeps the seeds apart for a garnish. The fruit makes two appearances: as juice mixed through the gelato, then as something you can see on top.

David Lebovitz’s passion fruit and tangerine sorbet takes a different route. The recipe accepts strained fresh pulp or thawed frozen purée for the fruit portion. A note suggests putting a few reserved seeds into the sorbet after churning. That places the occasional seed inside the scoop instead of on the surface. It’s a small example of how when you add something changes where it shows up in each spoonful.

If you’re reading a recipe, pay attention to when it tells you to measure. A cup scooped straight out of the shells includes the dark seeds and their coverings; a cup measured after straining doesn’t. The recipe may name the fruit in big letters at the top, but the smaller words next to the quantity tell you what actually needs to go into the bowl.

On a menu, the fruit name is a useful starting point, but it doesn’t answer those preparation questions either. And two icy desserts can share a fruit name while being different kinds of dessert. Our guide to sorbetto explains what the category can tell you and when to look up the exact item.

Two quick questions about passion fruit

Are the black specks inside a passion fruit seeds?

Yes. The dark seeds are wrapped in juicy pulp. Each seed has its own surrounding structure, called an aril.

Does passion fruit sorbet always contain seeds?

No. A recipe can strain the seeds out, leave them in, or add some back after churning. Check the specific recipe or product rather than assuming from the name.
