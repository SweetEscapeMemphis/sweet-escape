A citrus color mystery

Why Can Cool Weather Make Blood Oranges Redder?

Published November 14, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Cut into a blood orange and you may find a few red streaks, a dark center, or segments that look almost purple. The peel does not always give the surprise away. And here is the part that sounds backward if you are thinking about frozen dessert: some of that color can build in cool conditions before the fruit ever goes near a freezer. In experiments with whole blood oranges, it has even increased after harvest.

Cold is part of the story, but not because it turns ordinary orange juice red like a novelty cup changing color. The fruit can make more of its own pigment. That difference is the key to understanding the dark fruit behind a name like Sicilian Blood Orange sorbetto.

The red is a pigment made by the fruit

The reds and purples in blood orange flesh come from pigments called anthocyanins. The name describes a family, not a single pigment that appears in the same amount in every fruit. In a 2002 study of Florida-grown Budd Blood oranges, Hyoung S. Lee separated several anthocyanins. Two related cyanidin compounds accounted for about four-fifths of the anthocyanins measured in those samples. Even a glass of juice that looks like one color can contain several of the molecules that contribute to it.

The Florida detail matters. The study tells us what was measured in a particular orange variety, not the percentages in every variety or batch. It also tells us nothing about the amount in a scoop of sorbetto. A measurement of fresh fruit and a measurement of a finished dessert would be different pieces of evidence.

Nor does a red interior mean the fruit has lost its ordinary orange pigments. Lee and William Castle’s study of three orange juices compared carotenoids as the fruit matured. Budd Blood and Hamlin showed similar changes in those pigments, but the Budd Blood samples also developed red anthocyanin pigment. Think of that as an extra source of color, not a switch that replaces all the orange with red.

Peel and pulp don’t always give the same answer

The UC Riverside Citrus Variety Collection’s Moro record describes fruit with orange peel that may have a burgundy blush and flesh that can be deeply colored. Its notes distinguish the two tissues: in some growing conditions, the rind can lack that blush even when the flesh has substantial internal color. The part you see on the produce shelf is not necessarily the part that tells you the most about pigmentation.

There is variation within familiar names, too. The collection’s Thermal Tarocco record notes that this selection had the least pigmentation of the three Tarocco selections the collection compared at Riverside. That is an observation from one place, not a ranking for grocery shopping. It is a useful reason to resist the idea that every blood orange should match a single photograph.

If you are comparing fruit at home, look at the cut face before squeezing. A glass of juice shows the combined color. A cut fruit can show where that color was strong and where the flesh stayed lighter. You do not have to measure anything to see that those are two different views of the same fruit.

Why cool conditions can matter

In a 2012 study in The Plant Cell, Eugenio Butelli and colleagues investigated a gene with the fitting name Ruby. It makes a protein that helps turn on pigment production. The team traced the cold-responsive trait of the Sicilian blood oranges it studied to a piece of mobile DNA that had landed next to that gene. The change affected when the fruit turned the gene on.

The distinction is like the one between a lamp and its switch. The pigment is what you see, but the change the researchers traced was in how the fruit starts the process that makes it. Cool conditions could prompt the fruit to use those instructions. It was not a case of cold adding a red ingredient from outside.

The researchers also found a blood orange of Chinese origin with a separate insertion of similar mobile DNA. Its fruit’s pigmentation was cold-responsive too. The surprise there is that the team did not need a single family story to account for both examples: two separate changes were associated with a similar result. A name based on how the fruit looks does not tell you that every orange acquired the trait in the same event.

After harvest, colder was not always redder

Could the fruit still respond once it was off the tree? Lourdes Carmona and colleagues tested this in 2017 by storing harvested Moro blood oranges and Pera, a non-blood sweet orange, at two cool temperatures. Under those conditions, anthocyanins increased in the Moro fruit but not in the Pera comparison. And the warmer of the two cool treatments produced darker Moro flesh and higher pigment levels.

That is more informative than simply saying “cold makes oranges red.” One comparison asked whether the two varieties responded in the same way; the other asked whether the colder treatment had the bigger effect. In both cases, the answer was no. Picking the fruit did not end the process in the Moro samples, but neither did a temperature change make the Pera samples follow the same path.

In a separate 2020 study, Fariborz Habibi and colleagues looked at four blood orange cultivars over time at two storage temperatures. They measured individual anthocyanins as well as total pigment. The study gives a sense of the questions a single photo cannot answer: which cultivar was tested, how long was it stored, and which compounds actually changed? A dark-looking half tells you something about appearance, but it is not a record of those conditions.

These experiments were about whole harvested fruit, not a recipe for turning an orange darker at home. They also do not show that the same thing happens when a dessert freezes. The fruit’s ability to make pigment and the temperature at which you enjoy a sorbetto are different subjects.

There is one more useful distinction: color did not have to be judged by comparing pictures taken in different light. The researchers could compare measured pigment in pulp and juice with the appearance of the fruit. That makes the claim narrower and stronger. A photograph shows what caught the eye; a chemical measurement helps tell you which part of the color changed. Neither on its own can tell you whether a particular orange from a fruit stand has been stored under the conditions used in the study.

A pink orange can get its color another way

There is a useful grocery-shelf comparison. The Citrus Variety Collection’s Cara Cara profile describes a pink-fleshed navel orange and attributes its color to lycopene, which is a carotenoid. That is a different pigment family from the anthocyanins behind the red in blood oranges. Pink or reddish flesh on its own is not enough to tell the two stories apart.

Think of two paint samples that look similar in the store. They need not contain the same pigments. With fruit, the variety name or a measurement tells you more about the source of the color than the first glance does. It is also why a darker piece of fruit does not give you a shortcut to its sweetness, ingredient list, or nutritional value.

This is not quite the same puzzle as the blue color on a blueberry’s surface. That story starts with light scattering from wax on the outside. Here the most revealing view may be inside the fruit, and the question is which colored compounds are present there. The right explanation depends on which surface or tissue you are looking at.
