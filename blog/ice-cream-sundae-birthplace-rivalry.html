The Ice Cream Sundae Has a Birthplace Rivalry

Published November 7, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

One of the sweetest arguments in food history concerns a very simple idea: put something on top of ice cream. Two Rivers, Wisconsin, and Ithaca, New York, both claim a place at the beginning of the ice cream sundae story. Their versions involve different dates, different toppings, and different kinds of evidence. A dessert that fits in a bowl has become a much bigger local-history question.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, we think that makes a good fun fact to bring to a sundae conversation. Who invented the treat? The answer is more interesting than a single name on a trivia card. Here is how to enjoy the rivalry while keeping the documented details straight.

Two Rivers tells a chocolate-syrup story

The Two Rivers Historical Society credits local ice cream parlor proprietor Ed Berners with serving the first sundae in 1881. In its account, a customer ordered ice cream and asked for chocolate syrup on top.

That is a wonderfully small action to imagine at the center of a large invention claim. No new freezing machine or unfamiliar ingredient is required in the story. The change happens when the syrup becomes a topping on a dish of ice cream.

The society’s Washington House Museum description identifies Berners’ Ice Cream Parlor as a feature commemorating the local 1881 invention account. The story is part of how the community presents its heritage, not just a stray sentence on a list of dessert trivia.

For this comparison, the important distinction is between the date a community attributes to an invention and the date of a surviving record. The historical society clearly states its claim. That does not, by itself, settle every competing account of who first combined ice cream and a topping.

Ithaca tells a cherry story

The History Center in Tompkins County tells a different story. Its account places Reverend John M. Scott at Chester C. Platt’s pharmacy on Sunday, April 3, 1892. Vanilla ice cream was topped with cherry syrup and a candied cherry, and the dish was called a “Cherry Sunday.”

Cornell notes that accounts differ about who chose the Ithaca toppings. Its article also discusses Christiance’s 1936 letter describing the event, written 44 years after 1892. That time gap makes the difference between a contemporary advertisement and a later recollection especially important when reading the details.

The two accounts are not descriptions of identical plates. Chocolate syrup supplies the finishing touch in the Two Rivers version. Cherry syrup and a candied cherry define the Ithaca version. Both stories are about assembling a dessert, but the details give each community’s account its own identity.

It is also worth separating a name from a food combination. Someone might serve ice cream with syrup before another person advertises a particular named specialty. Establishing when a name appeared in print answers a narrower question than proving the earliest occasion anyone ever put a topping on ice cream.

A tiny advertisement makes the story tangible

In a 2025 Cornell University article, Corey Ryan Earle identifies an April 5, 1892, Ithaca Daily Journal advertisement for Platt & Colt’s Cherry Sunday. The article also describes a similar Daily Sun notice the following day.

This is where the history becomes something you can look at rather than simply repeat. Cornell reproduces a small advertisement amid other newspaper notices. Its dessert name stands out, followed by sales copy describing an ice cream specialty and identifying the fountain selling it.

Try reading the reproduction in two passes. First, find the product name and the seller. Then look at how little room the advertiser used to introduce the treat. A few lines had to tell a reader that something new was available and where to find it.

The dated advertisement is evidence that the named dessert was being promoted at that time. It is not a photograph of its first serving, and it cannot capture every similar dessert that may have gone unadvertised elsewhere. That limited conclusion is still valuable: it anchors part of the story to a specific printed record.

The paper trail extends beyond dessert advertising

There is a real archive behind the pharmacy name. Cornell University Library’s guide to the Chester C. Platt papers identifies collection 195 in its Division of Rare and Manuscript Collections. The collection includes drugstore correspondence, accounts, newspapers, local-history materials, and bound volumes.

The guide describes correspondence involving prices and statements of accounts, alongside records of Platt’s other activities. That is a reminder that the person in a dessert-origin story had an ordinary working life around it. Running a drugstore involved paperwork as well as memorable moments at a fountain.

An archive guide is a map to material, not a substitute for reading every document. Its existence should not be inflated into a claim that every detail of the sundae story has been independently proved. It tells a curious reader where further research could begin.

That is a useful habit with food history: ask what sort of object you are looking at. A newspaper advertisement, an account book, a later recollection, and a museum description can all contribute something, but they do not necessarily establish the same thing.

Why does “sundae” have that unusual ending?

The spelling supplies another little mystery. Cornell’s account says the reasons for the change from “Sunday” to “sundae” remain unclear. It discusses a later recollection by fountain worker DeForest Christiance mentioning syrup manufacturers and alternative spellings.

That leaves room for curiosity without requiring a neat, invented explanation. The early advertised name and the familiar modern word are visibly different. Explaining exactly how one spelling prevailed is a separate historical problem from identifying a bowl’s ingredients.

So if someone asks whether the word is simply a misspelling of a weekday, a careful answer is more useful than a confident legend: “Sunday” appears in the early Ithaca advertising, while the route to the modern spelling is not completely settled.

The sundae even became postage-stamp material

Move forward to June 30, 2016, and the dessert appears in another kind of printed record. The U.S. Postal Service’s Soda Fountain Favorites announcement describes a booklet with five illustrated designs: a double-scoop cone, an egg cream, a banana split, a root beer float, and a hot fudge sundae.

The dedication took place at Elliston Place Soda Shop in Nashville, giving this part of the story a Tennessee setting. Nancy Stahl illustrated the stamps, with art direction and design by Ethel Kessler. The booklet’s silver-toned geometric decoration was intended to evoke a chrome-accented soda fountain.

Those choices preserve more than a dessert name. They place the sundae beside other recognizable fountain treats and use design to recall the setting in which such foods were served. A very small image can carry a surprisingly detailed reference to a dining tradition.

The stamp announcement documents a celebration in 2016. It is not a birth certificate for the dessert or a verdict between the two origin stories. What it shows clearly is that the sundae had become something worth representing as a familiar American fountain favorite.

The interesting question is bigger than “who wins?”

There are several possible milestones hiding inside the word “invented”: an early serving, a memorable name, a printed advertisement, or the point when a dish became widely recognizable. A good conversation starts by deciding which milestone you mean.

You can enjoy the Two Rivers account of chocolate syrup and the Ithaca account of cherries without flattening them into one story. You can also value the dated advertisement without asking it to prove an event it never recorded. The fun is in noticing the differences, not erasing them.

For a quick table-side question, ask everyone which clue they would investigate first: a menu, a newspaper, a shop’s account book, or someone’s recollection. Each choice suggests a different way into the past. Suddenly, a familiar dessert has become a small history investigation.
