A fluffy shape with some serious science

Why Does Cotton Candy Seem to Disappear? A Sweet Escape Fun Fact

Published October 25, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Cotton candy looks substantial until it meets a wet mouth. Then the fluffy shape seems to vanish, leaving a much smaller, sweet experience behind. Where did that cloud go?

Here is a fun fact for your next dessert conversation at Sweet Escape in Memphis: the sugar has not disappeared. Water can dissolve the delicate sugar strands, and their open structure makes the change especially dramatic. Losing the visible shape is not the same as losing the material.

There is another twist worth keeping in mind at an ice-cream shop. Actual spun cotton candy and an ice cream with cotton candy in its name are different things. Understanding the first can make you curious about the second without assuming that their ingredients or textures match.

First, the fluffy candy is still a solid

Colorado State University’s Little Shop of Physics uses cotton candy to teach states of matter. Its explanation follows sugar through a machine: solid sugar becomes liquid, passes through small holes, and becomes long, thin solid strands as it cools.

The finished candy looks very different from the starting sugar, but both are solids. A cloudlike appearance does not make the sugar a gas. Nor does a soft-looking tangle mean each individual strand is liquid.

That is a useful distinction when talking with a curious child. Ask what changed about the shape before asking what happened to the substance. A collection of strands and a pile of grains can look nothing alike while both consist of solid material.

The machine changes the arrangement you can see. Looking at that arrangement is the first step toward explaining why a big-looking portion can collapse so dramatically.

Candy can be a kind of glass

The word glass usually brings a window to mind. In materials science, however, it also describes a noncrystalline solid structure. That broader meaning lets scientists talk about a sugar glass without suggesting that anyone should eat window glass.

The American Chemical Society’s candy-science explanation contrasts ordered sugar crystals with an amorphous, or irregularly arranged, solid. Rapid cooling during cotton-candy production prevents the molecules from organizing into the familiar crystalline arrangement.

The result is fine threads of sugar glass. The familiar ingredient has a different structure, not a new identity as cotton or fabric.

This also makes cotton candy an interesting comparison with rock candy. The latter is associated with large sugar crystals; cotton candy depends on a different arrangement. An ingredient list alone does not describe all the physical structures that an ingredient can form.

Dissolving is not the same as melting

In everyday conversation, “it melts away” is a perfectly understandable description. For the chemistry question, though, dissolving is the more useful process to follow when sugar meets water.

In its lesson on why water dissolves sugar, the American Chemical Society explains that water and sucrose molecules have polar regions that attract one another. Whole sucrose molecules can separate from neighboring sucrose molecules and mix among water molecules.

The sugar molecule itself does not break into its individual atoms during this dissolving process. The visible solid can disappear from view while its molecules remain in the liquid.

Melting describes a solid becoming liquid; dissolving involves mixing a substance into a solvent. Cotton-candy production includes heating and cooling, but that does not make every later loss of its shape another melting event.

Your mouth supplies liquid as well as warmth. Following the water helps explain the apparently disappearing candy without imagining that the sugar simply turns into air.

Thin threads give water many places to make contact

Think about the difference between a compact lump and the same amount of material stretched into fine strands. The shapes present different amounts of exposed surface. In the strands, liquid can make contact along many small pieces rather than just around the outside of one solid block.

A food-science review hosted by Texas A&M specifically describes cotton candy as a sugar glass with a high surface area and notes its susceptibility to moisture uptake.

The visible cloud is an arrangement of threads with spaces between them. Once that supporting arrangement is lost, you are no longer looking at the same fluffy outline, even though the sugar has not ceased to exist.

This is why shape belongs in the explanation alongside composition. “Made with sugar” tells you something important. “Made as a delicate network of fine sugar strands” tells you much more about the situation water encounters.

A single droplet can do something surprising

The science gets even more playful when researchers look at just one strand. In a 2026 Physical Review Fluids study, Dorbolo and colleagues analyzed a water droplet attached to the bottom of a vertical sugar fiber.

A droplet could remain suspended when capillary force balanced its weight. As sugar dissolved into the droplet, the fiber weakened and eventually broke. Depending on the conditions, the droplet could fall—or jump upward while staying attached to the remaining fiber.

The researchers related the behavior to droplet volume and fiber diameter. The upward movement followed an imbalance in capillary force after the fiber weakened or broke.

That is a wonderful example of an ordinary sweet inspiring a precise physics question. It is not a claim that every bite behaves like the laboratory setup. It shows that the changing relationship between water and a sugar strand has more going on than a simple before-and-after photograph reveals.

Even moisture in the air can change the structure

A candy does not need to be dropped into a drink to encounter water. Humidity matters, too.

In their 2004 cotton-candy storage study, Theodore and Peter Labuza examined samples held at a controlled temperature and different relative humidities. They tracked moisture changes, observed the samples, and used X-ray diffraction to investigate crystallization.

Their work describes cotton candy taking up moisture, becoming rubbery, and collapsing and crystallizing under sufficiently humid experimental conditions. Much drier conditions preserved the structure for longer.

This is a different setting from a wet mouth, but it adds another part to the story: water need not arrive as a visible splash. A glassy sugar structure can change through its interaction with its surroundings.

Those controlled observations are not a shelf-life promise for every packaged candy. Packaging, formulation, and conditions would need their own evidence. The practical takeaway is to follow the actual product’s storage directions rather than judge its stability from how dry it looks.

Scientists have tested ways to slow crystallization

Researchers have also investigated whether changing the sugar mixture can affect that instability. A 2006 study by Leinen and Labuza examined cotton candy made with sucrose and a small proportion of another sugar, raffinose.

They stored samples under different relative humidities and used X-ray diffraction patterns and glass-transition measurements to investigate the material. Their results showed that raffinose slowed sucrose crystallization under the tested conditions.

The point is not a recipe recommendation. It is a reminder that “cotton candy” can also be a model for studying how a food’s composition and environment influence its physical structure.

Collapsing and crystallizing are related observations, but they are not interchangeable words. One describes a change in the larger shape; the other concerns molecular order. Researchers use different measurements because looking at a sagging sample cannot answer every question about what happened inside it.
