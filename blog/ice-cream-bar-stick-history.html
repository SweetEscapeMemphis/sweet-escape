A small handle with two big jobs

The Ice Cream Stick Had a Job Before You Held It

Published November 12, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

The wooden stick in an ice-cream bar looks like the simplest part of dessert. But in some early designs, it had a job before anyone took a bite. It let a maker move a frozen portion through a coating station, or pull a water ice out of its mold in one piece. Only then did it become the little handle the customer carried away.

That is the fun fact hiding in the freezer aisle: two famous treats looked alike in the hand, but the sticks solved different problems on the workbench. Their patent drawings make those differences easier to see than any origin story about a lucky night of cold weather.

First, a chocolate shell did the work

In January 1922, Christian K. Nelson received a patent for a coated frozen confection. Picture a small brick of ice cream enclosed in an edible shell. The shell was meant to help keep the portion together while it was handled. Nelson also described a version in which a thin chocolate slab or a wafer could serve as the base before the rest was coated.

The interesting thing to look for in Nelson’s drawing is what is not there: a stick. The treat did not have to be lifted by a handle to count as a bar. Instead, Nelson treated the surface you could touch as part of the design. The coating had a job beyond adding another flavor.

A shell and a stick are two answers to a practical question: what do your fingers actually hold? It is the same question a cup, cone, or sandwich answers in a different way. Each changes how you pick up the dessert and where you start eating it.

Harry Burt put the handle in before cutting the bars

Look at the grid of upright sticks in Harry Burt’s 1923 process patent and it takes a second to see what you are looking at. It is not a tray of individual bars. The sticks go into one large, partially frozen block. They are spaced so that after the block hardens and is cut, each piece has a handle of its own.

The order solves two problems at once. The partly frozen mixture is firm enough to hold the handles in place, but not so hard that pushing in a stick would break the block. Once the portions are separated, each one can be picked up by its handle and dipped in a coating. One drawing even shows a rack that holds the sticks so the coated portions can stand apart.

In other words, the customer’s handle was also the maker’s grip. There was no need to find a fresh place to pinch the bar every time it moved. Burt even described an edible handle made of hard candy as a possibility. That is in the document, not a claim that it was what customers usually bought.

Frank Epperson’s ice pop had to let go of a mold

Epperson’s 1924 patent shows an individual portion, not a block cut into pieces. He pictured a smooth cylindrical vessel that could be an ordinary test tube. There is a stick down the length of the syrup in the tube, and in one drawing a board bears down on the sticks across several molds so they do not float out of position.

The shape of the stick mattered to Epperson. He illustrated a square cross-section and preferred porous wood. In his description, the frozen mixture could grip the corners and small surface irregularities. If you twisted the handle a little, you wanted that movement to turn the frozen dessert too, not leave it sitting in the tube while the stick spun loose.

That is a balancing act: the frozen material needs to hold tight to the handle, but release from the walls around it. Epperson described warming the outside of the vessel slightly to loosen the surface. The point of the warming was not to melt the whole treat. It was to free one boundary while keeping the other connection intact.

One stick could even stick out of both ends

By the 1930s, another patent took the placement problem in an unexpected direction. The 1935 frozen-confection mold patent illustrates one long handle through a frozen portion, with a piece sticking out at each end. A tapered mold guides the bottom of the stick into a narrow socket that keeps it centered. After the mold releases, the upper end is there to lift the confection.

That drawing is not the familiar two-stick ice pop, the kind you could try to snap into halves. The Popsicle brand’s own history describes that separately as a shareable design. Here, one piece of wood goes all the way through. Put the two ideas side by side and they tell you what to look for: is the second grip for another serving, or is it the other end of the very same handle?

Patents show what somebody described and drew. They do not tell us that every design on the page became a common treat. That is precisely what makes the odd ones worth finding: they let you see a problem someone thought was worth solving, even if your own freezer has nothing that looks quite like the sketch.

The shape was easier to recognize than the category

Looking at a stick alone would not tell a buyer whether the frozen part was water ice or ice cream. It did not settle the question for the companies, either. The National Archives’ account of the surviving court records describes a 1925 licensing deal that assigned water ices and sherbets to one side and reserved ice cream and similar treats for the other. The agreement addressed shape, too: cylindrical treats went on the ice-pop side of the deal, while rectangular forms were reserved to Burt.

A later milk-based pop prompted a dispute over what the companies had meant by sherbet. That detail makes the old dispute more revealing than a contest over who held a stick first. Two things can look much the same in a wrapper, but their ingredients and the way they were described can put them in different categories. The agreement was between those businesses; it is not a definition to apply to every dessert label today.

One particularly good detail for anyone reading the case: the courts upheld the interpretation of the product division, but did not settle the argument about shape. That is easy to miss if all you remember is that the companies fought over pops. The boxy outline might help you recognize a dessert, but that did not make it the deciding fact in this dispute.

From the workbench to the curb

Making a bar portable changed what was possible for the seller as well as the eater. The Mahoning Valley Historical Society’s account traces Burt’s business from a Youngstown confectionery to a factory and a fleet of refrigerated trucks. A bell that had belonged to the Burt family’s bobsled helped announce the neighborhood deliveries.

There is a later, very tangible piece of that story at the Smithsonian: a 1938 Chevrolet Good Humor truck used around Boston. It is not one of the patent drawings come to life. It documents another step in getting the treat to the customer. The stick could make a bar easy to hand over, but it took a refrigerated vehicle to bring that bar down the street frozen. The tiny handle and the full-size truck solved two different parts of the same trip.

If you compare this with the penny-lick glass, there is another quiet difference. The street seller needed the reusable glass back after a serving. A frozen bar’s handle could go with the customer. One arrangement treated the holder as a piece of the seller’s equipment; the other sent that piece away with the dessert.

When the handle is part of the dessert

Here in Memphis, Sweet Escape has a different answer to what your fingers hold. Our Cookie Ice Cream Sandwich listing describes your choice of ice cream pressed between two chocolate-chip cookies. The cookies give you a place to hold it and are part of the bite, which is a different arrangement from either a bar’s stick or a shell that encloses the whole center. This is a comparison of formats, not a claim that we serve the historical ice pops or bars.

Next time you choose a dessert, look at where you take the first bite. On a stick, it might be the top edge. With a sandwich, you can aim for a bite with both cookie and ice cream. A spoon gives you yet another choice. The holder might seem like an afterthought, but it helps decide how the dessert is eaten.
