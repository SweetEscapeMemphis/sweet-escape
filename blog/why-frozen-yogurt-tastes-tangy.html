A little frozen yogurt science

Why Does Frozen Yogurt Taste Tangy?

Published November 19, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Imagine two bowls of milk. One has been cultured into plain yogurt; the other is still milk. Now imagine freezing both. You could get cold, firm spoonfuls from either bowl, but only one went through the process that makes yogurt taste like yogurt. The classic yogurt tang starts with fermentation before the mix goes anywhere near a freezer.

That’s a fun distinction to bring to the self-serve frozen yogurt counter at Sweet Escape in Memphis. Cold changes the experience of a bite, but it isn’t what turns milk sugar into acid. And acid does more than give yogurt its tart edge: it helps turn a liquid into something that sits on a spoon.

The sugar that helps make yogurt sour

Milk contains a sugar called lactose. When yogurt starter cultures ferment milk, they use lactose and produce lactic acid, as the University of Wisconsin’s Center for Dairy Research explains. The two customary yogurt starters are Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus. If you see S. thermophilus or L. bulgaricus on an ingredient list, those are the shortened names you’re looking at.

The neat part is that the lactose and lactic acid belong to different stages of the story. The first is a sugar the bacteria can use; the second is one of the products of their work. You don’t need to squeeze a lemon into milk to get a sour note. Yogurt cultures can create one from ingredients already in the milk.

That does not mean the cultures use up all the lactose. The dairy center describes lactose as one of the substances that can remain dissolved in the watery part of finished yogurt. Nor should you confuse the lactose that serves as a starting material with sugar added to a dessert for taste. A tangy bite can still be sweet. Sourness is not a way to read the sugars line on a nutrition panel.

Why that same acid changes the texture

Picture the difference between pouring milk and tipping a spoonful of plain yogurt onto a plate. The first spreads quickly; the second can hold together. Neither has to be frozen. What changed?

The milk proteins called caseins normally occur in tiny clusters called micelles. As the cultures produce acid and the pH falls, the conditions that keep those clusters dispersed change. The caseins come together into a network. The dairy center describes how the resulting gel holds water and dissolved components, including lactic acid. The liquid hasn’t turned into ice; much of it is still held inside that protein network.

If a little clear liquid has collected on top of a cup of plain yogurt, you’ve seen a food that doesn’t hold all its liquid equally well. It’s called whey. In dairy processing, how the milk is heated before fermentation can affect how its proteins join up and how readily whey separates. That’s a different question from how firm a spoonful gets in the freezer. One is about a protein gel and the liquid it holds; the other also involves ice.

Two ways to describe acidity

Food scientists don’t rely on a single acidity number. A pH reading tells them about the activity of hydrogen ions in a sample. Another measurement, called titratable acidity, tells them how much base they need to add to reach a specified endpoint. One records a condition at the start of the test; the other records how much it takes to move the sample to a stopping point.

Those readings aren’t interchangeable. In a study of strained yogurt and its whey, the authors describe how milk proteins and dissolved substances such as phosphate and citrate contribute to the titration result alongside the acid made during fermentation. The amount reported as a lactic acid equivalent isn’t the same as an instrument counting only lactic acid molecules. Two dairy mixes can have similar pH readings without requiring the same amount of base in that test.

Neither measurement asks what a person actually tastes. That requires a separate sensory test. For a familiar parallel, think of a sample described as both sweet and sour: the two words can both be true, and they don’t tell you the recipe. That’s why a study can include a lab measurement, a taster’s sourness rating and a taster’s overall preference without asking the same question three times.

What happens when it becomes frozen dessert?

There’s more than one way researchers have combined culturing and freezing. In a frozen yogurt study by Christos Soukoulis and Constantina Tzia, one approach fermented the dessert mix directly. The other blended separately acidified milk with ice cream mix. Either way, the acid was there before freezing. The difference was where it developed and when it joined the rest of the mix.

In that study, fermenting the mix directly improved its viscosity, which describes resistance to flowing, but did not improve the tasters’ acceptance of the finished dessert. The authors reported more favorable texture for the blended approach in their comparison. A mix that flows more slowly before freezing isn’t necessarily the one tasters prefer after freezing. If a frozen dessert shop says “yogurt,” the word alone doesn’t tell you which production route its supplier used.

Here’s another way to separate the stages: take away the fermentation and you take away that source of lactic acid, even if the finished dessert is very cold. Take away the freezing and you can still have a tart, spoonable dairy food, but you haven’t made a frozen dessert. Both steps matter, just for different reasons. More sour doesn’t always mean more yogurt flavor

In a 2000 frozen yogurt study, R. H. Davidson and colleagues compared mixes fermented to two different endpoints. The samples finished at the lower-pH endpoint tasted more acidic in their comparison, but the team didn’t find a corresponding increase in its separate rating for yogurt flavor. The distinction is subtle but useful: a taster can notice more of one part of a flavor without describing the whole bite as more recognizably yogurt-like.

One reason to keep the words separate is that yogurt brings aromas to the spoon as well as a sour taste. In a comparison of traditional and mild yogurts, researchers sampled the air above the yogurt to measure compounds that can reach the nose. They found differences in compounds such as acetaldehyde between the samples. Sampling the air and testing the acidity of the yogurt answer different questions, even when both results describe the same cup.

It’s a distinction you already use when you describe a dessert as vanilla and tart in the same sentence. The first word can tell someone about the aroma; the second tells them about the taste. The two descriptions can fit a single bite without one canceling out the other. Our vanilla aroma story looks at why the senses can be so hard to sort out when you’re enjoying them together.

A different frozen-yogurt experiment with student tasters varied both sugar and acid in its samples. The researchers were asking a preference question, not looking for the most acidic dessert they could make. There’s a nice everyday takeaway in the way they set up the test. If you change the sweetness and tartness together, you can’t use a preference for one sample to tell which change mattered without carefully comparing several combinations.

You can borrow the vocabulary without staging an experiment. When you’re choosing a frozen dessert, “I want something with a little tang” is a different request from “I want something that isn’t very sweet.” Neither tells you how much sugar is in the cup. If you want to get to know a base, try a small bite from your own serving before you mix in the toppings. Then you have a better point of comparison for what the rest of the cup adds.
