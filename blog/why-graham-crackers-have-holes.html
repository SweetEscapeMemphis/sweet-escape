Why Do Graham Crackers Have Holes?

Published November 27, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Hold a graham cracker up to the light. There are little dots all over it and, on many crackers, a long line right down the middle. The line is there to help you snap the cracker. The dots have a different job, one that starts before the cracker goes into the oven. Bakers prick many cracker doughs to give steam a way out and limit how far the dough puffs. That’s called docking.

Once the cracker is baked, the holes are a clue to how it was made. You don’t need to preserve them to make a cheesecake crust. In fact, the moment you crush the cracker, the baking step that needed the holes is already behind you.

What a pinhole does in the oven

Think about a little bubble forming under the surface of a sheet of dough. As the sheet heats, some of its water becomes steam. Depending on how the dough was made, there can be gas from leavening, too. If the gas is trapped and the surface can stretch, it pushes a patch of dough upward as the cracker bakes. A small blister may be welcome. One big pocket that lifts the whole cracker is another matter.

The idea is old enough to turn up in John McCollum’s 1859 cracker-machine patent. He identifies opening a path for steam as the job of the docker. The holes are placed while the dough is still soft, not drilled into a baked cracker.

Venting is not necessarily the whole explanation in a layered dough. The authors of a 2004 cracker patent proposed that pressing at selected spots can also help hold the layers together. They described pressing pieces into the dough to manage the structure in their own design. Think of the difference between a vent and an anchor: the first gives gas a way out, while the second limits where two layers can pull apart. The patent presents anchoring as a theory, not a test result for every brand of graham cracker.

Nor is flat always the goal. A different snack patent gives oyster and soup crackers as examples that can be made with a deliberately pillowed shape by leaving out docking holes. The same document describes docked crackers with a generally flat bottom but blisters on top. The aim can be a cracker that sits flat, not one that’s perfectly smooth.

A hole doesn’t have to look the same all the way through a bake. A 2014 patent application about controlling blisters describes both partial punctures and ones that go through. In one example, a preheated but still pliable piece of dough could partly close around the opening after the pin was withdrawn. If you can’t see daylight through a finished cracker, that alone doesn’t tell you that it was never pricked. The patent is a useful example of what a dough can do, not a description of your particular cracker.

One machine can leave two kinds of marks

There is a small problem to solve after pressing a hole: the raw dough may want to come back up with the pin. McCollum’s patent shows a plate that releases the dough from the teeth so the pieces can continue toward the oven. If you’ve ever had dough cling to a fork, you already know the problem. What matters on a conveyor is that the next piece of dough arrives where the tool expects it to be.

Adam and John Exton’s 1875 machine patent shows a different arrangement. A moving table carried the dough below a roller fitted with points. In their design the roller pressed the pieces into shape and pricked them during the same pass. The neat spacing made by a machine needn’t mean somebody calculated a universal number of holes for every cracker. It means the tool put its marks in a regular place.

A 1959 rotary cutter patent makes the difference between a dotted field and a long line especially clear. It shows bars that score the dough sheet and separate pins that poke inside the outline. In the drawing, the pins point toward the roller’s center rather than all in the same direction, so as each part of the roller meets the sheet, its pins can enter squarely. Scoring shapes a future break; docking makes a set of small punctures. Having them appear at once doesn’t make them interchangeable.

For a contrast you can count, the Baking Industry Research Trust’s cracker guide describes one familiar soda-cracker layout: nine docking holes in three rows, with separate perforation lines for dividing the larger baked sheet. It also describes round snack crackers made with two docking holes. Those examples aren’t measurements of graham crackers. They show why the right question is where and how a particular dough is pricked, not how many holes a cracker is supposed to have.

Should you prick homemade graham crackers?

Check when the recipe tells you to do it. The homemade crackers in Gale Gand and Julia Moskin’s s’mores recipe get fork holes before they go into the oven. In King Arthur Baking’s whole-wheat graham cracker recipe, the sheet spends time in the oven first. The baker marks the rectangles partway through and can choose to prick them for the familiar appearance. Those are different doughs and directions. It’s not a contradiction: the holes can have a structural purpose in one recipe and be optional in another.

If your recipe makes pricking optional, try docking only a few crackers from the same sheet. Keep thickness and baking conditions as similar as you can. Once they’ve cooled, lay them flat and compare the tops and undersides, not just the amount of browning. Then look at where each breaks. You may find that the scoring matters to the snap even if the pinholes make little difference in that batch. That’s more revealing than comparing two different recipes and assigning every difference to the fork.

To see the marks without turning on the oven, compare a whole graham sheet with one already broken along its score. The score is one continuous weak line; the dots have an area of their own. A small surface bubble can sit between them. You’re reading three different traces: a break deliberately encouraged, a hole deliberately placed, and a little pocket that formed while baking.

Why the holes don’t follow the cracker into a crumb crust

A prebaked pastry shell starts as raw dough. King Arthur’s pastry method weighs the dough down for the first part of baking, then uses fork holes to deter bubbles when finishing the empty shell. The weights and holes do different jobs. As in a cracker, the baker is trying to manage a sheet that can lift away from the surface it’s sitting on.

Rossi Anastopoulo’s ice cream pie starts with baked grahams crushed and pressed with butter into a pan. The crust doesn’t take another trip through the oven in this recipe. There’s no intact raw sheet to dock. The crumbs are used to make a base for the filling, but they no longer need the marks the baker put in the original cracker. And in a scoop, small crust pieces are a different arrangement again: you meet them within the dessert rather than as the bottom of each slice.

Quick questions about cracker holes

Are the holes where you’re supposed to break a graham cracker?

The small holes are usually docking marks. A long scored line, when present, is the mark that guides a break. They can be made in the same pass but serve different purposes.

Do homemade graham crackers have to be docked?

Follow the recipe. One published recipe pricks the dough before baking. Another makes the fork holes optional after the dough has already baked partway.

Should you poke holes in a graham cracker crumb crust?

Not by default. A pressed crumb crust starts with crackers that are already baked, not a sheet of raw dough. Follow the crust recipe rather than borrowing a pastry step.
