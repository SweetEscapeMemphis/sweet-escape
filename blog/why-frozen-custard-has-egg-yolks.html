Why Does Frozen Custard Have Egg Yolks?

Published November 23, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Think of a spoon dipped into warm vanilla custard. Run a line down the back and the coating stays put on either side. That little trail tells you what the egg yolks did before the dessert went anywhere near a freezer. Yolks can thicken a custard base as it cooks, and they can change how fat and air are distributed when the base freezes. But simply finding eggs in an ice cream doesn’t make it frozen custard.

That distinction helps with a surprisingly practical question: if you see “French Vanilla,” are you looking at a flavor, a recipe style, or a particular kind of frozen dessert? The answer depends on which words you’re looking at, and where.

First job: thicken the mix while it’s warm

In a stovetop custard, heat makes some of the proteins in the yolk unfold and join up. As they form a loose network, the mixture starts to flow more slowly. The Exploratorium’s custard demonstration shows what happens if the heat goes too far: the network tightens and squeezes out liquid. Instead of a smooth mixture, you can wind up with little curds and watery patches. It’s the same tendency that lets an egg become solid in a skillet, applied to a mix that you’re trying to keep pourable.

King Arthur Baking’s published vanilla frozen custard recipe uses yolks in a dairy base and describes the spoon test. That test answers a texture question: is the liquid thick enough to leave a coating? It doesn’t tell you whether it’s cold enough to churn, and it isn’t a substitute for food-safety guidance. If you’re making ice cream at home, follow a tested recipe and the FDA’s guidance on eggs, especially for any recipe that leaves them raw or undercooked.

Notice what the recipe does when the custard reaches that point. A strainer and a bowl over ice are ready so the custard can come out of the hot pan right away. The strainer can catch bits that have already formed; cooling slows the cooking that would produce more of them. Neither tool does the same job as the ice cream maker. The goal here is a smooth, chilled liquid to put into the machine.

Second job: help shape what happens in the freezer

Thickening a liquid is only one job. Yolk also brings emulsifying substances such as lecithin. An emulsifier has parts that associate with water and parts that associate with fat, so it can sit at their boundary. Milk fat is already dispersed in little droplets in an ice cream mix. In Douglas Goff’s explanation of ice cream emulsifiers, these substances affect the coating on the droplets. That changes how readily some of the fat droplets link up during freezing and whipping.

Here’s the counterintuitive part: you don’t necessarily want the droplets to stay perfectly separate. Some clustering helps form a structure around air bubbles. That’s different from cooking the yolk proteins into a custard, and it’s also different from having so much fat stick together that it becomes butter. Our ice cream and butter comparison takes a closer look at that last distinction. For custard, the point is that one ingredient can do different jobs at different moments.

You don’t need eggs to freeze cream into ice cream. In King Arthur’s side-by-side kitchen tests, the Philadelphia-style version uses milk, cream, sugar and vanilla but no yolks. The author warms part of the dairy to dissolve the sugar, then chills the mixture before freezing. The difference isn’t simply whether a saucepan was used. It’s whether the cook made a starting mixture that thickens with egg.

In the same kitchen comparison, two versions with yolks differed in firmness after a night in the freezer. The softer one used more sugar and a different dairy blend. That comparison can’t isolate the effect of either change, but it makes a point home cooks can put to use: if a recipe isn’t as scoopable as you’d like, the egg count isn’t the only detail to look at. Compare the whole base before deciding which version to try next.

What if you replace the yolk?

In a 2008 French vanilla ice cream study, researchers compared a liquid-yolk version with three alternatives: a modified corn starch, a whey protein concentrate and a soy protein isolate. They held the target amount of incorporated air constant and made three batches of each treatment. Some of the alternatives also called for changes to the dairy or sugar proportions, so this was a comparison of finished formulations, not a test that changed only one molecule at a time.

For one test, about 80 grams of each ice cream went on a mesh screen at room temperature. The researchers weighed what dripped through into a beaker for an hour. Less liquid passed through from the starch and whey versions than from the yolk version by the end. That doesn’t mean all the ice left on the screen was still frozen: the test tells you how much material ran off, not how many crystals survived.

A separate test used a blade to measure the force it took to press into frozen samples. The whey version needed more force than the yolk version; the starch version didn’t show a clear difference from the yolk version on that measure. In a consumer tasting, all three alternatives scored lower on flavor than the yolk batch. The yolk batch also measured more yellow in the study. The recipe that performed well on the screen didn’t automatically perform the same way under the blade or on someone’s spoon.

Even yolks don’t all start with the same shade. In a 2021 poultry feeding experiment, researchers gave groups of hens diets that differed in the type of corn and measured the yolks with a color scale and an instrument. Eggs from hens fed the orange-corn diet had higher color scores and more of certain pigments than eggs from the yellow- and white-corn groups. That study measured eggs, not ice cream, but it gives another reason not to read a batch’s egg content from its shade.

Where the name “frozen custard” draws a line

In the United States, the federal standard for ice cream and frozen custard draws a line using egg yolk solids by weight. That means the part of the yolk other than water, not the weight of the wet yolk you tip into a bowl. For plain frozen custard the threshold is 1.4% of the finished dessert: think 14 grams of yolk solids in a kilogram of finished food, not 14 grams of fresh yolk.

There’s a provision for bulky flavorings: their weight can reduce the required percentage proportionally, with a floor of 1.12% of the finished food. It’s a way to account for a flavored dessert with more in it than the base. The rule also names “French ice cream” and “French custard ice cream” as alternatives to “frozen custard” for products in that category. That’s about the name of the food, not a claim that all products bearing the flavor words “French Vanilla” are frozen custard.

That’s why color and consistency are weak substitutes for a label. A yolk can make a particular batch yellower, as it did in the study, but yellow isn’t part of the definition. Nor do two spoonfuls that feel equally dense necessarily have the same formula. If your question is which dessert sounds good, use the menu description. If your question is what it’s made of, check the ingredient and allergen information for that exact listing.

Two questions worth asking at the freezer

Does all ice cream that contains egg count as frozen custard?

No. The U.S. standard distinguishes frozen custard by the amount of egg yolk solids, with an adjustment for bulky flavorings. Simply listing egg does not tell you the amount of yolk solids.

Is yellow ice cream always made with egg yolks?

No. Yolks can contribute yellow color, but color alone does not tell you whether a product contains egg. Check the exact ingredient and allergen information.

Two French Vanillas on Whitten Road

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, French Vanilla ice cream and French Vanilla frozen yogurt have separate listings. The scoop’s June 2019 reference for ⅔ cup (95 g) declares milk and eggs and notes shared equipment with peanuts, tree nuts, wheat and soy. It doesn’t give an egg-yolk-solids percentage.

The yogurt listing describes a custard-like character. Its published ingredient statement declares milk and doesn’t list egg, but its facility note includes eggs along with peanuts, tree nuts, soy, milk and wheat. It also lists annatto extract and caramel color. The shared name doesn’t make those two recipes or their allergen notes interchangeable.
