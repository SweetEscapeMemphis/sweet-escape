Evaporated or Condensed Milk? Why Ice Cream Cares

Published November 24, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

You’ve got the cream out, the bowl ready, and two nearly matching cans from the baking aisle. One says evaporated milk. The other says sweetened condensed milk. Does it matter which one goes into the ice cream? Yes: both are made by removing water from milk, but the sweetened can is also doing part of the job of the sugar bowl. Use the other can in its place and you’ve changed more than the sweetness.

The names are easy to mix up because evaporating water is one way of concentrating milk. For an ice cream recipe, the useful questions are what was removed, what was added, and which job the recipe expects that ingredient to do.

Start with what’s in the can

The U.S. standard for evaporated milk describes milk with part of its water removed. The standard also calls for a heat process to prevent spoilage, before or after the container is sealed. The one for sweetened condensed milk starts with a mixture of milk and sweeteners before describing the water removal. In both cases the milk is concentrated, but a substantial amount of sweetener is part of the second product’s identity.

Here’s one way to read the difference off the label. In the manufacturer’s U.S. panels checked November 24, 2026, two tablespoons of its sweetened condensed milk list 22 grams of total sugars, including 18 grams of added sugars. The same volume of its regular evaporated milk lists 3 grams of total sugars and 0 grams on the added-sugars line. These are two example labels, not values for every can. And 0 grams of added sugars on the panel doesn’t mean the milk has no sugars. Lactose is already in milk.

Check the units on the front, too. In these examples the condensed milk’s can is marked 14 ounces by weight, and the standard evaporated milk can is marked 12 fluid ounces. The first tells you how heavy the contents are, the second how much space they take up. Two cans with similar-looking numbers aren’t two equal portions. When a recipe gives cups, measure volume; when it gives grams, use a scale.

Concentrating milk isn’t the same as adding sugar. Imagine a cup of milk with a fixed amount of milk protein in it. If you remove water without removing protein, the protein accounts for more of what’s left. You haven’t made more protein. You’ve changed how much water comes along with it. Sweetening does something different: it brings in a new ingredient. That difference matters when you measure the milk for a frozen recipe.

Why an 1856 milk patent sounds backward

In Gail Borden’s 1856 patent, the inventor describes a pan that concentrates milk under reduced pressure. The drawing shows a vessel connected to a pump, not an open pot bubbling away over a stove. As the University of Guelph’s explanation of dairy evaporation puts it, lowering the pressure lowers the boiling temperature. That lets a dairy processor drive off water vapor without needing the high boiling temperature of an open pan.

The surprise is how Borden describes his claim. He calls the starting material sweet milk, but specifies that his concentrated product has no sugar mixed into it. He even acknowledges earlier work that combined concentrated milk with sugar. Read “sweet” as a description of the milk, not as “sweetened.” It’s a nineteenth-century patent for a particular process, not a recipe you’d follow to prepare or store milk at home.

The distinction shows up in an old Heller’s Guide for Ice-Cream Makers, preserved by the Library of Congress. Its dairy chapter discusses plain condensed milk and sweetened condensed milk separately. In the vanilla formulas, the book lists condensed milk alongside cream and regular milk, then lists granulated sugar as a separate ingredient. If you read the old ingredient list as an instruction to buy a modern sweetened can, you’d miss the difference the same book is trying to teach.

Sugar still has work to do after the mix tastes sweet

Douglas Goff’s ice cream technology guide explains why sweetness isn’t the whole story. Dissolved sugars, including the lactose naturally present in milk, lower the temperature at which water starts to freeze. That’s part of why ice cream can contain both ice and a portion of unfrozen liquid at the same serving temperature. Changing the sugar can change how much water is frozen, not just how sweet a spoonful tastes.

If you replace a sweetened can with an equal volume of plain evaporated milk in a recipe designed around the sweetened one, the base can end up less sweet and firmer at the same freezer temperature. The reverse swap also changes the balance. The result depends on the rest of the recipe, because the cans bring different amounts of water and dairy solids as well as sugar. Adding a spoonful of sugar until the mix tastes right doesn’t necessarily put it back where it started.

There’s a second time to think about this difference: after the ice cream has firmed up overnight. A base that seems easy to stir in a bowl isn’t necessarily easy to scoop from the freezer. Before freezing, you’re feeling the liquid mixture. Later, you’re also dealing with ice crystals and the liquid left between them. If you want to compare two recipes, taste them at similar temperatures; otherwise you may be comparing how far they’ve warmed rather than what the recipe changed.

That’s also why milk powder isn’t simply another name for either can. The University of Guelph’s dairy-solids chapter describes powders and concentrates as separate ways to supply the milk components other than fat. A powder brings those solids with much less water than a liquid concentrate. And milk sugar can do something that table sugar in a recipe doesn’t tell you much about: under some conditions, it can form noticeable lactose crystals. Our sandy ice cream story explains how to distinguish that from ice crystals.

Two recipes, two different jobs

In Martha Stewart’s no-churn vanilla recipe, sweetened condensed milk goes into the base and whipped cream brings in air before the mixture goes in the freezer. There’s no separate scoop of granulated sugar in the ingredient list. The can is already supplying the sugar as well as concentrated dairy. “No-churn” doesn’t mean no air was worked in: the cream was whipped first.

The cookbook The Art of Living According to Joe Beef takes a different route. Its base uses evaporated milk along with fresh dairy and powdered milk; it adds sugar and corn syrup separately. The chilled base goes into an ice cream maker. In a churned process, air is worked in while part of the water freezes, rather than folding in cream whipped beforehand.

And one recipe can use both cans without treating them as twins. In Carnation’s no-churn caramel recipe, the ingredients include plain sweetened condensed milk and a separate caramel-flavored evaporated milk. Both go into the whipped-cream mixture. The second can supplies the evaporated dairy and the specified flavor; the first is still part of the recipe. The two cans share a bowl, but each brings something different to it.

Neither recipe settles which style you should enjoy. They answer a pantry question: if your recipe was designed around the sweetness in the can, start with the sweetened can. If it expects you to add its sugar separately, get the dairy ingredient it calls for. Read the whole ingredient list before deciding that the names are close enough.

Two questions from the baking aisle

Is evaporated milk the same as sweetened condensed milk?

No. Both are concentrated milk products, but sweetened condensed milk includes a substantial amount of added sweetener as part of the product.

Can I swap the two cans in no-churn ice cream?

Not as a straight swap in a recipe designed for sweetened condensed milk. The change affects the sugar, water and dairy solids in the mix, which can change the texture as well as the taste.

A different place for sweetness in Memphis

Sweetness can go throughout a base, as in the recipes above, or show up in a separate part of the dessert. Sweet Escape’s Roasted Banana Dulce De Leche listing describes roasted banana gelato woven with thick Argentine dulce de leche. That tells you how the two elements are presented, not which canned milk, if any, was used to make them.
