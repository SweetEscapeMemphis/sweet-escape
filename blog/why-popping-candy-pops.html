Why Does Popping Candy Pop? A Dessert Science Fact

Published November 4, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Some dessert surprises arrive with a sound. Popping candy can look like a scattering of ordinary hard-candy pieces, then start crackling as it gets wet. The surprising ingredient is not a miniature firework or a reaction that needs your tongue. It is gas already trapped inside the sweet.

At Sweet Escape in Memphis, we like the questions that make a familiar dessert more interesting. This one takes us from a tiny candy particle to industrial food design, sound measurement, and even glacier ice. Along the way, it explains why adding a popping ingredient to ice cream can be trickier than it looks.

This is a general dessert-science story, not an announcement of a popping-candy topping at Sweet Escape. Our connection is the pleasure of understanding what different dessert components actually do—not guessing at ingredients from a playful name.

A solid sweet can carry pockets of gas

The American Chemical Society’s Chemistry Goes Pop activity explains the basic structure: a sugar candy surrounds tiny bubbles of pressurized carbon dioxide. When water dissolves the surrounding sugar, the gas escapes and makes the familiar popping noise.

That means the important action began before the package was opened. The gas is not being created from nothing in your mouth. Moisture provides a way to open the little sugar containers that were already holding it.

Imagine many tiny sealed rooms rather than one solid block. Removing their walls lets out what was inside. It is only an analogy—the pieces are not neatly built boxes—but it captures why a candy can look quite ordinary while carrying an unusual structure.

The idea appears in a 1961 patent

An early primary record makes that structure unusually concrete. U.S. Patent 3,012,893, issued December 12, 1961, names Leon Kremzner and William A. Mitchell and describes enclosing gas within a solid sugar matrix.

The document explains introducing gas into a molten sugar material and cooling it while maintaining pressure. The resulting solid can hold gas until contact with liquid releases it. This is a broad description of the invention, not a statement that every current candy uses one identical recipe or machine.

The old document is interesting because it treats a confection as a storage material. Something associated with sweetness becomes a way to contain gas. That is a clever change in perspective: the sugar has a structural job as well as a flavor role.

It also shows why the patent date should stay attached to what it actually documents. A patent establishes a dated technical disclosure; it does not, by itself, establish when a brand first appeared on every shop shelf.

Releasing gas is different from making it

A pop or a fizz does not identify one single mechanism. In the ACS Fizz Race activity, citric acid and sodium bicarbonate dissolve in water and react to form carbon dioxide. There, gas is a product of the reaction.

With popping candy, the central explanation is different: stored gas escapes as its surrounding material dissolves. Two things can produce bubbles without having the same internal design. Seeing a bubble is the beginning of the question, not the complete answer.

That distinction is useful well beyond candy. Ask whether gas was stored beforehand, dissolved in a liquid, or newly generated by reacting ingredients. You do not need to memorize an equation to notice that these are different possibilities. A shared sound does not make them interchangeable processes.

Why ice cream creates a timing problem

The frozen-dessert connection is documented directly in a 2019 published Korean patent application for an ice-cream topping. Its stated problem is that moisture from ice cream can reach carbonated candy and release its gas before the dessert is eaten.

The application describes trying to improve moisture resistance and delay that release. Its existence is evidence of a design problem people have worked on, not independent proof that a particular coating succeeds in every dessert. Nor does it identify anything about Sweet Escape’s ingredients or suppliers.

For someone thinking about a dessert at home, the useful takeaway is timing. A component that reacts to contact with moisture is not necessarily unchanged after sitting against a frozen base. The word frozen does not answer every question about how two ingredients interact.

If using a commercially packaged popping ingredient, follow its own directions for the intended use. Do not assume that a product meant to be eaten directly will perform identically after being mixed into a dessert and stored.

A coating can be part of the engineering

A separate 1981 patent on coated gasified candy describes dispersing candy pieces in fat-containing materials, including chocolate, and optionally adding an outer covering. Its discussion connects protection from moisture with retaining the gas inside.

In that design, the coating is not merely decoration. It separates the candy from an environment that could soften it and let gas escape. The document also describes the coating gradually exposing the candy to moisture during eating.

This helps explain why the same general ingredient can need different treatment in different foods. Designing a dry sweet and designing a combination of components are not necessarily the same task. Something surrounding a candy particle may have an important job even when it looks like a finishing touch.

These are examples from a technical disclosure, not home coating instructions or universal shelf-life promises. Industrial work involving hot sugar and pressure is not a kitchen experiment to improvise. The interesting part for a dessert lover is the function of the layers.

Even the shape presents an engineering puzzle

Why does this candy often come in uneven little pieces? A 1981 patent on pressurized deposit molding describes an earlier manufacturing process in which returning solidified carbonated candy to atmospheric pressure fractures it into assorted granules. Sieving then separates smaller fragments from larger pieces.

That background makes the patent’s proposed alternative easier to appreciate. It describes putting the gas-containing candy material into shaped molds on a moving belt, keeping the molding system under pressure while the pieces solidify, and using a pressure-lock arrangement to remove the candy.

In other words, making a regular shape is not simply a matter of finding a prettier mold. The process also has to account for gas inside the material and what happens as conditions change. A shape that seems effortless on the outside may represent a separate manufacturing problem.

This historical example does not tell us how every candy on sale today is formed. It does show how one simple snack can raise several distinct questions: how to trap the gas, how to keep it there, how to shape the solid, and when to let the gas escape.

Scientists can listen to the candy

A crackle can also become a measurement. In its 2019 description of acoustic testing, instrument maker Stable Micro Systems discusses recording popping candy after exposure to water and analyzing the resulting sound.

The method counts sound peaks over intervals and considers measures such as sound-pressure level and estimated sound energy. In the two products discussed, looking at the number of peaks helped distinguish behavior that a less processed sound-pressure measurement did not clearly separate.

The company also notes variation in gas-bubble and granule sizes, which can complicate repeatability. That is an important limitation. One loud pop from one piece does not tell you everything about a whole batch.

There is a nice lesson here for curious eaters: “louder,” “more pops,” and “longer-lasting” are different descriptions. A sound can be measured in several ways, just as a dessert can be described by more than one texture word. You first have to decide which question you are asking.

A surprising comparison: crackling glacier ice

The ACS draws a memorable parallel in Crackling of an Ice Core. It describes tidewater glacier ice making sizzling and hissing sounds as trapped air is released while the ice thaws in seawater.

The classroom activity uses dissolving popping candy as a model for gas release. But the differences matter: glacier ice melts, the sugar candy dissolves, and the gases do not have identical origins or compositions. A model helps us notice a shared feature without making the two materials the same.

According to the activity, gases captured in glacier ice can provide clues about the atmosphere when they were trapped. The candy in your cupboard is not an ancient climate sample. It is simply an unexpectedly useful way to picture a solid releasing enclosed gas.

A better question to bring to your next dessert

You do not need a popping topping to enjoy the broader idea. At Sweet Escape, the hand-dipped catalog, gelato catalog, and specialty-dessert pages help identify different formats and documented components. Start with what the exact listing actually says.

Then ask a more precise preference question. Are you looking for a straightforward frozen base, a named inclusion, or a dessert assembled from several components? That question is more useful than expecting every interesting name to promise a particular crunch, pop, or preparation method.

For ingredients, nutrition, or allergies, check the exact product information and ask about the current container label, facility/shared-equipment notes, handling, and cross-contact. A science explanation is not a complete ingredient statement or a dietary-safety guarantee.
