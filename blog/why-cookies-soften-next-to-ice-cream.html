The science of a cookie-and-scoop combination

Why Do Cookies Soften Next to Ice Cream?

Published October 23, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A cookie and a scoop seem like a simple partnership. One brings the baked part; the other brings the frozen part. Put them together, though, and you have a surprisingly interesting food-science question: why can a cookie that started crisp lose its snap beside ice cream?

Here is a fun fact for your next dessert conversation at Sweet Escape in Memphis: moisture can move between different parts of a food. The important clue is not just which part contains more water. Scientists also consider something called water activity.

That idea connects cookie sandwiches, filled biscuits, and ice-cream wafers. It explains a possible texture change without requiring a puddle of melted ice cream—and without making every cookie behave the same way.

Two different questions about water

Moisture content asks how much water a food contains. Water activity asks a different question about that water’s energy state. The two measurements are related, but they are not interchangeable.

AQUALAB’s water-activity introduction defines water activity using the vapor pressure of water in a substance compared with pure water at the same temperature. Values run on a scale up to one. You do not need a meter at the dessert counter; the useful point is that the number is not a percentage of water.

Imagine two recipe cards that list the same amount of water but different other ingredients. That shared amount alone would not establish equal water activity. A complete description needs more than “this one looks wet.”

For a dessert lover, this distinction clears up a common shortcut. Measuring an ingredient’s water quantity and predicting what its water will do are separate tasks. A food can look quite solid while still participating in moisture exchange.

Moisture follows a difference, not a menu category

When components have different water activities, moisture can move from the higher-water-activity part toward the lower-water-activity part. Novasina’s bakery explanation describes this issue in foods assembled from multiple components, such as a cake and its filling.

This is why putting a crisp baked component beside a moister component can change the first component’s texture. It is also why “cookie” is not enough information to calculate the outcome. The actual components and their properties matter.

Think of a sandwich as a meeting between foods, not merely a stack of ingredients. Each part starts with its own characteristics. Once they are combined, the boundary between them becomes part of the explanation.

Importantly, moisture migration does not mean the components must end up containing equal percentages of water. Novasina notes that components can have matching water activity despite different moisture contents. Equal activity and equal quantity are different destinations.

How water can take away the snap

Getting water into a crisp baked structure can change how that structure bends or breaks. Food scientists use the term plasticization for water’s ability to make parts of a food structure more mobile and less rigid.

In a study of commercial wafers, researchers examined water uptake, physical state, and bending behavior. The wafers became more deformable as their moisture increased. Under the study’s conditions, the material could change from a glassy state toward a rubbery one.

“Glassy” does not mean the wafer contains glass. It describes a physical state of the material. The everyday contrast is easier to picture: something that fractures sharply versus something that gives or bends.

The study also shows why it is unwise to borrow one exact moisture threshold for every dessert. Its measurements concerned particular wafers under specified conditions, not all cookies in all ice creams. The general lesson is that water can change structure, not that one laboratory number tells you when your sandwich will soften.

Even one cookie is not one uniform material

A cookie has an outside and an inside, and research can investigate those parts separately. That is more revealing than treating the whole cookie as a single identical substance from edge to center.

In a 2021 cookie study by Sogabe and colleagues, researchers compared starch-based and conventional cookies, including their crust and crumb. For both tested types, the crust components resisted water’s plasticizing effect more than the crumb components did.

The investigators also measured differences between the formulations. Their work is a useful reminder that cookie structure, composition, and the particular region being tested can all matter to the answer.

It does not establish how Sweet Escape’s cookies behave, and it does not rank every cookie recipe. Instead, it explains why a broad claim such as “all cookie sandwiches soften in exactly the same way” asks the evidence to do too much. Sometimes the interesting detail is inside the cookie, not just between the cookies.

But isn’t everything frozen?

Frozen storage does not make moisture migration irrelevant. Scientists study this question specifically in frozen desserts, rather than assuming the freezer ends the story.

Paschalia Mavrou’s University of Surrey doctoral research investigated temperature-dependent moisture transport, including below-zero conditions. The work used experimental information and a mathematical model to assess moisture movement in the wafer of a manufactured ice-cream cone over storage time.

A particularly useful distinction in the thesis abstract is that a wafer could be predicted to lose crispness without reaching the study’s threshold for being considered soggy. Those words describe different degrees of texture change, not one interchangeable condition.

This is research about a modeled manufactured cone, not a serving-time prediction for a local cookie sandwich. Still, it answers the freezer objection: frozen-dessert texture can involve movement and change over time. “Frozen” is not the same as “nothing can happen.”

Why some frozen desserts have a chocolate barrier

A coating can have a structural job as well as a place in the flavor description. AQUALAB’s technical guide to snack foods gives chocolate coating inside an ice-cream cone as an example of an edible barrier used to control moisture migration.

The same guide discusses other formulation and packaging approaches, including bringing components closer in water activity. Manufacturers therefore have more than one possible way to address a texture problem.

That does not mean every coating is a perfect shield or that every crisp inclusion uses one. More importantly, it does not tell us what any unnamed product contains. A useful question is “does this component have a documented coating?” rather than assuming every chocolate-looking piece has the same construction.

Sweet Escape does not publish a moisture-barrier specification for its cookie sandwich. The coating example explains a general food-design strategy; it is not a claim about the shop’s cookies.

What this means when you choose a dessert

Start with your preference, not a rule that crisp is always better than soft. You might want a cookie that breaks with a snap, or you might prefer a yielding outside around a frozen center. Those are different goals, and neither is a scientific requirement for enjoying dessert.

If texture matters to your choice, ask a direct question: “How would you describe the cookie texture in this specialty?” That is more useful than trying to predict it from the flavor name or a photograph. Ask about the actual item rather than expecting a general article to describe a particular batch.

Also separate the cookie question from the ice-cream question. “Does the cookie bend?” and “Is the scoop firm?” describe different components. If you are comparing two desserts, name the component you mean. It makes your preference easier to explain without turning an enjoyable decision into a laboratory test.

For example, saying “I like a firm center but a softer cookie” gives someone two clear pieces of information. Saying “I want a crunchy dessert” leaves open whether you mean the outside, an inclusion, or something alongside the scoop. You do not need technical vocabulary to be precise.

Finally, keep the scientific explanation in its proper role. It can tell you why moisture exchange deserves attention, but it cannot choose your favorite bite. Use the fun fact to sharpen a question, then let your own preferences guide the choice.
