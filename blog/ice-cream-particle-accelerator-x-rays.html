A surprisingly big way to study a tiny frozen world

Scientists Used a Particle Accelerator to Study Ice Cream

Published November 11, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A particle accelerator sounds like an unlikely place to take ice cream. Yet researchers really brought frozen samples to Diamond Light Source, the United Kingdom’s national synchrotron facility, to examine what was happening inside them. The experiment was about seeing a dessert’s hidden structure, not inventing an outlandish flavor.

For anyone enjoying an ice-cream conversation in Memphis, that is a fun fact worth keeping: an ordinary frozen treat can pose questions sophisticated enough for a major research facility. The interesting part is why scientists needed that equipment—and what the images could reveal that an ordinary photograph could not.

The giant machine supplies the light

According to Diamond’s explanation of its machine, electrons accelerate to nearly the speed of light before circulating in a storage ring. Magnets bend their path, and the electrons emit light that can be directed toward experimental stations called beamlines.

Some of that light is X-rays. The sample sits at an experimental station where the beam interacts with it; the ice cream is not racing around the electron ring. Think of the accelerator as the source of a specialized scientific light, rather than a carnival ride for a scoop.

The distinction makes the headline less mysterious without making it less impressive. The researchers needed a way to investigate a difficult material. Diamond supplies intense light and instruments for that investigation. Its beamlines also support other kinds of experiments, so saying that a study used a synchrotron does not, by itself, explain which measurement was made.

Why a photograph of the surface is not enough

Diamond’s 2018 account of the ice-cream work describes the problem with earlier microscopy approaches: they supplied two-dimensional views of a surface or a cut through a sample. Researchers wanted to investigate the interior in three dimensions.

A simple mental example helps. Imagine looking at one flat section through a winding tunnel. You might see several separate openings without knowing that they connect elsewhere. The unseen route matters if the question concerns a network rather than just the diameter of one opening.

That is the kind of additional information a three-dimensional method can provide. Instead of asking only what a particular section looks like, scientists can ask how features extend through a volume. The appeal is not a prettier dessert photograph. It is a more complete view of an internal arrangement that a single slice can leave ambiguous.

Many flat views become a three-dimensional picture

Diamond defines X-ray tomography as reconstructing a three-dimensional image from two-dimensional projections taken at different orientations. It is a way to work backward from many views to the volume that produced them.

Imagine photographing a complicated object from numerous directions. More angles give you information missing from one view. Tomography goes beyond that everyday analogy: the projections come from X-rays passing through the sample, and reconstruction turns the measurements into an internal image.

The facility explains that synchrotron light offers useful intensity, coherence, and control of X-ray energy for this work. Those properties help researchers obtain contrast and detail. There is still a distinction between collecting projections and interpreting the finished reconstruction; the machine does not simply hand over a complete scientific explanation.

For a reader, the word “tomography” is the key to the story. It tells you that the aim is a reconstructed volume, not merely a magnified snapshot of the outside.

The trick also involves contrast, not just magnification

Seeing small features requires distinguishing them from their surroundings. Diamond’s phase-contrast imaging guide explains that materials with different refractive indices change the phase of passing X-rays differently. With a suitable detector distance, interference can enhance image contrast.

You do not need the mathematics to appreciate the practical point: making an image larger does not automatically make every boundary easier to recognize. The experiment needs useful differences in the recorded signal.

Here, “phase contrast” names an imaging effect involving waves. It should not be confused with calling ice, air, and liquid different material phases. The same word appears in two related scientific conversations, but it is doing a different job in each.

A 2017 study found an interconnected air story

In Guo and colleagues’ 2017 RSC Advances paper, researchers combined synchrotron tomography with cryogenic scanning electron microscopy. They examined samples after zero, seven, and fourteen temperature cycles between minus 15 and minus 5 degrees Celsius.

In those samples, ice-crystal growth nearly stopped after seven cycles as crystals approached the dimensions of the walls between air cells. The air cells continued changing, forming interconnected channels. The researchers identified a role for the air structure in limiting further crystal growth.

That is more specific than saying that warming changes ice cream. Different features did not all keep growing in the same way. Their arrangement helped influence what could happen next.

It also shows why the earlier tunnel analogy matters. Separate-looking openings in a flat view may belong to a larger connected structure. Three-dimensional information gave the team a way to examine that connectivity rather than assuming the sample consisted of isolated round bubbles.

Adding time gives the experiment its fourth dimension

A 2018 Journal of Food Engineering study by Guo and colleagues used time-resolved synchrotron tomography during temperature cycling. Here, “4D” means three spatial dimensions plus time—not an additional direction hidden inside the dessert.

The study associated changes in ice-crystal size and shape with melting and recrystallization. Changes in air-cell size and connectivity were dominated by cells joining together. It also found interactions among ice crystals, air structures, and the surrounding unfrozen matrix.

A series of observations can therefore answer a different question from one final scan. A final structure shows where the experiment ended; observations through time help reveal how it developed.

For a familiar comparison, a photograph of an empty plate tells you little about the order in which dessert disappeared. A sequence contains information about events. In this research, the sequence was used to investigate microscopic changes under controlled conditions.

The actual sample was much smaller than a scoop

Mo and colleagues’ October 2018 Materials paper describes ice-cream mixture held in tubes with a three-millimeter internal diameter on a specially designed cold stage. In one scanning arrangement, the team collected 720 projections over a half-turn.

The paper also explains an important limitation: during rapid cooling, the scans could not keep pace with the temperature change, so the researchers acquired observations at the start and end rather than a continuous sequence. They likewise did not record scans during the rapid heating stage.

That detail makes the work more interesting, not less. A scientific movie has a sampling rate. If a change happens faster than the instrument can record it, there will be gaps.

The researchers also processed, labeled, and visually checked the reconstructed volumes. The colorful structures shown in scientific illustrations require analysis; they are not simply the natural colors of the dessert seen through an exceptionally powerful camera.

Another facility had to keep its tiny sample cold, too

A separate 2021 paper by Yoneyama and colleagues describes advanced imaging at Japan’s SAGA Light Source. Its low-temperature demonstration included ice cream inside a two-millimeter-diameter tube.

Cold nitrogen gas flowed over the sample during measurement. The reported ice-cream scan collected 1,000 projections over a full rotation, taking 1,000 seconds in total. The images revealed air bubbles and ice.

The lesson is that generating X-rays is only part of the challenge. Researchers also need a sample environment suited to the thing being measured. For a frozen dessert, maintaining cold conditions during a lengthy observation is part of making the experiment work.

This was laboratory imaging, not a serving technique. The gas system and X-ray equipment described in the paper are specialized research apparatus, not a suggested kitchen experiment or a claim about how Sweet Escape prepares its desserts.

One size number needs a little context

Here is an imaginary example, not a result from these experiments. Suppose a perfectly spherical feature has a diameter of ten micrometers, and another has a diameter of twenty. The second is twice as wide, but its volume is eight times larger, because a sphere’s volume scales with the cube of its diameter.

Now imagine counting ten of each. Equal counts would not mean equal total volumes. That is why a useful question about an image is, “What exactly was measured?” Width, volume, number, and connectivity describe different aspects of a structure.

Real features need not be perfect spheres. The simple arithmetic is only a reminder to read the measurement alongside the picture. A visually striking reconstruction and a carefully defined quantity do different jobs; together, they make the research easier to understand.
