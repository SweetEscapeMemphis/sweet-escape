A fruit behind the coffee flavor

Coffee Beans Are Seeds Inside a Fruit

Published November 6, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

The coffee connection in your dessert has a fruit story behind it. What we call a coffee bean is actually a seed inside a coffee fruit, commonly called a cherry. That is a fun thought to bring to Sweet Escape in Memphis: before coffee becomes a familiar roasted flavor, it begins as part of a flowering plant.

The seed-to-dessert journey involves more than changing a name. There are fruit layers to remove, different processing routes, and a roast that changes the seed’s chemistry. There is even a rounded, single-seed variation with its own nickname. Here is how those pieces fit together, without needing to become a coffee expert.

First come flowers, then the coffee cherries

The Royal Horticultural Society’s arabica profile describes an evergreen shrub or tree with glossy leaves and small, fragrant white flowers. Those flowers are followed by green fruits that ripen over several months into red cherries.

That picture is quite different from a bag of brown beans. The familiar object is hidden inside a fleshy fruit growing on a branch. The plant has already gone through flowering and fruit development before anyone begins preparing the seeds for roasting.

Indiana University’s greenhouse guide says each coffee cherry usually contains two seeds. It identifies the plant as Coffea arabica, in the family Rubiaceae. “Bean” is the everyday name for the seed in this food context.

If you look at the university’s plant photograph, start with the branch and leaves, then find the fruit. It is a simple way to reconnect an ingredient with the living plant that produced it.

Sometimes one seed grows instead of two

Two seeds are the usual arrangement, not an absolute rule. In an explanation of how peaberries form, Starbucks educator Lincoln Bechard describes the variation called a peaberry: only one seed develops inside the cherry.

With two seeds growing side by side, their facing surfaces become flattened. A single seed has room to develop a rounder shape instead. The result is a different outline, not an entirely different coffee species.

The nickname is a handy clue when you are looking at coffee. A peaberry is not a separate little fruit added to a bag of beans. It is a seed whose development took a different route inside the fruit.

There is no need to turn that shape difference into a claim that one bean must taste better. For this fun fact, the interesting part is structural: the number of developing seeds helps explain the shape of the object you eventually recognize as coffee.

The seed comes wrapped in several layers

A coffee cherry is not just a loose seed inside a thin skin. The Royal Society of Chemistry’s chapter on coffee plants describes outer fruit tissues and a seed made up of an embryo, endosperm, and silverskin.

Working inward, there is an outer skin, fleshy material often discussed as pulp and mucilage, and a covering called parchment. The thin silverskin belongs with the seed. These names describe different layers, even though they may all disappear from view by the time you see roasted coffee.

The endosperm is especially interesting: it is the reserve tissue that supports early plant growth after germination. The embryo is much smaller. So the substantial object called a bean is not simply a tiny plant folded into a brown shell.

Think of the anatomy as a set of nested parts rather than a vocabulary quiz. Skin is not seed, parchment is not roasted coffee, and the fruit surrounding the seed has its own structure. Those distinctions explain why processing takes several steps instead of one quick peel.

“Washed” and “dry processed” describe different routes

The Uganda Coffee Development Authority’s processing guide explains two main ways of separating coffee seeds from their coverings. In wet processing, pulp and mucilage are removed, the coffee is washed and dried in its parchment, and the remaining covering is later removed by hulling.

In dry processing, the cherries are dried first, then the dried outer coverings are removed mechanically. Both routes work toward exposing the coffee seeds, but the order of drying and removal differs.

That is what makes a processing term useful. It describes part of the ingredient’s journey, rather than merely supplying a decorative word on a coffee bag. “Dry processed” does not mean a dried beverage, and “washed” is not a brewing instruction.

These are general explanations, not a farm-to-shop account for Sweet Escape’s coffee desserts. Their value is that they let you ask a more precise question when learning about coffee: which part was removed, and at what stage? The fruit anatomy suddenly has a practical purpose.

Roasting changes the seed, not just its temperature

Once the seeds have been prepared, roasting creates another transformation. The American Chemical Society’s coffee-chemistry overview describes interactions among carbohydrates, proteins, and fats during roasting. Roast temperature and time affect the final chemical and flavor profile.

One important group of reactions is Maillard chemistry, involving sugars and amino-containing compounds. Among its products are brown compounds called melanoidins. This is more than a raw ingredient being warmed until it happens to look darker.

Roasting forms and changes compounds involved in coffee’s aroma and flavor. That helps explain why a photograph of a coffee plant does not immediately suggest the experience of opening a bag of roasted beans.

For a dessert lover, the useful distinction is between an ingredient’s biological identity and its preparation. Calling coffee a seed tells you what part of the plant it is. Describing a roast tells you something about what has happened to it afterward. Both can be true at once, and neither alone is a complete recipe for a finished dessert.

Brewing is another step, not the same as roasting

Roasting changes the solid seed; brewing brings compounds from roasted, ground coffee into water. A 2020 study by Niny Z. Rao, Megan Fuller, and Meghan D. Grim examined how brewing temperature and roast level interacted in coffees prepared from one Colombian arabica source.

The researchers compared hot and cold brewing across light, medium, and dark roasts. They measured chemical and physical properties, including dissolved solids and browned compounds. Their results showed that cold water did not extract every measured component as effectively as hot water under the tested conditions.

That is not a ranking of every hot and cold coffee, and it is not a test of Sweet Escape gelato. It shows why two preparation questions should stay separate: how was the seed roasted, and how were compounds extracted into the drink?

A coffee-based frozen dessert adds its own formulation question after that. Knowing the word “coffee” does not establish a particular roast, extraction method, concentration, or finished serving’s composition. The plant story explains the starting material, not every later choice.

A small observation for your next coffee conversation

Try following the ingredient backward in your imagination: dessert description, coffee preparation, roasted seed, processed seed, fruit, flower. Each step answers a different question about the same ingredient.

You can also compare the plant photographs with a picture of roasted coffee. Which visible parts remain recognizable? Which were removed? Which changed during preparation? There is no tasting competition or special equipment required to enjoy that comparison.

That is the pleasure of a food-origin fact. A familiar menu word becomes a doorway into plant growth, careful processing, and chemistry. You can still choose dessert for the simple reason that it sounds appealing; now you have something interesting to talk about while you do.
