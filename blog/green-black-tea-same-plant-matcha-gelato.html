A tea-plant fun fact behind a frozen-dessert name

Green and Black Tea Can Come From the Same Plant

Published November 12, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A green-tea dessert and a cup of black tea may sound like distant relatives. Here is the fun fact: their tea can begin with the same plant. Green, oolong, and black tea are not simply leaves from three different, conveniently color-coded bushes. What happens around harvest and during processing helps create those different tea styles.

That makes Sweet Escape’s Green Tea gelato listing a good starting point for a little ingredient curiosity in Memphis. Its published description names shade-grown Japanese matcha. Follow that detail backward and you find a leafy plant, decisions about light and heat, and a powder that means something more specific than “anything green.”

One tea species, many possible directions

Kew’s tea-plant profile identifies Camellia sinensis as the species behind green and black tea. It describes an evergreen shrub that can grow much taller when it is not kept pruned for cultivation. The tea we recognize is a prepared product of its leaves, not a plant that naturally grows little black-tea bags.

Kew also distinguishes Chinese and Assam tea varieties. Sharing a species therefore does not mean that every tea comes from an identical cultivar, growing place, or harvest. The botanical relationship is the starting point, not a claim that the finished products should all be interchangeable.

Think of the plant name as an introduction. To understand what ends up in a cup or a dessert ingredient, you still need to ask what happened to the leaves.

The leaves can take different processing paths

Washington State University’s tea-processing guide makes the central point especially clearly: different tea types can be made from leaves of the same plant. Its examples include green, white, oolong, and black teas.

For green tea, heat treatment happens early, limiting oxidation. Oolong involves partial oxidation followed by heat treatment once the desired stage has been reached. Black tea follows a more extensively oxidized route. Those are broad processing distinctions, not a promise that every producer uses precisely the same sequence or equipment.

This is why “green tea” does not mean an unfinished black tea that someone forgot to finish making. It is a deliberately different destination. Holding back a particular transformation can be just as purposeful as encouraging it.

These steps describe tea production, not instructions for preparing Sweet Escape’s gelato. The finished dessert has its own product information.

Rolling changes more than the shape of a leaf

Tamil Nadu Agricultural University’s processing account explains that rolling can break leaf-cell walls and expose their contents to oxygen. Before that, withering reduces moisture and conditions leaves for subsequent handling.

So rolling is not only a way to make attractive twists. It also changes how the material inside the leaf encounters its surroundings. The university describes the traditional “fermentation” stage for black and oolong teas as an oxidation process involving tea enzymes and oxygen.

That wording matters. In this context, “fermentation” does not mean the same thing as explaining a cultured yogurt through its microorganisms. Tea terminology has a history, and the familiar word can hide a different mechanism.

Heat and drying then help control the process and produce the prepared tea. A leaf’s route through these stages helps explain how one botanical starting point can lead to markedly different-looking products.

Matcha is a particular powdered tea

The Japan Matcha Association describes matcha as powdered tencha. In its account, fresh leaves are steamed, dried without the rubbing step, and milled. That is more specific than taking any finished green tea and crushing it.

The name “tencha” is useful because it identifies the tea prepared for that powdered destination. “Matcha” tells us about a particular leaf product, not just the color of a drink or a scoop.

It also explains a familiar contrast between preparations. A leaf infusion and a bowl made by whisking tea powder are not identical formats: in the latter, the finely ground leaf material is part of what is being mixed into the preparation.

That distinction is helpful when reading dessert descriptions. If a listing explicitly says matcha, you have more information than a general green-tea name supplies. It still does not tell you the amount used or the complete dessert formula.

Growing in shade is part of the story, too

A 2020 study by Yamashita and colleagues investigated tea plants exposed to repeated shade cultivation. The researchers examined immature plants under controlled conditions and also assessed changes in field-grown tea.

Their work found reduced photosynthetic ability and changes in stored carbohydrate resources under repeated shading. Overstressed plants in the field also showed changes including warmer canopies in midsummer. These were observations under the study’s treatments, not a rule for every shaded tea garden.

It is a useful reminder that “shade-grown” describes an agricultural practice with real plant responses. It is not a magic switch that can be turned farther and farther without consequences.

For a dessert customer, appreciating that care does not require becoming a tea farmer. It simply makes the phrase on a product description more meaningful: the story begins before the leaf reaches a mill or a kitchen.

Even the drying stage can change the green

Mao and colleagues’ 2024 tencha study examined different drying-temperature arrangements using sensory evaluation, color measurements, and chemical analysis. It identified chlorophylls and their derivatives as important contributors to the color differences.

In the tested conditions, staged drying arrangements—including a short higher-temperature period followed by lower-temperature drying—could preserve green color better than a single-temperature approach. The finding concerns those experiments, not a universal recipe for every tea.

This adds another layer to the same-plant fact. A tea’s appearance can reflect choices made after harvesting as well as conditions in the garden. Keeping a product green can involve careful processing rather than an absence of processing.

The researchers were working with tencha, the tea material prepared for matcha, rather than measuring the color of a finished frozen dessert. That is an important stage in the leaf-to-powder journey: changes before milling can help shape the ingredient that comes afterward.

Processing matters, but the cultivar still matters

A 2022 study by Fang and colleagues compared green, black, and freeze-dried samples from five tea cultivars. The researchers split harvested leaves among the different preparations rather than simply buying unrelated teas and assuming the differences came only from processing.

Among their dry-tea samples, only two cultivars produced the floral scents described in the study. The team measured volatile compounds and found an important role for cultivar in that floral-aroma story.

That prevents the fun fact from becoming an oversimplification. Processing can take leaves in different directions, while the plant material itself still contributes to the result. Neither explanation cancels the other.

It also means that a research description of a particular dry tea should not become a promised tasting note for every green-tea dessert. The study’s samples were not Sweet Escape products, and a named tea category is not a complete sensory fingerprint.

Japanese green tea is not one single format

JETRO’s Japanese green-tea guide places matcha alongside styles such as sencha, gyokuro, hojicha, and genmaicha. Its matcha account describes shaded leaves that are steamed, dried, and stone-milled.

The same guide identifies hojicha as a roasted tea and genmaicha as a preparation containing roasted brown rice. Those examples show why “Japanese green tea” is a broad starting point rather than one fixed format or ingredient combination.

Roasting, shading, milling, and adding another documented component are different kinds of decisions. When a name catches your attention, asking which of those details is actually stated is more useful than guessing from color alone.
