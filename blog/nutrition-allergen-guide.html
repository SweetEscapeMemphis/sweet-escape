Reading the details

How to Read Sweet Escape's Nutrition and Allergen Information

Published September 6, 2026 · Updated September 29, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

A flavor name can help you find a dessert you like. It cannot tell you everything that goes into it. At Sweet Escape in Memphis, our product pages bring together published nutrition details, ingredient statements, and allergen notes so you can begin asking the right questions before you order.

The useful part is knowing how those details fit together. A nutrition panel describes a stated serving. An ingredient list describes the product's components. An allergen declaration and a facility caution answer different questions. This guide shows you where to look, using examples from our catalogs without treating online information as a substitute for current labels or a discussion about handling in the shop.

Find the exact product, not just a familiar name

Begin with the category you are considering: hand-dipped scoop products, frozen yogurt, or gelato and sorbetto. The complete item directory also gives you a way to find documented products by name. Keep the category attached to the name while you compare.

For example, French Vanilla appears in both the scoop and yogurt records. Those records are not interchangeable. The scoop record declares milk and eggs; the yogurt record marks milk “Yes” and egg “No,” alongside a separate facility caution. Reading one does not establish the ingredients or allergen information for the other. The point is to identify the exact item, not use this comparison as a safety recommendation.

A similar distinction applies to a finished specialty. Cookie Dough Base includes a baked component and a scoop. Looking up Cookie Dough ice cream alone would not tell you the contents of that entire dessert. Write down the full item you mean before comparing its details.

Start a nutrition comparison with the serving line

Before comparing calories or another nutrient, read the amount the numbers describe. The FDA's serving-size guide explains that nutrition information is usually based on one serving and that the serving line includes a household measure and a metric amount. That line is the starting point for understanding the rest of the panel.

Our published French Vanilla scoop record, for instance, uses 2/3 cup, or 95 grams, and lists 210 calories for that serving. That is a reference from the documented product information, not a statement that every scoop served in the shop weighs 95 grams. The record's source date is June 2019, so the current container label remains important when checking whether details have changed.

Do not compare a number for 95 grams with a number for a different amount as though both describe identical portions. Also keep units together: grams and milligrams are not the same measure. If a card lacks a clear serving amount, avoid making a numerical ranking from it. Missing context can make a precise-looking comparison misleading.

Keep the base separate from the finished dessert

A product panel does not automatically describe everything added to it. The nutrition information for frozen yogurt is not a complete total for yogurt with toppings. Likewise, a scoop's numbers do not include a brownie underneath it or cookies surrounding it. Each additional component would need its own information and a known amount for a meaningful calculation.

This is why we do not present an improvised total for a custom cup or specialty in this guide. Even if you know the base's figures, an unspecified topping quantity leaves a gap. Use available labels to understand the individual products, and ask what information is available for the complete dessert rather than relying on a guessed total.

Read declared allergens and additional cautions separately

On the scoop cards, the “Contains” information is distinct from the additional allergen note. In the French Vanilla example, milk and eggs are declared, while peanuts, tree nuts, wheat, and soy appear in the equipment-related record. These fields should remain separate when you read or share the information; combining them into one unexplained list loses the distinction.

Yogurt cards use a status table plus a facility statement. A “No” in one row does not cancel the facility note beneath it. The published yogurt caution says the product is manufactured in a facility that also processes peanuts, tree nuts, eggs, soy, milk, and wheat products. That manufacturing information also does not describe every aspect of handling after the product reaches the shop.

The FDA's allergen-label guidance emphasizes reading food labels when you have food allergies. Our online summaries are an aid to that process, not an assurance that a particular item or preparation is appropriate for you.

Use filters to find mentions, not to establish safety

Our catalog allergen selectors are lookup tools. In the yogurt catalog, selecting an allergen shows entries where that allergen is marked “Yes.” In the gelato catalog, the selector checks the published known-allergen and “may contain” lists. A filter labeled “Milk,” for example, is not a request to remove milk-containing products.

That direction matters. A visitor who mistakes an inclusion filter for an exclusion filter could interpret the results backward. Read the selected control and then open the resulting card. Reset the filter when you move to another question. The absence of an item from filtered results can reflect how the available information is recorded; it is not a dietary certification.

Look inside compound ingredients

Some ingredients contain other ingredients of their own. Parentheses in a product statement can explain what goes into a base, cookie piece, syrup, or other component. Those details are worth reading instead of stopping at the first recognizable word.

Cookie Monster yogurt illustrates this well: its published statement includes a cookie base containing ingredients such as butter and soy lecithin, and its allergen table declares milk and soy. Cookies 'N Cream yogurt has a different statement, with cookie pieces that include wheat flour, and declares milk, wheat, and soy. They are both cookie-themed yogurts, but the similar theme does not make their formulas identical.

Our Cookie Monster and Cookies 'N Cream comparison explores that specific choice. When comparing any two flavors, keep each ingredient statement with its own product instead of assuming a shared name or color means shared ingredients.

Recognize an incomplete record

Some gelato and sorbetto entries have less publicly documented information than others. Alphonso Mango, for example, is categorized as sorbetto, but its note says a complete major-allergen statement was not published online and directs visitors to verify the current container label. An empty allergen list in that record means incomplete information, not a confirmed absence of allergens.

Other entries provide a known-allergen list and additional possible-allergen details. Read both, along with any source link. Do not transfer information from a similarly named product, another brand, or a previous purchase to fill a gap. If the information needed for your decision is unavailable, leave the question open until it can be verified.

If you save a screenshot for a later visit, keep the product name and source context with it. A cropped number or allergen row can lose the qualification that made it meaningful. Reopen the current page when you return, and check the label again rather than treating a saved image as a permanent statement about the product.

Do not ask category labels to do too much

“Nonfat,” “no sugar added,” “vegan,” and “sorbet” appear as product descriptions or categories in the wider catalog. They are not interchangeable claims. A nonfat category is not a statement that an item contains no milk; Birthday Cake yogurt, for example, is categorized as Nonfat and declares milk.

Similarly, a product category is not a complete explanation of ingredients, serving size, or cross-contact. Start with the exact wording shown for the specific item. Then read the detailed record and current label. This approach keeps a useful browsing shortcut from turning into a broader conclusion the information does not support.

Bring a clear question to the shop

Before a visit, check the live menu separately from your ingredient research. A documented product is not necessarily listed in stock today. If a particular choice is essential, use the phone number on our Visit page to ask about it before traveling.

Be specific about the product and the information you need checked: the current container label, all components of a specialty, or relevant handling and cross-contact questions. Do not rely on the website alone to establish suitability. Clear questions are more useful than assuming that a familiar flavor, a filter result, or an older label tells the whole story.
