A little cherry, a big food-science story

Maraschino Cherries Aren’t a Cherry-Tree Variety

Published November 7, 2026 · Sweet Escape, Memphis

Here is a fun fact for your next ice cream conversation: you cannot plant a “maraschino cherry” tree and wait for a crop of ready-made sundae garnishes. Maraschino describes a prepared cherry, not a cherry-tree variety. The familiar little finishing touch has a food-processing story behind it, involving fruit preservation, texture, color, and a university laboratory.

There is an even stranger side story. Oregon State University researchers once investigated whether the leftover liquid from cherry processing could become fuel. That does not mean anyone should pour cherry syrup into a gas tank. It means a dessert garnish gave scientists more than one interesting problem to solve.

Start with the fruit, then ask about the preparation

A tree’s identity and a food’s name answer different questions. NC State Extension identifies the sweet cherry tree as Prunus avium, a member of the rose family. Its fleshy fruit surrounds a hard stone: botanically, it is a drupe. That describes the plant and the structure of its fruit, not the contents of a finished jar.

“Maraschino” instead sends us toward what people do with cherries after harvest. Think of the difference between asking which fruit started a recipe and asking which finished food a recipe produced. Both questions matter, but one answer cannot automatically stand in for the other.

This distinction is useful before you even reach the ingredient list. A prepared-food name may tell you what category you are looking at while leaving important details unresolved. To learn the actual fruit, syrup, flavoring, or coloring in a particular product, you need that product’s description and label.

The name has a European backstory

Oregon State’s food-processing timeline traces the earlier preparation to marasca cherries from the coast of present-day Croatia. It describes preservation involving seawater and, later, marasca liqueur made from the cherries’ juice and pits.

The name therefore carries a connection to a particular cherry even though “maraschino” is not itself the name of a tree variety. That is the little linguistic trap at the center of this fact: the source fruit and the prepared product have related names, but they are not interchangeable terms.

OSU’s account then moves to the 1920s, when Ernest Wiegand at Oregon Agricultural College improved a process using Oregon Royal Anne cherries. A name with an older European history became attached to a different processing approach and a different starting cherry. Food names can travel farther than the original recipe.

Why firmness was a serious research question

In a 2015 oral-history interview, OSU food scientist Ron Wrolstad explains that maraschino products existed before Wiegand’s work. He describes the investigation of a preserving brine and the use of calcium chloride to make cherries firmer, which also helped with mechanical pitting.

That last detail changes how you picture the problem. A cherry was not only being prepared for someone’s spoon. It also needed a workable texture during processing. Removing a stone mechanically is a different challenge from placing a finished cherry on a dessert.

A cherry pitter belongs in a museum

You can see the stone-removal problem in an actual historical object. The Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History catalog includes a cherry pitter, also called a cherry stoner, dated to 1880–1900. Its listed materials include an iron plunger and several kinds of wood, including poplar and walnut.

Look at the object’s photograph and compare it with the finished fruit you normally notice. One is equipment; the other is food. Putting the two pictures together makes an otherwise invisible step easier to imagine. The museum record does not connect this particular tool to Wiegand, but it gives the broader processing question a tangible shape.

The catalog gives overall measurements of 8½ by 20⅛ by 12¼ inches. This is not the tiny hand tool you might first picture from the name. It also distinguishes different materials for different parts: poplar for the board and arm, walnut for the top grip, and beech or maple for the side grip. Reading those component details offers a closer look at a tool built around one small fruit.

Bright red is a processing story, too

OSU’s 2009 article about cherries describes a familiar bright-red preparation in which cherries are bleached white, colored red, sweetened, and packed in an almond-flavored syrup. That is a description of a process, not a rule that every jar sold under the name must follow the same formula.

Notice how many separate choices are tucked into that description. The fruit supplies the starting material. The processing changes its appearance. The syrup and flavoring belong to the finished preparation. The final color alone cannot identify all of those steps.

This is a good place to resist judging an entire product from a photograph. A picture can show a red cherry. It cannot tell you the complete ingredient list, the exact source of that color, or whether another jar was made the same way. Those are questions for the actual product information.

One laboratory looked to radishes for red

Wrolstad’s oral history adds an unexpected vegetable to the dessert story. He recalls his laboratory’s work in the 1990s investigating radish extract as a cherry colorant and achieving a color match to a Red 40 reference.

The surprise is the research connection, not a claim that every maraschino cherry contains radish. A scientist studying color can ask whether a pigment from one food could serve a purpose in another. The question crosses the boundary we casually draw between vegetables and dessert.

The investigation concerned a colorant, not a universal cherry recipe or a promise about flavor.

Not every maraschino cherry follows the bright-red example

For a concrete contrast, Luxardo describes its own Original Maraschino Cherries as candied marasca cherries in marasca cherry syrup. Its product description identifies a dark-red color. That first-party example is enough to show why the bright-red process should not be treated as the definition of every product bearing the name.

This is not a ranking of cherry brands, and it is not a statement about Sweet Escape’s suppliers. It is a useful comparison between a broad food name and a specific manufacturer’s description.

If you are curious about a jar at home, start with three questions: what cherry is named, what liquid surrounds it, and what ingredients are listed? Then distinguish what the manufacturer actually says from what the product’s appearance makes you expect. You will learn more that way than by assuming all maraschinos are identical.

The garnish even has a campus-building connection

OSU Libraries’ history of Wiegand Hall records that the building opened in 1951 as the Food Technology Building. It also identifies Wiegand’s leadership of the university’s horticultural-products processing program, established in 1919, and his association with the modern maraschino process.

A tiny dessert finishing touch can make university research seem unexpectedly concrete. We often think about the final bite and overlook the earlier questions about preserving, handling, and processing a crop. The building’s history puts those questions back into view: this was part of an organized field of study, not merely an odd recipe footnote.

The strangest detour: could cherry brine become fuel?

In a July 24, 2009, research announcement, OSU reported Alan Bakalinsky’s investigation into fermenting sugar-bearing leftover cherry brine to produce ethanol. Small-scale tests had been completed. The proposed starting material was a processing byproduct, not a bowl of finished dessert toppings.

The same announcement explained why the economics were difficult at that time. Transporting a lot of water, the limited scale, and the cost of handling and distilling the material complicated the idea. An interesting laboratory result did not automatically become an economical fuel operation.

That distinction is the best part of this bizarre fact. The research question was real, but the honest takeaway is not “maraschino cherries power cars.” It is that a food-processing leftover prompted a feasibility study. The 2009 report does not establish a current commercial operation, and it offers no reason to experiment with engine fuel at home.
